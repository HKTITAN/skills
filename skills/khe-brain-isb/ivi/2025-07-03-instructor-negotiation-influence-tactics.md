# Negotiation, influence tactics and a multi-issue case debrief

A negotiation instructor from the ISB faculty, not named in the recording, taught the second class of a negotiation module. The first half covered psychological biases that can be used to influence the other side, mostly through classic experiments and her own stories. The class then ran a team negotiation over a licensing deal for a TV show, and she debriefed it.

## What was said

### Biases as tools

- The instructor says everyone has cognitive limits, and a negotiator can use them when presenting arguments and must also watch for them being used on her.
- She says people behave very differently from their self-image once they are put in a particular situation, and cites the Stanford prison experiment.
- She says most of the tactics in this class come from the book Influence, which she recommends for understanding human psychology.
- She says the tactics can be combined, and a negotiator should use more than one where possible.

### Contrast and the decoy

- She says people judge an option as good or bad only in comparison with something else, so the negotiator should supply the comparison.
- She says people find it hard to compare unlike things, so to steer someone to one option you add a similar but inferior version of that option.
- Her experiment: students offered six dollars or an elegant pen mostly took the money, and adding a third option to the set moved more of them to the pen.
- She says the decoy must be similar to the target and slightly worse. If it is very different, people cannot compare the two.
- She says the decoy works only when the person is already choosing within that set, such as variants of a car they have decided to buy.
- She describes a three-tier subscription offer where the middle option looked useless, and says removing it changed what people chose.
- She says she helped a consulting partner add a similar but inferior option to a client proposal, so that the package he wanted chosen looked better.
- Her advice is to design the packages in an offer with the decoy in mind.

### Commitment and consistency

- She says once people make a choice they want to stay consistent with it, more so when the choice was active, written or public.
- She describes foot in the door: start with a small request, then make a larger request of the same kind.
- Her field study: fewer than 17% of households agreed to put up a large, ugly road safety sign when asked directly, and over 76% agreed when they had first accepted a small request.
- She tells two stories of agreeing to a small favour that grew step by step into a much larger one, and says she would have refused if asked for the whole thing at the start.
- She links escalation of commitment to sunk-cost bias, and cites the book Negotiating Rationally for a public fight between two chief executives that both lost.
- She says a commitment made in public is more likely to be honoured.
- In a multi-issue negotiation she advises getting early agreement on easy issues where both sides are aligned, because that makes the other side more willing to work through the hard ones.
- She says investors show the same pattern: once they have put money into a company they tend to put in more.

### Door in the face

- She describes door in the face as the reverse: open with a large request, take the refusal, then make a smaller request, which the other side finds hard to refuse a second time.
- She says foot in the door fits when there is a working relationship, and door in the face is the one used when there is none.
- She says research supports door in the face, and adds that she has no case of her own where she has seen it work.
- She says refusing takes more mental effort than agreeing, because a refusal needs a reason and has to protect the relationship.
- She says a request that might be refused is more likely to be accepted when the other person is busy and has no bandwidth to think.

### Resisting these tactics

- She says to stay focused on the goal and not to accept a deal just because of something already agreed.
- Her test is to ask whether you would accept this offer if the earlier deal did not exist.
- She suggests asking a neutral person to check your judgment.
- She says to avoid quick or public commitments, and that "I'll think about it" is a valid answer.

### Loss aversion

- Citing Daniel Kahneman's prospect theory, she says losses weigh more than equal gains.
- She says it helps to give away, early in the negotiation, things that matter little to you and a lot to the other side.
- Her reason is that once the other side has received something, walking away from the deal feels like a loss to them.
- She says a negotiator does not have to fight on every issue.

### Availability and mere exposure

- She defines availability bias as treating whatever comes to mind easily as reliable and frequent.
- She says people start out negative towards what they do not know and warm to what is familiar.
- She says advertisers repeat ads for this reason: a shopper facing two unknown shampoos picks the one seen in an ad.
- She says people decide under uncertainty and time pressure, and that is when these shortcuts take over.
- For negotiation, she says to raise a difficult issue ahead of time so it does not arrive as a shock.
- She relays a guest speaker's practice of meeting each board member separately before a board meeting to tell them a serious issue is coming.
- She describes a study in which a student who only sat in on a professor's classes, nodding and smiling, was liked more at the end of the term.
- She says to show your face at meetings and networking events before you need something, and to keep your video on in online classes.

### Argument dilution

- She describes research in which a medicine's serious side effects seemed less serious when minor ones were listed with them.
- She says listeners average the arguments they hear, so adding weak arguments lowers the force of the strong ones.

### Confirmation bias, previewed

- She says people accept without testing any argument that fits what they already believe, and discard one that contradicts it.
- She says a negotiator should learn the other side's beliefs and present information in a way that confirms them, which also builds trust.
- She names the Challenger shuttle disaster as the classic case, to be taught in the next class.

### Negotiating in teams

- She says teams reach fewer deals when roles are unclear and members speak over each other.
- She says being in a team makes people feel they must win for the group, which reduces their willingness to compromise. She calls this inter-group conflict.
- She cites a study where half a class wore red and half wore blue all term, and each half came to identify with its colour and dislike the other.
- She says a team with clearly divided roles does better than a solo negotiator, because it has more expertise and processes information more easily.
- She says that when she worked in sales her side went to negotiations in teams of at least three.

### Lessons from the case debrief

- She says to work out who the counterpart is and how they earn. Her examples are a sales representative paid on commission and a platform whose revenue comes from advertising.
- She says to think about the issues that matter to the other side as well as your own, so they can be added to the bargaining mix.
- She says the more issues on the table that both sides care about, the more room there is to trade.
- She says when someone asks what you care about there is usually no reason to hide it.
- She recounts playing this case herself: her counterpart gave her the contract length she wanted within five minutes, and after that she found it hard to refuse what he asked for.
- Her rule for a multi-issue negotiation is never to go issue by issue, and to negotiate in packages.
- She teaches multiple equivalent simultaneous offers: build three or four packages worth the same to you and present them together.
- She says the purpose is to ask which package looks better and why, because the answers reveal the other side's interests. It is not to tell them to pick one.
- She says a demanding counterpart is useful, because without demands there is nothing to trade.
- She says if you must go issue by issue, do not start with the hardest, purely distributive issue, or it will eat the whole session.

### Contingent contracts

- She says two sides will always hold different forecasts about a future result, and meeting in the middle does not resolve that.
- Her answer is a contingent contract, where payment depends on the result that actually occurs, so both sides bet on their own forecast.
- She says any deal whose profit or loss arrives in the future needs an agreed formula for dividing the surplus or the loss.
- She gives three uses of a contingent contract: it shares the risk, it aligns both sides' incentives to make the venture succeed, and it works as a lie detector.
- She explains the lie detector point: a party that overstated its confidence will not agree to be paid only if its forecast comes true.
- She says that in this case adding the contingent clause created about one million of extra value, and teams that skipped it left that on the table.

### Walking away

- She says never to accept a deal worse than your best alternative.
- She names agreement bias: after negotiating for a while people feel they must close, and that urge has to be resisted.

## Examples and cases used

- Stanford prison experiment: situations change behaviour more than people expect.
- Six dollars or a pen experiment: adding a third option changes the split.
- Car variants: a slightly inferior variant sells the one above it. She says one model once had nine variants and notes that too many options tire buyers.
- Road safety sign field study in California: a small first request multiplies agreement to a large one.
- Shampoo on a shelf and repeated ads: familiarity decides under time pressure.
- Classroom confederate study: mere presence and nodding builds liking.
- Red and blue shirt study: group identity forms from an arbitrary label.
- Challenger shuttle: named as the confirmation bias case for the next class.
- TV show licensing case: she says it is based on a real acquisition of a show by Disney in Japan, with names changed. The issues were licence fee, upfront payment, contract length and viewership forecasts.

## Numbers given

- 64% and 36%: the shares of students who chose six dollars and the pen in the two-option version of the experiment she describes.
- Under 17% and over 76%: households agreeing to the large sign without and with a prior small request, in the field study she describes.
- About 1 million: the extra value she says a contingent contract added in the class case.
