# Users and product-market fit

This file answers four questions a founder brings: how to talk to users, how to know whether the company has product-market fit, what to build next, and whether to pivot. The speakers are Gustaf Alstromer, Michael Seibel, Dalton Caldwell, Emmett Shear, Paul Graham and one unsigned Y Combinator post, together with David Rusenko, a founder of Weebly who spoke at Startup School and is not a YC partner. Read every line with its speaker, its stage and its year: the advice changes between before launch, before product-market fit and after it, and the speakers differ on charging early, on how good a first launch must be and on how soon to change ideas.

## How do I find users to talk to, and when do I start?

- Alstromer says, in a talk the library dates to 2022, that great founders talk to future customers before they have a product and that the very best keep learning directly from users for the life of the company. (How to talk to users, https://www.ycombinator.com/library/Iq-how-to-talk-to-users)
- Alstromer's reason is that users and customers keep a founder honest: they are the only stakeholders paying anything, so if anyone tells the truth it will be them. (How to talk to users, https://www.ycombinator.com/library/Iq-how-to-talk-to-users)
- Alstromer says the easiest first interviews are with people you know, who are the most likely to respond but may be less honest so as not to offend you, and after them with coworkers and former coworkers. (How to talk to users, https://www.ycombinator.com/library/Iq-how-to-talk-to-users)
- Alstromer reports that when YC founders were asked how they found their early users, LinkedIn, Reddit, Slack or Discord communities and sometimes in-person events were among the most common answers. (How to talk to users, https://www.ycombinator.com/library/Iq-how-to-talk-to-users)
- Alstromer wants interviews held by video call, phone call or in person, and says a five-minute video interview teaches more than 500 or 5,000 survey responses. (How to talk to users, https://www.ycombinator.com/library/Iq-how-to-talk-to-users)
- Rusenko, in a talk the library dates to 2018, says about five to ten customer interviews are what a founder needs, each lasting around an hour or a little longer, by phone or at the customer's place of business. (How to find product-market fit, https://www.ycombinator.com/library/7V-how-to-find-product-market-fit)
- Graham's 2006 essay says more startups destroy themselves than are crushed by competitors, names internal disputes, inertia and ignoring users as the three main ways, and picks ignoring users as the worst. (The Hardest Lessons for Startups to Learn, https://paulgraham.com/startuplessons.html)
- Graham's 2006 essay argues that no product idea reached by thinking alone is as clever as the ones found by putting prototypes in front of users. (The Hardest Lessons for Startups to Learn, https://paulgraham.com/startuplessons.html)
- The Y Combinator post says an early stage company's most important tasks are writing code and talking to users, that launching, talking to users and iterating should take almost all of a founder's time, and that for great companies the cycle never ends. (YC's essential startup advice, https://www.ycombinator.com/library/4D-yc-s-essential-startup-advice)
- Seibel says, in a talk the library dates to 2018, that gamers were about twenty percent of Justin.tv's traffic for years and were ignored, and that the first thing that changed when work on Twitch began was that the team started talking to them. (Building product, https://www.ycombinator.com/library/7s-building-product)

## What do I ask in a user interview, and what do I never ask?

- Alstromer calls it the most important rule of a problem interview not to introduce your idea until the end of the call, or not at all, because raising it early can bias the answers and the founder's role is to listen. (How to talk to users, https://www.ycombinator.com/library/Iq-how-to-talk-to-users)
- Alstromer's six questions ask how the person does the task today, what is hardest about it, why it is hard, how often they do it, why it matters to their company, and what they do to solve it for themselves today. (How to talk to users, https://www.ycombinator.com/library/Iq-how-to-talk-to-users)
- Alstromer says to watch the person do the task where possible, for example over a screen share, because what they do matters beyond what they say and changing behaviour will be hard. (How to talk to users, https://www.ycombinator.com/library/Iq-how-to-talk-to-users)
- Alstromer says never to ask whether someone will use your product, because they are likely to say yes and the yes will mean nothing. (How to talk to users, https://www.ycombinator.com/library/Iq-how-to-talk-to-users)
- Alstromer also rules out asking which features would improve a product or what a better one would look like, since describing the problem is the interviewee's job and working out features is the founder's, and he rules out yes or no questions and two questions at once. (How to talk to users, https://www.ycombinator.com/library/Iq-how-to-talk-to-users)
- Alstromer says "users generally have good problems but also generally bad solutions", and his example is early Gmail users asking to see the inbox and the open email on one screen, when the real problem was that Gmail was too slow. (How to talk to users, https://www.ycombinator.com/library/Iq-how-to-talk-to-users)
- Alstromer says users have no incentive to refuse any extra feature and are likely to say yes to all of them, so working out which features matter most falls to the founder. (How to talk to users, https://www.ycombinator.com/library/Iq-how-to-talk-to-users)
- Shear says customer interviews are for understanding the problem and generating ideas from it, and writes "Validating an idea you already have is an anti-goal." because no amount of validation improves an idea. (How to prioritize features, https://www.ycombinator.com/library/8p-how-to-prioritize-features)
- Shear's method for winning users from competitors is to interview five or six dedicated users each of your own service, every competing service and closely adjacent ones, about 50 interviews in all, then categorize and score the answers in a spreadsheet and look for patterns. (How to prioritize features, https://www.ycombinator.com/library/8p-how-to-prioritize-features)
- Alstromer says that once a prototype exists, even a clickable design, you hand it to a user with a goal and do not explain how to get through each screen, because with a real product you would not be standing next to them. (How to talk to users, https://www.ycombinator.com/library/Iq-how-to-talk-to-users)
- Rusenko's version of the same test is to give a task and then neither touch the device nor speak until the person finishes, even when they ask for help, and he says three to five sessions are enough to find the most critical usability problems. (How to find product-market fit, https://www.ycombinator.com/library/7V-how-to-find-product-market-fit)
- Alstromer says that after five or ten interviews, and before any MVP, a founder should sort the notes into problem buckets, write down conclusions, form a hypothesis for the solution and start building an MVP as fast as possible, then test it with the same users. (How to talk to users, https://www.ycombinator.com/library/Iq-how-to-talk-to-users)

## Which customers, and whose feedback, should I listen to first?

- Alstromer suggests checking that a problem is worth solving by asking whether people already pay for other solutions in the space today, which he reads as a good sign. (How to talk to users, https://www.ycombinator.com/library/Iq-how-to-talk-to-users)
- Seibel says, in a talk the library dates to 2018, to check how often and how intensely the customer has the problem: a car buyer keeps the car for about seven years, while a dealer has to hit numbers every day and is the real customer of car shopping sites. (Building product, https://www.ycombinator.com/library/7s-building-product)
- Seibel says an MVP is bad by definition, so the first customers have to be the most desperate ones, and that going after the hardest or most impressive customers first is the wrong instinct. (Building product, https://www.ycombinator.com/library/7s-building-product)
- Seibel's test, in a talk the library dates to 2018, is that a six-month conversation to sell simple software at a thousand dollars a month means the company is not desperate and the founder should move on. (Building product, https://www.ycombinator.com/library/7s-building-product)
- Seibel says not to take product feedback from friends or investors, who usually do not have the problem and will lead a founder astray out of good intentions. (Building product, https://www.ycombinator.com/library/7s-building-product)
- Seibel tells founders entering YC that starting with a price is almost always better than starting free, because free draws in people who do not really have the problem and learning from them leads the product astray. (Building product, https://www.ycombinator.com/library/7s-building-product)
- Seibel adds, answering a question in the same talk, that free is fine for users you never plan to charge, and that otherwise you should charge as soon as possible to learn whether they will pay. (Building product, https://www.ycombinator.com/library/7s-building-product)
- Rusenko, in a talk the library dates to 2018, says not to worry about monetization at first and to make sure the product is working for people, though he also says not to shy away from charging. (How to find product-market fit, https://www.ycombinator.com/library/7V-how-to-find-product-market-fit)
- The Y Combinator post says founders choose customers as much as customers choose them, and that 10 customers with a burning problem are much better than 1000 with a passing annoyance. (YC's essential startup advice, https://www.ycombinator.com/library/4D-yc-s-essential-startup-advice)
- Rusenko says to test with target customers but to stay flexible about who they are, and to consider changing the target when an unexpected group shows up and starts hacking the product for its own use. (How to find product-market fit, https://www.ycombinator.com/library/7V-how-to-find-product-market-fit)

## What is product-market fit, and what is it not?

- Seibel's written post takes Marc Andreessen's definition and restates it: you have reached fit when you are overwhelmed with usage, usually so swamped keeping the product running that you cannot make major changes to it. (The real product-market fit, https://www.ycombinator.com/library/5z-the-real-product-market-fit)
- Seibel's post says headcount or a major funding round does not show fit, and that what he looks for is "a frantic founding team" coping with a growing crowd of customers who are happy, loyal and, ideally, paying. (The real product-market fit, https://www.ycombinator.com/library/5z-the-real-product-market-fit)
- Seibel's post says a false belief in fit is a huge problem because founders then hire, raise burn and optimize the product before they have discovered what needs to be built. (The real product-market fit, https://www.ycombinator.com/library/5z-the-real-product-market-fit)
- Seibel's post says founders grip solutions too tightly and problems too loosely, that the first idea for the solution is usually wrong, and that only launching, talking to customers and iterating finds the product. (The real product-market fit, https://www.ycombinator.com/library/5z-the-real-product-market-fit)
- Seibel's post extends Sequoia's hair on fire image: a person whose hair is on fire would grab even a brick to put it out, so founders should look for problems dire enough that users will try a half-baked first version. (The real product-market fit, https://www.ycombinator.com/library/5z-the-real-product-market-fit)
- In the recorded discussion of Seibel's post, which the library dates to 2018, the claim to have built what customers want is rejected as a stand-in for fit: without explosive usage, the speaker says, you do not have what customers want. (The real product-market fit, https://www.ycombinator.com/library/5z-the-real-product-market-fit)
- In the same recorded discussion of Seibel's post, the definition is read as requiring profit as well, since money piling up in the bank is part of it, and the speaker mocks companies that say growth is killing them while they scale at negative margins. (The real product-market fit, https://www.ycombinator.com/library/5z-the-real-product-market-fit)
- The Y Combinator post says it makes little sense to grow before product-market fit because poor retention always follows, and that growth is the result of talking to users, building what they want and iterating quickly. (YC's essential startup advice, https://www.ycombinator.com/library/4D-yc-s-essential-startup-advice)
- Seibel, speaking as a Socialcam founder in the recorded discussion of his post, says Socialcam was not at fit despite sixteen million downloads in four months and a top five App Store rank, because it had no monetization and a new user had almost no chance of still using the app 10 days later. (The real product-market fit, https://www.ycombinator.com/library/5z-the-real-product-market-fit)
- Seibel, speaking as a Justin.tv founder in the same discussion, says that by about 2010 the company had eight million dollars in revenue and one million in profit and was still not at fit, because it had no repeatable engine for growth and could not reliably monetize a site full of copyrighted content. (The real product-market fit, https://www.ycombinator.com/library/5z-the-real-product-market-fit)
- Seibel says in that discussion that fit arrived only with Twitch and gamers, a repeatable machine that produced more users, more money and retention, and that it took six years to get a hint that gamers were the customer base. (The real product-market fit, https://www.ycombinator.com/library/5z-the-real-product-market-fit)
- Caldwell, in a talk the library dates to 2019, says most people never get product-market fit, that he thinks most of his audience lacks it even if they believe otherwise, and that the sign of having it is that growth has stopped being your biggest problem. (All about pivoting, https://www.ycombinator.com/library/6p-all-about-pivoting)
- Rusenko places product-market fit between launch and traction, says most companies never find it, and thinks the stretch from idea to traction is the hardest one and the one that kills the most companies. (How to find product-market fit, https://www.ycombinator.com/library/7V-how-to-find-product-market-fit)
- Rusenko recounts that he wrote Weebly's first code in February 2006, that eighteen months in it still had no fit, and that new users per day turned upward only about twenty months in, in October 2007. (How to find product-market fit, https://www.ycombinator.com/library/7V-how-to-find-product-market-fit)

## How do I tell whether I have product-market fit?

- Rusenko tracks three things, returning usage, NPS and paying customer renewal rates, and treats the number of people who come back within one, three, seven and thirty days as the main sign that things are working. (How to find product-market fit, https://www.ycombinator.com/library/7V-how-to-find-product-market-fit)
- Rusenko says some people set the NPS bar at 40 and he puts it at about 50, and that Weebly's early score was 80. (How to find product-market fit, https://www.ycombinator.com/library/7V-how-to-find-product-market-fit)
- Rusenko says not to pay much attention to signups and to watch active users, and not to weigh conversion rate at the start of a SaaS business because conversion rates start low and build over time. (How to find product-market fit, https://www.ycombinator.com/library/7V-how-to-find-product-market-fit)
- Rusenko says Weebly looked at signups during its first 18 months and was not measuring its key numbers, and he thinks that is why the search took 18 months. (How to find product-market fit, https://www.ycombinator.com/library/7V-how-to-find-product-market-fit)
- Rusenko says a press mention sends signups straight up for a day and straight back down the next, and that fourteen months in he read Weebly's lack of fit from new users per day declining after its press mentions. (How to find product-market fit, https://www.ycombinator.com/library/7V-how-to-find-product-market-fit)
- Rusenko describes the feeling: without fit you are pushing customers toward a solution and they are too nice to say no but do not come back, and with it customers beat a path to your door and pull you along. (How to find product-market fit, https://www.ycombinator.com/library/7V-how-to-find-product-market-fit)
- Caldwell, in a talk the library dates to 2019, warns that politeness is easy to mistake for traction, since people will not call an idea horrible to your face and will ask for a few more features, which a founder can chase for years without getting customers. (All about pivoting, https://www.ycombinator.com/library/6p-all-about-pivoting)
- Caldwell calls a little traction "the uncanny valley of product-market fit", and says a founder whose idea fails at once is better placed than one with a few users or one customer, because the first can drop the idea with no regret. (All about pivoting, https://www.ycombinator.com/library/6p-all-about-pivoting)
- Seibel, in a talk the library dates to 2018, says a company needs one number that shows how it is doing: revenue if it will ever charge customers, and a usage measure such as DAU only if it never will. (Building product, https://www.ycombinator.com/library/7s-building-product)
- In the recorded discussion of Seibel's post, which the library dates to 2018, the speaker refuses to choose among growth, retention and profitability, calls revenue that you keep the closest thing to a single metric, and says even that needs revenue lost each month, the cost of getting it and customer satisfaction beside it. (The real product-market fit, https://www.ycombinator.com/library/5z-the-real-product-market-fit)
- The Y Combinator post says a startup should choose very early one or two key metrics to judge success by, and that founders should then pick tasks almost only by their effect on those metrics. (YC's essential startup advice, https://www.ycombinator.com/library/4D-yc-s-essential-startup-advice)
- Seibel says to measure with an events-based analytics product, to start with five to ten simple stats, and to write the stats into the product spec so that a feature never ships unmeasured. (Building product, https://www.ycombinator.com/library/7s-building-product)

## How do I get there: launching, prototypes and iterations?

- Graham's 2006 essay says to get a version 1 out fast and improve it from users' reactions, meaning minimal and not buggy, and that he has seen many startups die from releasing too slowly and none from releasing too quickly. (The Hardest Lessons for Startups to Learn, https://paulgraham.com/startuplessons.html)
- Graham's 2006 essay says you will not know who your users are, so guessing what they like is dangerous, and cites Wufoo, which released its form-builder before the database behind it and learned that Linux users objected to how much Flash it used. (The Hardest Lessons for Startups to Learn, https://paulgraham.com/startuplessons.html)
- Graham's 2006 essay says a startup should keep adding improvements for as long as it wants to be considered a startup, at least some small one every day or two. (The Hardest Lessons for Startups to Learn, https://paulgraham.com/startuplessons.html)
- The Y Combinator post tells founders before launch to release a mediocre product as soon as possible and then talk to customers and iterate, provided the product gives some customers enough value to outweigh its flaws. (YC's essential startup advice, https://www.ycombinator.com/library/4D-yc-s-essential-startup-advice)
- Rusenko, in a talk the library dates to 2018, dislikes the word viable and prefers a minimum remarkable product, citing Paul Buchheit's advice to launch when the product is better than what is out there, and says not to launch before that point. (How to find product-market fit, https://www.ycombinator.com/library/7V-how-to-find-product-market-fit)
- Rusenko explains in answer to a question that better does not mean more features: one feature, or ease of use, can make up for what is missing if the product does the one job the customer has better than anyone else. (How to find product-market fit, https://www.ycombinator.com/library/7V-how-to-find-product-market-fit)
- The Y Combinator post says to win the first customers by manual work that would never scale, as when Airbnb's founders photographed early hosts' homes themselves, and that scaling too early often leads to failure. (YC's essential startup advice, https://www.ycombinator.com/library/4D-yc-s-essential-startup-advice)
- The Y Combinator post reports Paul Buchheit's advice, for when talking to users produces a long feature list, to look for the 90/10 solution that gets 90% of the result for 10% of the work. (YC's essential startup advice, https://www.ycombinator.com/library/4D-yc-s-essential-startup-advice)
- Rusenko says building a fully functional product to test a hypothesis is a very expensive test, a mistake he says Weebly made a lot early on, and that a clickable mock-up or throwaway code in front of users is enough. (How to find product-market fit, https://www.ycombinator.com/library/7V-how-to-find-product-market-fit)
- Rusenko says to expect about ten times as many iterations as planned, which is why he says to keep burn low and why outsourced coding, which gives one shot, generally does not work in the early days. (How to find product-market fit, https://www.ycombinator.com/library/7V-how-to-find-product-market-fit)
- Rusenko would not grow past about 20 people before fit, because a flat company breaks at about 23 to 25 and management layers are poor for the search, and after fit he would scale aggressively but says a company should probably never more than double its headcount in a year. (How to find product-market fit, https://www.ycombinator.com/library/7V-how-to-find-product-market-fit)

## How should I run the product development cycle?

- Seibel's post, written for startups that have already released an MVP, lists what was broken at Justin.tv: meandering meetings with no written decisions, loose specs, analytics rarely specified, cycles that often ran months, and product decisions made by the founders alone. (Guide to product development, https://www.ycombinator.com/library/4e-guide-to-product-development)
- Seibel, in a talk the library dates to 2018, says Justin.tv released every three months for a web-only product, and that two months into a cycle it had about three weeks of useful work and about five weeks of work to throw away. (Building product, https://www.ycombinator.com/library/7s-building-product)
- Seibel's post says the product should set the cycle length: Socialcam used two weeks because it shipped on iOS and needed thorough testing before each release, a web app can go shorter and hardware may need longer. (Guide to product development, https://www.ycombinator.com/library/4e-guide-to-product-development)
- Seibel's post describes one team meeting, on the first day of the cycle, built around a single goal chosen from three: more content creation, more new users or more retention. (Guide to product development, https://www.ycombinator.com/library/4e-guide-to-product-development)
- Seibel's post says everyone was expected to contribute ideas, debate and putting down ideas were not allowed, and getting an idea written on the board, even one never built, greatly raised buy-in. (Guide to product development, https://www.ycombinator.com/library/4e-guide-to-product-development)
- Seibel's post has the engineer closest to each area grade every idea easy, meaning several in a day, medium, meaning half a day for one person, or hard, meaning most of the cycle, and nothing could run into the next cycle. (Guide to product development, https://www.ycombinator.com/library/4e-guide-to-product-development)
- Seibel, in the talk the library dates to 2018, gives the same three grades with medium as a day or two, and adds that most hard ideas can be restated as easy ones by removing the parts that are both useless and hard. (Building product, https://www.ycombinator.com/library/7s-building-product)
- Seibel's post says every chosen item got a detailed spec, an owner and the stats to track, and that no feature was released without its analytics and a specific measurable result in mind. (Guide to product development, https://www.ycombinator.com/library/4e-guide-to-product-development)
- Seibel's post says the team kept no roadmap beyond the two weeks: it photographed the whiteboard, erased it and started the next meeting from a new goal, new analytics and insights from in-person user testing it tried to run once a month. (Guide to product development, https://www.ycombinator.com/library/4e-guide-to-product-development)
- Seibel's post is plain that the process bought fast iteration and a team without major arguments, and not the right product: Socialcam did not become what he hoped, and he says it should have built something closer to Snapchat. (Guide to product development, https://www.ycombinator.com/library/4e-guide-to-product-development)
- Caldwell, in a talk the library dates to 2019, says a pure software startup should see major progress in each iteration within weeks and thinks a cycle of months or longer is too slow, while biotech may take months or years. (All about pivoting, https://www.ycombinator.com/library/6p-all-about-pivoting)

## How do I choose what to build next?

- Seibel, answering a question in a talk the library dates to 2018, says there is usually no right answer and most things a team wants to build will not work, so a process that builds quickly, measures and iterates only on what works beats trying to foresee the future. (Building product, https://www.ycombinator.com/library/7s-building-product)
- Rusenko, crediting Astro Teller, says sorting tasks by cost times benefit suits continuous improvement, but for a big leap you "optimize for learning" by naming your biggest unknown and working to answer it, which then rewrites the whole priority list. (How to find product-market fit, https://www.ycombinator.com/library/7V-how-to-find-product-market-fit)
- Shear describes three ways to prioritize features, which he names Built For Me, Switch To Us and Three Numbers Matter, and says that in his experience startups move through them in that order as the challenge changes. (How to prioritize features, https://www.ycombinator.com/library/8p-how-to-prioritize-features)
- Shear says Built For Me means you are the most enthusiastic user of your own product, so you introspect on what you wish it did and build that, as he did for the viewer side of Twitch. (How to prioritize features, https://www.ycombinator.com/library/8p-how-to-prioritize-features)
- Shear limits Built For Me to small teams, because intuition is hard to pass to other people, and to founders who can tell what they want from what they think they are supposed to want. (How to prioritize features, https://www.ycombinator.com/library/8p-how-to-prioritize-features)
- Shear says Switch To Us means choosing the work that will move users over from competitors, suggests targeting one competitor at a time, and favours changes that are fast to make without avoiding hard ones that are necessary. (How to prioritize features, https://www.ycombinator.com/library/8p-how-to-prioritize-features)
- Shear says Switch To Us works when the product matters a great deal to customers who can state their tradeoffs, as Twitch streamers can, and when the category already exists, which is why it would not have worked at Justin.tv. (How to prioritize features, https://www.ycombinator.com/library/8p-how-to-prioritize-features)
- Shear says Three Numbers Matter means naming the three qualities customers care about most, as Amazon does with prices, selection and shipping, giving each a metric, revisiting the metrics every few months and giving priority to ideas that move them. (How to prioritize features, https://www.ycombinator.com/library/8p-how-to-prioritize-features)
- Shear holds the list at three because that is the most focus areas he finds people keep in mind, and puts work that must be done regardless, such as PCI compliance and uptime, in a separate fourth bucket. (How to prioritize features, https://www.ycombinator.com/library/8p-how-to-prioritize-features)
- Shear says the metrics approach suits products chosen for measurable reasons such as price, audience size or speed, and hardly works for products chosen for fun, connection or community. (How to prioritize features, https://www.ycombinator.com/library/8p-how-to-prioritize-features)
- The Y Combinator post says that when an early stage product is not working, the temptation is to build features for every problem the customer seems to have, and the better course is to talk to the customer and work only on the most acute one. (YC's essential startup advice, https://www.ycombinator.com/library/4D-yc-s-essential-startup-advice)

## When should I pivot, and how do I choose what to pivot to?

- Seibel, in a talk the library dates to 2018, defines a pivot as changing the customer or the problem and an iteration as changing the solution, and says a pivot should be rare and many times means starting a new company. (Building product, https://www.ycombinator.com/library/7s-building-product)
- Seibel answers founders who want to pivot after two months with "What impressive thing only took two months to build?", and says finding the solution is more like a two-year process. (Building product, https://www.ycombinator.com/library/7s-building-product)
- Caldwell, in a talk the library dates to 2019, says that for founders before launch or just after it a pivot is only a change of idea, that changing ideas constantly is the norm, and that founders who are not doing so are likely moving too slowly. (All about pivoting, https://www.ycombinator.com/library/6p-all-about-pivoting)
- Caldwell's main reason to pivot is opportunity cost: a founder can work on one thing at a time, so staying on something with evidence it is not working costs the alternative. (All about pivoting, https://www.ycombinator.com/library/6p-all-about-pivoting)
- Caldwell's good reasons to pivot are hating the work, no growth, being a poor fit for the idea, depending on something outside your control such as mainstream VR headset or crypto adoption, and running out of ideas to make it work. (All about pivoting, https://www.ycombinator.com/library/6p-all-about-pivoting)
- Caldwell's bad reasons are dodging hard work, as when a founder pivots each time the product reaches the point of selling, a chronic habit of changing ideas, and chasing a hot area after reading that someone raised money for it.
