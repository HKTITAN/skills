# Go-to-market motions and revenue models

This is the second half of a class by a faculty instructor who is not named in the recording, following a first half on metrics. The instructor covers revenue models, cost and unit economics, three go-to-market motions and a staged view of how go-to-market grows, with questions from founders throughout. The instructor says twice that marketing channels and pricing are not their specialism and points founders to marketing faculty for those.

## What was said

### What go-to-market includes

- The instructor says choosing a marketing channel comes down to asking where the target customer hangs out: on the internet, in physical spaces or at conferences.
- The instructor says the revenue model is a key part of go-to-market, though many people leave it out, and that understanding it also needs an understanding of the cost structure.
- The instructor says a founder may plan to make money a year or 18 months later and still have a revenue model, because the model is not about timing.
- The instructor does not accept a plan of getting users now and working out how to make money later, and says it means the founder does not know how to make money.
- The instructor says a community platform counts as go-to-market only if it is an acquisition channel, with a built-in way for its members to convert into customers.

### Monetising users and profit

- The instructor says only two companies, Google and Meta, have worked out how to monetise users at scale.
- The instructor says other companies do make money from users but not enough of it, and that monetising users at scale realistically needs a billion users.
- The instructor says Google invented the digital ad space and Meta invented placing digital ads in context, inside content.
- The instructor says monetising users is in practice an advertising business.
- The instructor tells founders that profit matters more than valuation, and that a billion dollar valuation without net profit sounds good but is not useful.
- The instructor says unit economics, cost per unit and revenue per unit, matter more than anything else in a business.

### Revenue model questions

- The instructor says a revenue model must answer five questions: who is paying, what is being paid, what it is paid for, how payment is made and how much. Leaving one unanswered makes it incomplete.
- The instructor says who pays means the user or an institution, and what is paid can be cash, data or engagement.
- The instructor says what it is paid for means one feature, a set of features or the whole product.
- The instructor says how payment is made is mainly about frequency, such as per transaction, monthly, yearly, licence or subscription.
- The instructor lists revenue models: transaction based for platforms, ad based, commission based, affiliate, interest earned on money held before it is paid out, and donations or grants.
- The instructor lists revenue sources: physical products, digital products, subscriptions, ads, commissions or referrals, and marketplaces, including a fee just to access a marketplace.

### Costs and unit economics

- The instructor says customer acquisition cost and distribution cost are among the main cost heads for digital products, and that the same heads apply to any business.
- The instructor separates acquisition from distribution: in e-commerce a company can acquire the customer and still have to pay for delivery.
- The instructor computes customer acquisition cost as salaries, overheads, paid marketing and tools, divided by the number of new customers.
- The instructor computes lifetime value as average sale value times number of transactions times the retention period, and customer lifetime value as that figure times the profit margin.
- The instructor says a lifetime value to acquisition cost ratio of 3 to 1 is the minimum for profitability, and higher is better.

### Product-led growth

- The instructor describes product-led growth as self-service, low-touch onboarding with no sales team, where users find the product, use it and pass it on by themselves.
- The instructor says the core task in product-led growth is to be as data driven as possible, so user behaviour can be nudged towards more use and more sharing.
- The instructor says a product-led product must be simple, intuitive and valuable, and that if someone has to explain it the company is in sales-led territory.
- The instructor says product-led growth works well for B2C products.
- The instructor defines the virality coefficient as the number of people who become users because of one existing user, and compares it to the R0 figure quoted during COVID.
- The instructor says a coefficient above 1 gives exponential growth, 1 only sustains the level, and below 1 gives decay.
- The instructor says that with no sales team, virality is the only way a product-led company can grow.
- The instructor names four sources of virality: direct network effects, word of mouth, casual contact and incentives.
- The instructor explains a direct network effect as a product that can only be used if the other person is on it, such as WhatsApp or Skype.
- The instructor explains casual contact as things like a sign-up link in an email signature, with a very low hit rate that still brings some conversions.
- The instructor calls a feature that builds virality into the product a growth hacking feature.

### Sales-led growth

- The instructor says sales-led growth suits complex, high-touch products where someone has to explain the product, teach its use and help integrate it with other systems.
- The instructor calls this value-based buying: the buyer purchases because the value has been explained, and realising the value is not easy.
- The instructor says sales-led growth is mainly a B2B strategy where the buyer and the user are different people and the relationship with the buyer is critical.
- The instructor says a seller of HR software needs HR as a friend and a seller of finance software needs finance as a friend, or they will not get in.
- The instructor says sales-led deals involve several user types, a buyer, an influencer and a user, each with different goals.

### Product-led sales

- The instructor describes product-led sales as a hybrid that came about because sales-led growth is expensive, with salaries and commissions.
- The instructor says the idea is to use product stickiness and usage data to find which individual users can lead to an institutional sale, then send a salesperson only to those.
- The instructor says the real money is in B2B: B2C gives volume without margin and needs about a million users, while B2B can make money with ten customers.
- The instructor gives three pressures behind the model: crowded acquisition channels with rising costs, buyers who hesitate because purchased software goes unused, and end users in companies who have more say.
- The instructor says a new customer type, the prosumer or professional consumer, is emerging.
- The instructor says the product is built in tiers that solve the same problem for an individual, then a team, then the whole enterprise.
- The instructor says the model only works where the company can collect data on its power users and see them using the product in a wider company setting.
- The instructor contrasts the funnels: traditional enterprise sales goes from marketing qualified lead to sales qualified lead to usage, while product-led sales starts with usage and finds product qualified accounts.
- The instructor says the strategy is called land and expand, or the Trojan horse strategy: get in through hero customers, make them champions, then expand.
- The instructor says to be user focused first even when the aim is a B2B company, and to build a deep relationship with users.
- The instructor says new B2B products that customers love started with a consumer ethos and kept it while adding enterprise features.
- The instructor says the weakness of land and expand is that it relies on influencers inside the customer, who may leave before enough value has been created for the enterprise.
- The instructor says product-led sales takes five to eight years to pay off, while sales-led growth makes money as soon as a salesperson sells.
- The instructor says the strategy makes no sense for a pure B2C product, because a company will never buy it.

### How go-to-market grows, as a tree

- The instructor says go-to-market starts with seeds and saplings, which are unscalable things: sharing with friends, cold calls, early adopters, viral content, physical ads and hosting events.
- The instructor says once a trunk forms the business starts to sustain itself, and its roots need three things: users, data and cash.
- The instructor says cash comes from raising money or from being profitable early, and is what keeps growth going.
- The instructor says nutrients come later and are periodic interventions: working on conversion with incentives or discounts, retention, brand awareness spend and customer success.
- The instructor says reaping fruit needs pruning and grafting, which means adding channel partners, new geographies and new categories.
- The instructor says expansion should begin only after the trunk is strong and there is enough cash.
- The instructor shows a chart of how well-known companies found their first customers and says all of them began with unscalable things.

### Long game and value realisation

- The instructor says winning does not mean 95 percent market share. A company needs to be profitable, and 20 percent of a fast growing market can still be worth a billion dollars.
- The instructor says an entrepreneur is in it for the long haul and should choose to enjoy the process over the outcome.
- The instructor says getting users is hard and getting paying users is harder, so a plan for a million users in three months does not work.
- The instructor says a company makes money at the point of value realisation, which may be the sale, usage or renewal, and each founder must find theirs.
- The instructor says there is no single go-to-market strategy. It has to keep changing as the product gains features and as customers change.
- The instructor says the questions to answer are who you are targeting, why they will pay, how to target them and when.
- On pricing, the instructor passes on only what they learned in an MBA: understand cost, understand willingness to pay, and maximise the margin between the two.

## Examples and cases used

- Dropbox: sharing a public folder made the recipient open an account to see the files, which the instructor calls a growth hacking feature. Its referral scheme gave free storage to both the person referring and the person referred.
- WhatsApp and Skype: direct network effects, since both sides must be on the product.
- SAP: a sales-led product whose value appears only after integration, which someone must explain. The instructor adds that SAP created the SAP consultant as a profession.
- Figma: began with individual UX designers, spread to their teams because design is collaborative, then went to the technology head with data on how many teams already used it and an offer of a cheaper enterprise deal.
- Tableau: free desktop download used by individual analysts, a community built through its championship event, then sales teams sent to companies with heavy usage. The instructor says this love for the product is why Salesforce acquired it.
- Miro and Canva: named as following the same route from B2C to B2B.
- Zoho: named as a B2B company that grew without a large sales force.
- Excel: the instructor says it is the reason people buy Office.
- Netflix: found its first customers through physical placement and hoardings telling people a movie could be posted to their home.
- Spotify: users move to premium when ads and limits get in the way, which the instructor calls monetising the point of value realisation.
- Reddit: the instructor uses it to press the point that founders should look at net profit and not revenue.

## Numbers given

- 3 to 1, the instructor's minimum ratio of lifetime value to customer acquisition cost.
- A virality coefficient above 1 for exponential growth.
- A billion users, the instructor's figure for monetising users at scale.
- Five to eight years, the instructor's range for a product-led sales strategy to pay off.
- 16 teams and 30 percent cheaper, the instructor's illustration of the Figma enterprise pitch.
- 2010 to 2011, when the instructor says Canva's first prototype came out.
