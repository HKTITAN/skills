# Applying to YC

This file answers how to apply to Y Combinator, what happens in the interview, what the people on the other side are looking for, and how to use the batch. The speakers are Dalton Caldwell, in a talk the library dates to 2018, and Paul Graham, in an essay printed December 2020, with a few lines from a guide for students by Tamanna Khemani and Kat Manalac. Every detail of the programme here, such as interview length, batch length and shares of a batch, is a fact about the year its piece was made; the programme has changed since, and current terms are on ycombinator.com, not in these pieces.

## What goes in the application, and how long should it take?

- Caldwell, in a talk the library dates to 2018, says filling in the application is worth it regardless of whether the founders do Y Combinator, because it forces them to answer what they are building, how it differs from other options, who the competitors are and what the equity split is. (How to apply and succeed at Y Combinator, https://www.ycombinator.com/library/6t-how-to-apply-and-succeed-at-y-combinator)
- Caldwell, in a talk the library dates to 2018, estimates the application should take two or three hours, and argues the possible upside is large next to other ways of spending the same time. (How to apply and succeed at Y Combinator, https://www.ycombinator.com/library/6t-how-to-apply-and-succeed-at-y-combinator)
- Caldwell, in a talk the library dates to 2018, says applications sent early or after the deadline are still accepted, though it is slightly harder to get in that way because it falls outside the main period for going through them. (How to apply and succeed at Y Combinator, https://www.ycombinator.com/library/6t-how-to-apply-and-succeed-at-y-combinator)
- Caldwell says a surprising share of applicants leave questions unanswered, omit URLs or their bio, or submit with many errors, and that this lack of professionalism does not go with success, since humans, Caldwell among them, read the applications. (How to apply and succeed at Y Combinator, https://www.ycombinator.com/library/6t-how-to-apply-and-succeed-at-y-combinator)
- Caldwell, in a talk the library dates to 2018, says the founder video directions ask for one minute with all the founders in it, and that 20-minute videos or product demos with no humans in them do not follow those directions. (How to apply and succeed at Y Combinator, https://www.ycombinator.com/library/6t-how-to-apply-and-succeed-at-y-combinator)
- Khemani and Manalac write in an undated post that no business plan is needed to apply, and their tip is to be concise and explain the idea simply. (Startups for students, https://www.ycombinator.com/library/D0-startups-for-students)

## What are the application readers looking for?

- Caldwell says the applications he knows will get in are effortless to read: no jargon, everything adds up, and the narrative holds together. (How to apply and succeed at Y Combinator, https://www.ycombinator.com/library/6t-how-to-apply-and-succeed-at-y-combinator)
- Caldwell says weak applications hide the idea, who writes the code, how the product works and whether it is launched or has users, so that reading one is like solving a puzzle, and he says this does not help the applicant. (How to apply and succeed at Y Combinator, https://www.ycombinator.com/library/6t-how-to-apply-and-succeed-at-y-combinator)
- Caldwell holds up the Remix answer, about three sentences long, as a model, because it takes almost no effort to see what the company is, what it does and who it is for. (How to apply and succeed at Y Combinator, https://www.ycombinator.com/library/6t-how-to-apply-and-succeed-at-y-combinator)
- Caldwell lists what he tries to pull out of any application: who is on the team, what they are working on, why, what they have done so far, even if that is little, and why they will make it work. (How to apply and succeed at Y Combinator, https://www.ycombinator.com/library/6t-how-to-apply-and-succeed-at-y-combinator)
- Caldwell's number one tip for applicants is to make clear that at least one person on the team can make the thing, and he says this means the relevant expertise, such as a scientist for a cancer cure, and not always a programmer. (How to apply and succeed at Y Combinator, https://www.ycombinator.com/library/6t-how-to-apply-and-succeed-at-y-combinator)
- Caldwell says teams without a known software background are still funded, but the burden of proof is on them, and he gives the example of a self-taught programmer who built the prototype alone and got in. (How to apply and succeed at Y Combinator, https://www.ycombinator.com/library/6t-how-to-apply-and-succeed-at-y-combinator)
- Caldwell, in a talk the library dates to 2018, says an application that follows the directions, is well written, shows someone who can build the product and shows the founders are serious gets about four times higher odds of an interview. (How to apply and succeed at Y Combinator, https://www.ycombinator.com/library/6t-how-to-apply-and-succeed-at-y-combinator)
- Caldwell says application readers are on the applicant's side, looking for evidence of seriousness and effort and not for reasons to pick the company apart. (How to apply and succeed at Y Combinator, https://www.ycombinator.com/library/6t-how-to-apply-and-succeed-at-y-combinator)
- Graham's 2020 essay says what Y Combinator looks for above all is founders who understand some group of users and can make what they want. (Billionaires Build, https://paulgraham.com/ace.html)
- Khemani and Manalac write in an undated post that the programme likes teams with the ability to build, usually meaning a technical founder, and that about 60 percent of the companies it funds have no launched product. (Startups for students, https://www.ycombinator.com/library/D0-startups-for-students)

## What happens in the interview?

- Caldwell, in a talk the library dates to 2018, says interviews last 10 minutes, are held in person with the full founding team expected, and usually have three to four people from Y Combinator in them. (How to apply and succeed at Y Combinator, https://www.ycombinator.com/library/6t-how-to-apply-and-succeed-at-y-combinator)
- Graham's 2020 essay says interviews were made as short as possible, 10 minutes, so that as many founders as possible could get a chance at funding. (Billionaires Build, https://paulgraham.com/ace.html)
- Caldwell says the questions are basic, with no trick questions: whether you are launched, how you are getting users, what the next step is and how this gets big. (How to apply and succeed at Y Combinator, https://www.ycombinator.com/library/6t-how-to-apply-and-succeed-at-y-combinator)
- Graham's 2020 essay says interviews take many forms depending on what the partners most want to know: some are all about the founders, some all about the idea, some about one narrow part of it. (Billionaires Build, https://paulgraham.com/ace.html)
- Graham's 2020 essay says the interview is not a pitch: ten minutes is too short to judge founders from the indirect evidence of a pitch, so the partners need to dig in with questions in any order. (Billionaires Build, https://paulgraham.com/ace.html)
- Graham calls the advice to take control of the interview and deliver your own message the worst he has heard, because a prepared block of pitch in place of an answer uses up the 10 minutes fast. (Billionaires Build, https://paulgraham.com/ace.html)
- Caldwell says much online advice on preparing is a little wrong because it frames the interview as an adversarial contest to beat, and he thinks those posts are usually written by people who did not get in. (How to apply and succeed at Y Combinator, https://www.ycombinator.com/library/6t-how-to-apply-and-succeed-at-y-combinator)
- Graham's 2020 essay says one of the best ways to prepare is to go and talk to your users and find out exactly what they think, since the best thing to do in the interview is to teach the partners about your users. (Billionaires Build, https://paulgraham.com/ace.html)
- Caldwell, in a talk the library dates to 2018, says founders who are interviewed and not selected get an email with feedback, usually about the one thing the interviewers were most concerned about. (How to apply and succeed at Y Combinator, https://www.ycombinator.com/library/6t-how-to-apply-and-succeed-at-y-combinator)
- Caldwell says the way to use that feedback is to apply for the next batch showing either that you proved it wrong or that you addressed it, and that the interviewers love being disproven. (How to apply and succeed at Y Combinator, https://www.ycombinator.com/library/6t-how-to-apply-and-succeed-at-y-combinator)

## What is the interview really testing?

- Graham's 2020 essay says the first thing the partners usually try to work out is whether there is a path to a huge market for what you are making, which need not be wanted by a lot of people yet. (Billionaires Build, https://paulgraham.com/ace.html)
- Graham says the usual path is a small market that can grow, which he names a "larval market", with Apple's market in 1976 as his example. (Billionaires Build, https://paulgraham.com/ace.html)
- Graham says the biggest flaw in most startup ideas is that the first market does not exist: some people must want the product now, urgently enough to use it with its bugs from a small company they have never heard of. (Billionaires Build, https://paulgraham.com/ace.html)
- Graham says the partners will almost certainly dig into who the first users will be and how you know they want this, and that the second is the one question he would use if he had to decide funding on a single question. (Billionaires Build, https://paulgraham.com/ace.html)
- Graham says the most convincing answer is that you and your friends want it, and that if a crude prototype is already being used by friends and spreading by word of mouth, the partners switch from default no to default yes. (Billionaires Build, https://paulgraham.com/ace.html)
- Graham says Airbnb did not meet that standard, because it was not spreading, and that in practice the partners are satisfied if they feel the founders deeply understand their users' needs. (Billionaires Build, https://paulgraham.com/ace.html)
- Graham says the partners want to use the founders as the domain experts on their own idea, which works only if the partners are convinced the founders know what they are talking about and are not lying. (Billionaires Build, https://paulgraham.com/ace.html)
- Graham says to answer candidly: if you do not know, say so and explain how you would find out, because the partners are practised at detecting a bluff and may not tell you that it failed. (Billionaires Build, https://paulgraham.com/ace.html)
- Graham says the worst answer to a question about what could go wrong is "nothing", and that going into detail about the risks is what experts do. (Billionaires Build, https://paulgraham.com/ace.html)
- Caldwell makes the matching point: founders who claim to have everything figured out show a lack of self-awareness, and a sense of what is hard about the business is expected. (How to apply and succeed at Y Combinator, https://www.ycombinator.com/library/6t-how-to-apply-and-succeed-at-y-combinator)
- Graham says competitors rarely kill startups and will not be held against you much, but seeming unaware of them or minimizing the threat they pose will be. (Billionaires Build, https://paulgraham.com/ace.html)
- Graham says once the partners are convinced there is a path to a big market, the question becomes whether these founders can find it, which depends on their general qualities, their expertise in the domain and the relationship between them. (Billionaires Build, https://paulgraham.com/ace.html)
- Graham says the story of the Airbnb founders funding themselves by making Obama and McCain themed breakfast cereal was the single most important factor in the decision to fund them, because it showed they were resourceful, determined and able to work together. (Billionaires Build, https://paulgraham.com/ace.html)
- Graham says what Y Combinator looks for in founders is authenticity: founders who seem to be doing it just for money or just to seem cool are not likely to succeed on a big scale. (Billionaires Build, https://paulgraham.com/ace.html)
- Caldwell says much of what he looks for is whether he can have a productive conversation with the founder, because funding a company means working very closely with its founders from then on. (How to apply and succeed at Y Combinator, https://www.ycombinator.com/library/6t-how-to-apply-and-succeed-at-y-combinator)
- Caldwell says in a successful interview the founders show mastery of their own business: their numbers, how many users they have, their plans, their equity split and what they meant by anything in the application. (How to apply and succeed at Y Combinator, https://www.ycombinator.com/library/6t-how-to-apply-and-succeed-at-y-combinator)

## Am I too early, too late, or the wrong kind of company to apply?

- Caldwell, in a talk the library dates to 2018, says there is no such thing as too early: some founders changed their idea between the interview invitation and the interview, others pivoted mid batch, and the monthly recurring revenue needed to get in is zero. (How to apply and succeed at Y Combinator, https://www.ycombinator.com/library/6t-how-to-apply-and-succeed-at-y-combinator)
- Caldwell, in a talk the library dates to 2018, says most founders who think they are too far along are not, since every batch has included companies with over $1 million in annual recurring revenue, with 20 or 30 employees, or with a fair amount of money raised. (How to apply and succeed at Y Combinator, https://www.ycombinator.com/library/6t-how-to-apply-and-succeed-at-y-combinator)
- Caldwell says being a solo founder is not a reason to hold back from applying, while noting that the programme encourages founders to have co-founders. (How to apply and succeed at Y Combinator, https://www.ycombinator.com/library/6t-how-to-apply-and-succeed-at-y-combinator)
- Caldwell, in a talk the library dates to 2018, says having already raised money is extremely common, with a large share of the previous batch having raised on the order of six figures and some seven. (How to apply and succeed at Y Combinator, https://www.ycombinator.com/library/6t-how-to-apply-and-succeed-at-y-combinator)
- Caldwell, in a talk the library dates to 2018, says about a third of the previous batch had applied before without getting in, and that a second application showing progress is a strong positive signal. (How to apply and succeed at Y Combinator, https://www.ycombinator.com/library/6t-how-to-apply-and-succeed-at-y-combinator)
- Khemani and Manalac write in an undated post that about 50 percent of the companies funded these days have applied more than once, and that a founder applying a second time needs to show progress. (Startups for students, https://www.ycombinator.com/library/D0-startups-for-students)
- Caldwell says it is basically impossible to network your way in, that the vast majority of accepted founders are complete strangers to the partners, and that repeated cold emails and mailed objects do not help. (How to apply and succeed at Y Combinator, https://www.ycombinator.com/library/6t-how-to-apply-and-succeed-at-y-combinator)
- Caldwell warns that alumni and others near the network give well-intentioned bad advice, such as waiting for $5k MRR or offering to put in a good word, and says to be skeptical of it. (How to apply and succeed at Y Combinator, https://www.ycombinator.com/library/6t-how-to-apply-and-succeed-at-y-combinator)
- Caldwell names two good reasons not to apply: a business that is not expected to grow fast enough for venture capital to make sense, and founders who are not sure they want to work on it for very long. (How to apply and succeed at Y Combinator, https://www.ycombinator.com/library/6t-how-to-apply-and-succeed-at-y-combinator)
- Caldwell, in a talk the library dates to 2018, says the programme is three months in the Bay Area and founders then go and do what they want, so wanting to live in another city does not rule out applying. (How to apply and succeed at Y Combinator, https://www.ycombinator.com/library/6t-how-to-apply-and-succeed-at-y-combinator)
- Caldwell says many worries about applying stem from fear of not getting in, and that founders create luck by setting themselves up to be lucky as often as possible, which means being told no often. (How to apply and succeed at Y Combinator, https://www.ycombinator.com/library/6t-how-to-apply-and-succeed-at-y-combinator)

## How do I use the batch once I am in?

- Caldwell, in a talk the library dates to 2018, says the founders in a partner's group get that partner's cell phone number and call and text all the time, and that the working relationship lasts a long time. (How to apply and succeed at Y Combinator, https://www.ycombinator.com/library/6t-how-to-apply-and-succeed-at-y-combinator)
- Caldwell says one thing worked on a lot in the batch is being clear: making sure the other party understands what you do first, and selling them second. (How to apply and succeed at Y Combinator, https://www.ycombinator.com/library/6t-how-to-apply-and-succeed-at-y-combinator)
- Caldwell says the programme also works on encouraging founders not to be so afraid of being told no. (How to apply and succeed at Y Combinator, https://www.ycombinator.com/library/6t-how-to-apply-and-succeed-at-y-combinator)
- Caldwell points to Justin Kan, who did Y Combinator three times and by Caldwell's account gets something out of it every time, as his answer to founders who think they are too experienced. (How to apply and succeed at Y Combinator, https://www.ycombinator.com/library/6t-how-to-apply-and-succeed-at-y-combinator)
- Graham recalls that Airbnb was not growing yet when the partners funded it, and that its founders figured out how to make it grow about three weeks into the batch. (Billionaires Build, https://paulgraham.com/ace.html)
- Khemani and Manalac write in an undated post that founders in the batch should expect to be writing code, talking to users, solving problems, and taking part in weekly batch events, group activities and office hours. (Startups for students, https://www.ycombinator.com/library/D0-startups-for-students)
