# Interfaces past and future

He treats the interface as a subject in its own right: every look has a cause, every pattern was invented by someone, and each kind of interface is good at one thing and bad at another. Most of this comes from what-happened-to-user-interfaces, the-weird-death-of-user-interfaces, the-weird-future-of-user-interfaces, the-ridiculous-engineering-of-text, the-most-important-tiny-decision-in-tech-history, social-media-is-broken-but-we-can-fix-it and how-silicon-valley-killed-fun. His view moved a lot between 2022 and 2026, so read every prediction and every verdict on AI with its year; the Figma and Lovable videos were made with those companies' teams.

## How did interface styles change over time?

- He holds that the look of an interface is never random and always reflects its moment: the 1960s Space Age look came from a real space age, and Frutiger Aero came from computers and the internet going mainstream. (What Happened To User Interfaces at 0:00, https://www.youtube.com/watch?v=HdozqSVF-VM&t=0s)
- He says interfaces in the 80s and 90s were minimal and grey only because the hardware could do nothing else; his own first computer showed 256 colours. (What Happened To User Interfaces at 1:30, https://www.youtube.com/watch?v=HdozqSVF-VM&t=90s)
- He says Frutiger Aero existed to make tech **anti-scary**: intimidating grey boxes gave way to colour, bubbles, glassy effects, the Windows XP hills and Apple's watery Aqua buttons. (What Happened To User Interfaces at 0:45, https://www.youtube.com/watch?v=HdozqSVF-VM&t=45s)
- Once technology caught up, he says designers went wild because they could: HTC Sense had 3D elements and a metal ring you pulled up to unlock, and the original Xbox menu took far more work than today's minimal UIs. He calls that era a celebration of technology catching up with creativity. (What Happened To User Interfaces at 2:15, https://www.youtube.com/watch?v=HdozqSVF-VM&t=135s)
- He calls that period the golden age of personalisation and says he misses it. Skins and themes were sometimes excessive or cringe but cool, and after them came flat design, whites and greys, where you are lucky to get a dark mode. (What Happened To User Interfaces at 3:00, https://www.youtube.com/watch?v=HdozqSVF-VM&t=180s)
- He describes grey, minimal interfaces in the 80s and 90s forced by technical limits, the Frutiger Aero and Aqua look that made tech less scary as computers went mainstream, and a because-they-can era of skins, themes and 3D effects once technology caught up. (What Happened To User Interfaces at 0:45, https://www.youtube.com/watch?v=HdozqSVF-VM&t=45s)
- He says the real revolution of graphical computers was metaphors, not screens. Designers had to teach millions of people who had never seen a computer what a file or dragging was, so a folder holds things and the desktop is the top of your desk. (Why Our Tech Got Silent at 0:45, https://www.youtube.com/watch?v=_rFJutK25VU&t=45s)
- He uses Microsoft Bob as the wildest case of real-world metaphor, where the desktop is literally a desk and email is a parrot, and says it also shows how badly designers wanted text off the screen. (The Lies That Make Your Tech Actually Work at 4:30, https://www.youtube.com/watch?v=rAVV4kAYlI8&t=270s; The Ridiculous Engineering Of Text at 10:30, https://www.youtube.com/watch?v=7DN3Ectm6UY&t=630s)
- He says early iOS used textures, fabrics and lookalikes such as a Newsstand shaped like a bookshelf to make an unusual device feel familiar. Once people no longer needed that, things got simpler and more conceptual, which drove the iOS 7 flat redesign and Windows Phone. (What Happened To User Interfaces at 6:45, https://www.youtube.com/watch?v=HdozqSVF-VM&t=405s)
- He says technology rewired our brains and that this is what made minimal design possible: we now know where to scroll and tap without cues, so designers could drop explicit affordances and skeuomorphism. (The Lies That Make Your Tech Actually Work at 5:15, https://www.youtube.com/watch?v=rAVV4kAYlI8&t=315s)
- In January 2026 he said the old metaphors are dying. A teenager knows the save icon only because save buttons look like that, and younger people increasingly do not sort files into folders because search, and now AI, finds things. (Why Our Tech Got Silent at 8:15, https://www.youtube.com/watch?v=_rFJutK25VU&t=495s)
- He says digital interfaces took over because they broke the idea that complex technology needs complex controls. A phone has a thousand times the complexity of a Concorde-era device and three buttons, because the complexity moved from **atoms to pixels**. (What Happened To Physical Tech at 2:15, https://www.youtube.com/watch?v=TIFF-rinlbo&t=135s)
- He says the move from physical to digital took a real change of mindset: in 1984 Apple took a 20-page magazine ad just to explain the mouse, pointing and what a button is. (What Happened To Physical Tech at 3:45, https://www.youtube.com/watch?v=TIFF-rinlbo&t=225s)
- He says Douglas Engelbart's demo of December 9 1968 showed the mouse, video conferencing on a shared document, hyperlinks and movable document blocks, and that for the next 15 years almost nothing from it reached the market. (The Man Who Made Apple Possible In 1968 at 1:30, https://www.youtube.com/watch?v=BXSHJXHeaqM&t=90s; The Man Who Made Apple Possible In 1968 at 3:00, https://www.youtube.com/watch?v=BXSHJXHeaqM&t=180s)
- He says people who left Engelbart's lab for Xerox PARC came up with the graphical interface, icons and windows, but neither they nor Xerox understood the potential; a visit by Steve Jobs led Apple to the Macintosh in 1984. (The Man Who Made Apple Possible In 1968 at 3:00, https://www.youtube.com/watch?v=BXSHJXHeaqM&t=180s; The Man Who Made Apple Possible In 1968 at 3:45, https://www.youtube.com/watch?v=BXSHJXHeaqM&t=225s)
- In 2023 he drew the history of interfaces on a chart of how human-like the interaction is: punch cards and assembly, the graphical interface, the touchscreen, voice assistants, then generative AI as the next leap. He says each jump created a new generation of technology and trillions of dollars of value. (Smartphones Are Dead But We Finally Know What's Next at 2:15, https://www.youtube.com/watch?v=pB6TdukUsAg&t=135s)

## Why did interfaces go bland?

- In July 2024 he said apps and websites all look basically the same, bland, boring and lifeless, and said three key things are to blame, the first being usability and another being design patterns. (What Happened To User Interfaces at 0:00, https://www.youtube.com/watch?v=HdozqSVF-VM&t=0s; What Happened To User Interfaces at 3:45, https://www.youtube.com/watch?v=HdozqSVF-VM&t=225s)
- He says usability is scientific: you measure how long people take and where they click, the plainer interface wins the test, and so products became simpler and more approachable but also more boring. He adds that usability is great and that he worked for a company making usability testing tools at the time. (What Happened To User Interfaces at 3:45, https://www.youtube.com/watch?v=HdozqSVF-VM&t=225s; What Happened To User Interfaces at 4:30, https://www.youtube.com/watch?v=HdozqSVF-VM&t=270s)
- He says the best products feel intuitive and need no manual, and contrasts the Space Shuttle cockpit, which looks like ten years of training, with the SpaceX Dragon capsule, which makes you feel you could fly it. (What Happened To User Interfaces at 3:45, https://www.youtube.com/watch?v=HdozqSVF-VM&t=225s)
- His small example of usability chipping away at charm is Spotify swapping the heart for a plus icon. In 2025 he was blunter: he guessed someone in a usability test did not understand the heart, and said the plus is clearer and more efficient but has no soul. (What Happened To User Interfaces at 4:30, https://www.youtube.com/watch?v=HdozqSVF-VM&t=270s; How Silicon Valley Killed Fun at 6:00, https://www.youtube.com/watch?v=DYdLq9icgrI&t=360s)
- In November 2025 he gave a second cause: tech became lifeless because operators who optimize by metrics replaced the creators who built for fun. He dates the turn to 2011 when Google killed Google Labs, 2012 when 20% time needed manager approval, and 2013 when iOS 7 dropped skeuomorphism. (How Silicon Valley Killed Fun at 4:30, https://www.youtube.com/watch?v=DYdLq9icgrI&t=270s)
- He says products came to be built by brute forcing numbers: ten variations of a button, ship the winner, minimize clicks for the least tech-savvy user. Under that method textures, quirks and Easter eggs count as noise and get cut. (How Silicon Valley Killed Fun at 5:15, https://www.youtube.com/watch?v=DYdLq9icgrI&t=315s)
- He says metrics are why every product looks the same. Netflix tested everything for over a decade, found that bigger poster art, horizontal rows and autoplay previews raise engagement, and competitors either tested their way to the same result or copied it. (How Silicon Valley Killed Fun at 7:30, https://www.youtube.com/watch?v=DYdLq9icgrI&t=450s)
- He says minimalism sometimes goes too far and leaves no way to know how you can interact with something, and asks whether you even know you can swipe on Spotify's player to change songs. (The Lies That Make Your Tech Actually Work at 6:00, https://www.youtube.com/watch?v=rAVV4kAYlI8&t=360s)
- He says science fiction shows the opposite extreme, interfaces built only to look cool. Minority Report controls are great on film, but imagine answering email that way all day. He calls those interfaces art and says that artistic element has been lost in real products. (What Happened To User Interfaces at 9:00, https://www.youtube.com/watch?v=HdozqSVF-VM&t=540s; What Happened To User Interfaces at 9:45, https://www.youtube.com/watch?v=HdozqSVF-VM&t=585s)
- He defends ornament directly. "Sometimes the unnecessary is what makes things beautiful." His examples are the over-the-top Wallet animation for deleting a ticket and the small details around doors and windows in his hometown. (What Happened To User Interfaces at 7:30, https://www.youtube.com/watch?v=HdozqSVF-VM&t=450s)

## Why are interfaces getting interesting again?

- In 2024 he argued that because functional software keeps getting easier to make, beauty and craft are now how you stand out. He cites Rivian drawing its trucks in an anime style with Unreal Engine, and Arc inventing a swipeable sidebar and bringing back colour pickers. (What Happened To User Interfaces at 10:30, https://www.youtube.com/watch?v=HdozqSVF-VM&t=630s)
- He gives two routes to making tech cool again: go bold with new patterns and customisation as Arc and Rivian do, or stay understated and add micro interactions such as Airbnb's photo animation and the Apple Pencil's virtual shadow. (What Happened To User Interfaces at 11:15, https://www.youtube.com/watch?v=HdozqSVF-VM&t=675s)
- He says we leaned too far into minimalism and are rebounding, because however simple we want things, people crave beauty and craft. (What Happened To User Interfaces at 12:00, https://www.youtube.com/watch?v=HdozqSVF-VM&t=720s)
- He frames creators against operators as a pendulum, not a switch: the 90s and 2000s drew engineers, designers and artists into tech, the 2010s drew MBAs and operators, and he says the pendulum is starting to swing back. (How Silicon Valley Killed Fun at 9:45, https://www.youtube.com/watch?v=DYdLq9icgrI&t=585s)
- In November 2025 he said you could see the swing in interfaces: Apple's liquid glass, the Comet browser onboarding, the Atlas shimmer, a personality for Copilot, Notion's characters and hats, Airbnb's 3D icons, and YouTube like animations that change with the video topic. (How Silicon Valley Killed Fun at 14:15, https://www.youtube.com/watch?v=DYdLq9icgrI&t=855s)
- He says fun features are worth shipping even when they move no metric. Of YouTube's like animations he says they are not necessary, will not improve conversion or engagement and may confuse someone, but are fun; of a jokey product-page footer he says it would never have flown five or ten years earlier and would have been A/B tested to death. (How Silicon Valley Killed Fun at 15:45, https://www.youtube.com/watch?v=DYdLq9icgrI&t=945s)
- Two months earlier, in September 2025, he read the same polish the other way: a simple toggle had become real-time physics, glass refraction and animated 3D icons, and he called that level of refinement the sign that graphical interfaces had reached their end. (The Weird Death Of User Interfaces at 0:00, https://www.youtube.com/watch?v=KG4ONHqF1qg&t=0s)
- In October 2024 he said tech products had always tried to be perfect, symmetrical and neatly on a grid, but were starting to take first steps toward imperfection: Material You takes colours from your wallpaper and uses odd shapes, and iOS 18 lets people tint icons into combinations he expects to be horrific, which he calls human and imperfect. (Why Ugly Things Are Cool Now at 6:45, https://www.youtube.com/watch?v=q1SWw7i9LQc&t=405s)
- In September 2024 he said tech went too far in replacing physical controls with touch, because pressing buttons and turning knobs is human nature. His proof is the Touch Bar, which was more capable than keys and so hated that Apple reversed it. (What Happened To Physical Tech at 6:00, https://www.youtube.com/watch?v=TIFF-rinlbo&t=360s; What Happened To Physical Tech at 6:45, https://www.youtube.com/watch?v=TIFF-rinlbo&t=405s)
- In March 2024 he said the keyboard was coming back as a main input because the mouse is intuitive but slow: Arc teaches its command bar first, and Linear and Superhuman are built around a bar where you type any action without memorizing commands. (The Secrets That Make Your Phone Actually Work at 9:45, https://www.youtube.com/watch?v=-YVtZV4DjCE&t=585s)

## What are design patterns, and why does every app look the same?

- He blames design patterns for almost every website and app looking alike: people are creatures of habit, so once a design like the hamburger menu or the bottom bar is established anything that deviates looks off, the patterns harden into rules, and you are left playing with colours. (What Happened To User Interfaces at 5:15, https://www.youtube.com/watch?v=HdozqSVF-VM&t=315s)
- He says the circle around a profile picture tells billions of people a whole rulebook in milliseconds: photos and videos from the last 24 hours that will disappear, videos no longer than 30 seconds, progress rectangles on top, tap either side to move. (Social Media Is Broken But We Can Fix It at 0:00, https://www.youtube.com/watch?v=HqYIUV7Eqjk&t=0s)
- He calls design patterns the hidden way tech changes the behaviour of billions of people, and says that without them you could not use your tech at all. (Social Media Is Broken But We Can Fix It at 0:45, https://www.youtube.com/watch?v=HqYIUV7Eqjk&t=45s)
- He says thinking different on basic patterns kills an app. In his made-up shop, 3D category swiping and triple tap to buy each need a tutorial and nobody uses it; a normal grid, search bar and add-to-cart button need no explaining, make money, and look like every other shop. (Social Media Is Broken But We Can Fix It at 1:30, https://www.youtube.com/watch?v=HqYIUV7Eqjk&t=90s; Social Media Is Broken But We Can Fix It at 2:15, https://www.youtube.com/watch?v=HqYIUV7Eqjk&t=135s)
- His first rule of patterns: "Once the design pattern works, everyone adopts it." Snapchat launched stories, Instagram copied them, and for a while even YouTube had them. He sees this as an opening, since one new pattern can redesign the whole internet. (Social Media Is Broken But We Can Fix It at 3:00, https://www.youtube.com/watch?v=HqYIUV7Eqjk&t=180s)
- He calls "intuitive and simple" golden rule number one of design patterns, with pull to refresh as the perfect example, and he adds that designing something simple and intuitive is not simple at all. (Social Media Is Broken But We Can Fix It at 7:30, https://www.youtube.com/watch?v=HqYIUV7Eqjk&t=450s; Social Media Is Broken But We Can Fix It at 8:15, https://www.youtube.com/watch?v=HqYIUV7Eqjk&t=495s)
- His last and, he says, most controversial golden rule is that a pattern must be good for the company that implements it, so just making social media less addictive does not work: a social media company will only adopt a design that gives it a benefit. (Social Media Is Broken But We Can Fix It at 12:00, https://www.youtube.com/watch?v=HqYIUV7Eqjk&t=720s)
- He calls the vertical video feed the ultimate case of a pattern taking over and possibly the most replicated interface ever made: TikTok basically invented it and Instagram, YouTube, Spotify and Amazon followed. (Social Media Is Broken But We Can Fix It at 3:00, https://www.youtube.com/watch?v=HqYIUV7Eqjk&t=180s; The Ridiculous Engineering Of Short Form Content at 5:15, https://www.youtube.com/watch?v=69Gw7aoWMMI&t=315s)
- He uses Jakob's law to explain why all chat apps look alike: people spend most of their time in other products, so they want yours to work the same way. He thinks a better chat design probably exists but that habit makes it almost impossible to change without something groundbreaking. (The Insane Design Of Messaging at 15:00, https://www.youtube.com/watch?v=6DSW1rN-ZV4&t=900s)
- He traces the chat interface to one Apple engineer, Jens Alfke, who kept vertical scrolling, put your messages on the right and wrapped each in a comic-style balloon. He says the first iPhone spread it everywhere and that all chat today, AI tools and agents included, descends from it. (The Insane Design Of Messaging at 12:45, https://www.youtube.com/watch?v=6DSW1rN-ZV4&t=765s; The Insane Design Of Messaging at 14:15, https://www.youtube.com/watch?v=6DSW1rN-ZV4&t=855s)
- He says someone had to **invent the obvious**: stories you grasp in a tenth of a second and dragging a corner to resize feel inevitable only because a person once designed them. (The Ridiculous Engineering Of Figma at 19:30, https://www.youtube.com/watch?v=5t8dh3DSdBk&t=1170s)
- He says patterns keep evolving. In 2024 he pointed to Apple's hybrid of tabs and sidebar in iPadOS as a new one, and in January 2025 to the Dynamic Island as Apple's latest attempt, which Android had not adopted. (What Happened To User Interfaces at 6:00, https://www.youtube.com/watch?v=HdozqSVF-VM&t=360s; Social Media Is Broken But We Can Fix It at 8:15, https://www.youtube.com/watch?v=HqYIUV7Eqjk&t=495s)
- He says the shape of a text box and the job of the enter key changed how billions write. Email's multi-line box, where enter makes a new line, produces long formal messages; chat's single line, where enter sends, produces short casual ones. (Social Media Is Broken But We Can Fix It at 10:30, https://www.youtube.com/watch?v=HqYIUV7Eqjk&t=630s; The Insane Design Of Messaging at 3:00, https://www.youtube.com/watch?v=6DSW1rN-ZV4&t=180s)
- He says users themselves keep old interfaces alive. QWERTY was laid out in the 1870s to stop typewriter keys jamming, the faster Dvorak layout lost anyway, and loss aversion means people will not throw away years of learning. (The Weird Reason Your Tech Stays Broken at 12:00, https://www.youtube.com/watch?v=-51ocAD6zBI&t=720s)

## What decides whether an interface works?

- His core input theory, from March 2026, says every device is defined by one core interaction that everything else builds on, and that once you understand it you can predict which products will succeed and which will fail before they launch. (The Most Important Tiny Decision In Tech History at 0:45, https://www.youtube.com/watch?v=7EVZpW9Rdwk&t=45s; The Most Important Tiny Decision In Tech History at 10:30, https://www.youtube.com/watch?v=7EVZpW9Rdwk&t=630s)
- He says a phone does not register a tap when your finger lands; it waits to see if you move. In Android's source the tap timeout is 100 milliseconds and the touch slop is 8 dp, and he reports the iPhone as about 150 milliseconds. (The Most Important Tiny Decision In Tech History at 4:30, https://www.youtube.com/watch?v=7EVZpW9Rdwk&t=270s)
- He sums up the phone as "Scrolling comes first, and tapping comes second." On a computer the click comes first, and he calls this difference the main reason smartphones as we know them won. (The Most Important Tiny Decision In Tech History at 5:15, https://www.youtube.com/watch?v=7EVZpW9Rdwk&t=315s)
- He says that when scrolling is the main interaction the screen itself becomes the control, which is why arrows and buttons vanish on mobile and why draggable carousels feel clunky on desktop. (The Most Important Tiny Decision In Tech History at 6:00, https://www.youtube.com/watch?v=7EVZpW9Rdwk&t=360s)
- He says scrolling as a core input allows only a few actions, one to maybe four, that feel incredibly natural, while clicking lets you do way more things (a desktop can show 30 buttons at once) in a less natural way, and that this is why the phone ended up a consumption device and the laptop a creation and work device. (The Most Important Tiny Decision In Tech History at 9:00, https://www.youtube.com/watch?v=7EVZpW9Rdwk&t=540s; The Most Important Tiny Decision In Tech History at 10:30, https://www.youtube.com/watch?v=7EVZpW9Rdwk&t=630s)
- He says smart TVs are hated because the interface and the core input do not match: rows of thumbnails made for scrolling are driven by four arrows and a button. He adds that Apple's 2015 touch remote proved you cannot transplant one device's core input into another. (The Most Important Tiny Decision In Tech History at 12:00, https://www.youtube.com/watch?v=7EVZpW9Rdwk&t=720s; The Most Important Tiny Decision In Tech History at 12:45, https://www.youtube.com/watch?v=7EVZpW9Rdwk&t=765s)
- In November 2023 he set a test for any new interface: it must be easier than the one before it. Touchscreens beat buttons that way, and the AI Pin's projector failed it, since double tapping an earbud beats holding out a hand, waiting, tilting and pinching. (We Need To Talk About The AI Pin at 4:30, https://www.youtube.com/watch?v=E2-L-Gv0wr0&t=270s)
- In 2026 he applied core input to new hardware: the AI Pin's projector and gestures looked broken even in its demo, while everyone who tried Meta's neural band, himself included, found it natural, so he could see it succeeding. (The Most Important Tiny Decision In Tech History at 12:45, https://www.youtube.com/watch?v=7EVZpW9Rdwk&t=765s)
- He names **direct manipulation** as the idea that makes graphical interfaces work: you pinch a photo instead of typing a zoom command and drag a file to the trash instead of describing it, and everything you can do is in front of you. (The Weird Future Of User Interfaces at 6:45, https://www.youtube.com/watch?v=f32W5BEzWN0&t=405s)
- He says direct manipulation breaks down as options grow into menus, submenus, panels and customizable panels, which is how professional tools became scary. Instagram's story editor is what it is great at; After Effects is beyond what it can handle. (The Weird Future Of User Interfaces at 7:30, https://www.youtube.com/watch?v=f32W5BEzWN0&t=450s)
- His central trade-off is that graphical interfaces are great at output and bad at input, while chatbots and voice assistants are the reverse. A UI lays out options and prices but makes you hunt through menus; a chatbot understands a rambling prompt but does not show you information or make you feel in control. (The Weird Death Of User Interfaces at 4:30, https://www.youtube.com/watch?v=KG4ONHqF1qg&t=270s)
- In December 2023 he said it took 15 years to build today's natural touch interactions and that hand-based control for AR would be the challenge of the next ten, with eye tracking hard to shrink into glasses and keyboards a real pain. He predicted this would make or break the phone's successor. (The Lies That Make Your Tech Actually Work at 9:45, https://www.youtube.com/watch?v=rAVV4kAYlI8&t=585s; The Lies That Make Your Tech Actually Work at 10:30, https://www.youtube.com/watch?v=rAVV4kAYlI8&t=630s)
- In 2022 he called human input and output the bottleneck of computing: a computer could do a person's work in seconds, but moving a mouse, typing and reading the screen limit what people and machines do together. (The Man Who Made Apple Possible In 1968 at 6:00, https://www.youtube.com/watch?v=BXSHJXHeaqM&t=360s)
- He says a familiar interface is what makes a new one feel safe: in a Waymo the moving steering wheel made him feel in control, while today's chatbots are like a robotaxi with no wheel or pedals. (The Weird Death Of User Interfaces at 10:30, https://www.youtube.com/watch?v=KG4ONHqF1qg&t=630s)
- Four months earlier, in a May 2025 video made with Lovable, he argued the other side: hiding the code is like removing the steering wheel, and to bring people into the new world you have to take the old one away. (Behind The Scenes Of Lovable's Insane Design at 13:30, https://www.youtube.com/watch?v=ZoVLS-Wxr1s&t=810s)

## What happened to text and the command line?

- He says that for 20 years tech was built to be found by shape and colour, not words: he counts two words on the Apple Watch home screen, six in Shazam and three in the camera app. (The Ridiculous Engineering Of Text at 9:00, https://www.youtube.com/watch?v=7DN3Ectm6UY&t=540s)
- He says graphical interfaces exist because text terminals were above most people's limit: you typed commands perfectly, got no confirmation and had no trash can, and the Commodore 64 needed its manual for nearly everything. (The Ridiculous Engineering Of Text at 9:45, https://www.youtube.com/watch?v=7DN3Ectm6UY&t=585s)
- "Word by word, we started taking text off the screen." He says the delete command became a trash can, the file name a picture you drag, the list of commands a dropdown, and actions a row of symbols. (The Ridiculous Engineering Of Text at 10:30, https://www.youtube.com/watch?v=7DN3Ectm6UY&t=630s)
- His complexity line is the maximum level of complexity your users can take. Early text-only computers sat way above it for regular people, and visuals, graphics and metaphors brought tech down under it so billions could use it. (The Ridiculous Engineering Of Text at 9:00, https://www.youtube.com/watch?v=7DN3Ectm6UY&t=540s)
- In October 2022 he predicted AI would move interfaces from graphical back to conversational, after 40 years spent going from command lines to graphics, and named this **conversational computing**; he hedged on whether the Runway editor he showed would deliver. (AI Generated Videos Are Scary at 6:45, https://www.youtube.com/watch?v=Es01hmaXux0&t=405s)
- In December 2022 he said ChatGPT seemed almost magical because it speaks the language of human conversation, and that he thought it was the first time chatbots had surpassed the uncanny valley. (ChatGPT is Frighteningly Good at 3:00, https://www.youtube.com/watch?v=2lzQD-7lZJU&t=180s)
- In May 2024 he said AI chatbots had recreated **the command line problem** that graphical interfaces solved more than 40 years ago: with ChatGPT and Midjourney people were back to typing text, learning prompts and reading guides. (AI Has A Huge Problem at 9:00, https://www.youtube.com/watch?v=CY_b8w8u9NY&t=540s)
- He also said in 2024 that slapping buttons and sliders onto a model does not fix it: Automatic1111, a graphical front end for Stable Diffusion, is even scarier than a chatbot, and he expected a real answer to take a while. (AI Has A Huge Problem at 11:15, https://www.youtube.com/watch?v=CY_b8w8u9NY&t=675s)
- By September 2026 he explained text's return differently: a machine that can do anything cannot have a bounded interface, almost every flow is a question and an answer, and the simplest interface for that is a chat box. (The Ridiculous Engineering Of Text at 12:00, https://www.youtube.com/watch?v=7DN3Ectm6UY&t=720s)
- He says ChatGPT reached the mainstream where terminals never did because its text is **fuzzy text**: it needs no exact file names and does not break on typos. (The Ridiculous Engineering Of Text at 12:00, https://www.youtube.com/watch?v=7DN3Ectm6UY&t=720s)
- In 2026 he said AI chat took off because its core input works: a text box that forgives errors and rambling beats clicking through 40 submenus, so there is no need for a settings menu or search filters if you can just say what you want. (The Most Important Tiny Decision In Tech History at 13:30, https://www.youtube.com/watch?v=7EVZpW9Rdwk&t=810s)
- In April 2026 he noted that Claude Code, a hard-to-install, terminal-only, black-and-white text app, hooked millions of mostly non-developers, while beautiful, friendly AI products built to be approachable were dead or dying. (Why Everyone is Suddenly Obsessed With Claude Code at 0:00, https://www.youtube.com/watch?v=gzt52Trk9w0&t=0s; Why Everyone is Suddenly Obsessed With Claude Code at 5:15, https://www.youtube.com/watch?v=gzt52Trk9w0&t=315s)
- In September 2026 he said agents had pushed AI tools back above the complexity line: coding, deep research and computer actions all still happen in a chat box and a wall of text, and mainstream users are locked out. He calls bringing these tools back down to regular people one of the most valuable problems in tech. (The Ridiculous Engineering Of Text at 13:30, https://www.youtube.com/watch?v=7DN3Ectm6UY&t=810s; The Ridiculous Engineering Of Text at 14:15, https://www.youtube.com/watch?v=7DN3Ectm6UY&t=855s)
- He said in September 2026 that the industry was back to killing text and making interfaces graphical again, in an experimentation phase: Conductor and Cursor building GUIs for parallel coding agents, NotebookLM making podcasts in place of walls of text, Flora and Higgsfield making prompting visual. (The Ridiculous Engineering Of Text at 15:00, https://www.youtube.com/watch?v=7DN3Ectm6UY&t=900s)

## Where does chat beat buttons, and where does it lose?

- In May 2024 he said chatbots themselves are not the problem; the problem is that for most things a chatbot is the wrong way to interact with a computer. For trip planning it returns a wall of text with no images, maps or easy fare comparison. (AI Has A Huge Problem at 1:30, https://www.youtube.com/watch?v=CY_b8w8u9NY&t=90s; AI Has A Huge Problem at 2:15, https://www.youtube.com/watch?v=CY_b8w8u9NY&t=135s)
- He called the omniscient assistant, the dream behind Jarvis, HAL, Siri and Alexa, a big illusion in 2024, because it needs access to everything and no company will let a rival's bot into its app. (AI Has A Huge Problem at 6:00, https://www.youtube.com/watch?v=CY_b8w8u9NY&t=360s; AI Has A Huge Problem at 7:30, https://www.youtube.com/watch?v=CY_b8w8u9NY&t=450s)
- His 2024 fix was **invisible AI**: start from a real problem, apply AI inside the interface people already know, and do not announce it, because users only care that the problem is solved. (AI Has A Huge Problem at 12:00, https://www.youtube.com/watch?v=CY_b8w8u9NY&t=720s; AI Has A Huge Problem at 12:45, https://www.youtube.com/watch?v=CY_b8w8u9NY&t=765s)
- He says assistants like Alexa are not the right interface for relaying complex information, which is why they ended up used for lights and timers. In the AI Pin's book-buying demo you cannot tell which card is used, where the $28 price comes from or whether it is the best price. (We Need To Talk About The AI Pin at 6:00, https://www.youtube.com/watch?v=E2-L-Gv0wr0&t=360s)
- He says no human would use a pure voice agent like the 2025 Alexa demo: if it books a ride for grandma you cannot see which car, when, what it cost or what happens if the flight is late. (The Weird Death Of User Interfaces at 3:45, https://www.youtube.com/watch?v=KG4ONHqF1qg&t=225s)
- In June 2026 he showed a chat agent losing to a few buttons: the Starbucks plugin for ChatGPT was still working on his drink after the regular app was done, so the most advanced technology ever built lost to a 15-year-old app. (The Weird Future Of User Interfaces at 0:00, https://www.youtube.com/watch?v=f32W5BEzWN0&t=0s)
- He said in 2026 that every attempt to kill the user interface had been a gigantic failure, listing the Humane Pin, which he says raised $230 million, the Rabbit, and the original vision for Siri, Alexa and Google Home; his own Google Home is a light switch and a pasta timer. (The Weird Future Of User Interfaces at 0:45, https://www.youtube.com/watch?v=f32W5BEzWN0&t=45s)
- He says those products failed because they attacked what graphical interfaces already do well: booking a ride, ordering coffee and sending a message are habitual tasks in apps you could open with your eyes closed. (The Weird Future Of User Interfaces at 2:15, https://www.youtube.com/watch?v=f32W5BEzWN0&t=135s)
- He says products are rebuilding themselves for agents: Linear and PostHog changed their look, Salesforce shipped a headless version with no UI, and Notion and Google released command line tools. Companies that spent years on their interfaces are in effect saying you do not want to use them. (The Weird Future Of User Interfaces at 3:45, https://www.youtube.com/watch?v=f32W5BEzWN0&t=225s)
- He says complex professional interfaces make him want an agent: hunting for one feature in Blender's menus makes him sick, and an animation that was painful in After Effects he now makes without touching a GUI button. (The Weird Future Of User Interfaces at 4:30, https://www.youtube.com/watch?v=f32W5BEzWN0&t=270s)
- He says past a certain complexity a graphical interface stops being an asset, because chat is free form and not limited to the buttons a designer built. He calls asking a machine to do it **indirect manipulation**. (The Weird Future Of User Interfaces at 8:15, https://www.youtube.com/watch?v=f32W5BEzWN0&t=495s)
- He rejects the conclusion that everything becomes agentic, controlled by chatbots with no UI, and says that to see what is actually happening you have to understand direct manipulation. (The Weird Future Of User Interfaces at 5:15, https://www.youtube.com/watch?v=f32W5BEzWN0&t=315s)
- He says most everyday tasks are code with a human interface wrapped around it, and that a fixed UI cannot answer odd requests: Skyscanner cannot be asked for a one-day layover in Singapore on the way to Taiwan, but a coding agent can call the data and present an answer. (Why Everyone is Suddenly Obsessed With Claude Code at 9:00, https://www.youtube.com/watch?v=gzt52Trk9w0&t=540s; Why Everyone is Suddenly Obsessed With Claude Code at 9:45, https://www.youtube.com/watch?v=gzt52Trk9w0&t=585s)
- In May 2025 he said probably 80% of AI comes as a chatbot because ChatGPT was what brought AI to the mainstream, making chat the default way we interact with AI, and that a chatbot might not be where things go from there. (Behind The Scenes Of Lovable's Insane Design at 15:45, https://www.youtube.com/watch?v=ZoVLS-Wxr1s&t=945s)
- He relays that Airbnb's CEO Brian Chesky said the company tried chat-based booking, scrapped it because it did not work, and started a lab building models for generative UI. (The Weird Future Of User Interfaces at 12:00, https://www.youtube.com/watch?v=f32W5BEzWN0&t=720s)
- He says the chat layout itself steers a product. In December 2025 he argued that Google is designed to make you leave while the chatbot UI, where the main action is to ask something else, makes ChatGPT a product built for depth and time spent. (How ChatGPT Is Weirdly Turning Into Facebook at 6:00, https://www.youtube.com/watch?v=C7Z11ghwevQ&t=360s)

## What does he think comes after the graphical interface?

- In September 2025 he declared the user interfaces of the past 40 years dead, on the argument that real UI innovation such as pull to refresh, the Start menu, the Dock and slide to unlock is behind us on the S-curve, and that we now cycle flat, glassy, flat, glassy while everything works the same. (The Weird Death Of User Interfaces at 0:00, https://www.youtube.com/watch?v=KG4ONHqF1qg&t=0s; The Weird Death Of User Interfaces at 0:45, https://www.youtube.com/watch?v=KG4ONHqF1qg&t=45s)
- He says today's chatbots feel magical because they solved input, but their output went back to the 1980s command line. (The Weird Death Of User Interfaces at 5:15, https://www.youtube.com/watch?v=KG4ONHqF1qg&t=315s)
- He said in 2025 that the next phase was being built by small companies and individuals while Apple and Google polish glass, and compared it to Xerox PARC inventing the graphical interface before Apple took it mainstream. (The Weird Death Of User Interfaces at 6:00, https://www.youtube.com/watch?v=KG4ONHqF1qg&t=360s)
- His **post-UI world** has three levels. Level one is chat beside the existing UI, as in PostHog's sidebar, Cursor and Comet; he says it is already here and makes you use the GUI less and less. (The Weird Death Of User Interfaces at 6:45, https://www.youtube.com/watch?v=KG4ONHqF1qg&t=405s; The Weird Death Of User Interfaces at 8:15, https://www.youtube.com/watch?v=KG4ONHqF1qg&t=495s)
- Level two merges natural language into the existing UI, which stays on screen and gives live feedback, as in Todoist's experimental Ramble button. He thinks mainstream adoption starts here. (The Weird Death Of User Interfaces at 9:45, https://www.youtube.com/watch?v=KG4ONHqF1qg&t=585s)
- Level three is UI generated on the fly for each request, such as a flight search built for your exact query. "We will not be building interfaces anymore. We'll be building interface generators." (The Weird Death Of User Interfaces at 12:00, https://www.youtube.com/watch?v=KG4ONHqF1qg&t=720s)
- He says designers will not disappear but will go from drawing rectangles to writing rules, teaching AI when to show a calendar or a list, a map or a form; flows of pixel-perfect screens are gone. (The Weird Death Of User Interfaces at 12:45, https://www.youtube.com/watch?v=KG4ONHqF1qg&t=765s)
- He concedes generated UI might take a while and lists wrong output, consistency and muscle memory as open problems, then compares the worry to 1984 fears about finding saved files without typing a path. (The Weird Death Of User Interfaces at 13:30, https://www.youtube.com/watch?v=KG4ONHqF1qg&t=810s)
- By June 2026 his position was more measured: task complexity is a normal distribution, not a gradient, and most everyday tasks such as booking a flight or writing a presentation sit in the middle, between setting a timer and analysing huge databases. (The Weird Future Of User Interfaces at 9:00, https://www.youtube.com/watch?v=f32W5BEzWN0&t=540s)
- He calls **generative UI** the missing link for that middle: a machine decides a graphical interface is the best response and builds it on the spot, like Claude producing an interactive visualization of relativity in place of a wall of text. (The Weird Future Of User Interfaces at 9:45, https://www.youtube.com/watch?v=f32W5BEzWN0&t=585s)
- He says normal flight search works until the request gets unusual: a date picker beats typing, but Google Flights' AI mode could not show his multi-city trip with unsure dates because a prompt box sat on top of a fixed grid. (The Weird Future Of User Interfaces at 10:30, https://www.youtube.com/watch?v=f32W5BEzWN0&t=630s)
- He says generative UI has three levels. Level one is an interactive UI built on the spot inside a chat and styled in the product's brand, as in Claude; level two is a regular app where some pieces adapt, like a date slider with price colours that no designer at Google directly designed. (The Weird Future Of User Interfaces at 10:30, https://www.youtube.com/watch?v=f32W5BEzWN0&t=630s)
- He says builders will then design a kit of Lego-like blocks in the product's visual language plus rules such as when to show a map or a list. "The rules that tell the product what to show are the product." (The Weird Future Of User Interfaces at 12:45, https://www.youtube.com/watch?v=f32W5BEzWN0&t=765s)
- His 2026 conclusion is that the future is all three together: traditional UI where direct manipulation is best, agents for very complex jobs, generative UI for the rest. He calls it the biggest shift most products will go through in the coming years. (The Weird Future Of User Interfaces at 13:30, https://www.youtube.com/watch?v=f32W5BEzWN0&t=810s)
- In June 2026 he said Figma, now putting AI into its existing canvas, has to invent a new pattern that will become obvious, which he calls one of the hardest things in tech. He thinks Figma's edge there is its strong canvas, where a wrong result is quick to fix, and not the model. (The Ridiculous Engineering Of Figma at 19:30, https://www.youtube.com/watch?v=5t8dh3DSdBk&t=1170s; The Ridiculous Engineering Of Figma at 27:45, https://www.youtube.com/watch?v=5t8dh3DSdBk&t=1665s)

(The Man Who Made Apple Possible In 1968 at 4:30, https://www.youtube.com/watch?v=BXSHJXHeaqM&t=270s)
