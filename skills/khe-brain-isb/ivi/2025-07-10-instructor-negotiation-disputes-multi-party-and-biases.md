# Dispute resolution, multi-party negotiation and decision biases

The closing part of the sixth and last session of the negotiation course, taught by the course professor, who is not named in this recording. She debriefs a dispute resolution exercise between a supplier and a buyer, then moves quickly through threats, low power, multi-party negotiation, emotions and the influence principles, playing short clips of classic psychology experiments. The recorder's own class notes in the same file fill some gaps in the transcript.

## What was said

### Judging the other side

- The instructor describes a study in which people who were told they had time stopped to help a person, while people in the other condition did not.
- Her point from it is the fundamental attribution error: behaviour comes from personality and situation together, so the other side are not bad people because of one action.
- She says holding this view is one way to manage your own emotions in a dispute.

### How a dispute differs

- She says target prices, aggressive first offers and best alternatives belong to deal making. In a dispute nobody gets everything they want.
- She says to think about your worst alternative if the dispute is not resolved, not your best one.
- She says both sides lose without a resolution, and that a party with nothing to lose can hurt you badly.
- She says who holds more power in a dispute is a matter of perception, and both sides may believe they hold it.

### Interests, rights and power

- She names three approaches to a dispute: interests, rights and power.
- She says to focus on interests even when you have the contract or the power on your side.
- Her reason is that the other side may not see your power the way you do, so power should be used only when you are certain they also know you have it.
- She says rights do not vanish when you talk about interests. The contract sits in the background and both sides know it.
- She says research shows that parties who opened with an interest-based statement or an apology reached better resolutions.
- She says an apology and a request for help can disarm a counterpart whose whole plan was to use anger.
- She says that if you do mention your rights or power, you should add an interest-based proposal immediately, because people listen when you bring a solution.
- She says blaming and arguing over the past wastes time, and a dispute resolution has to be future focused.
- She says to stay a role model when the other side uses threats or anger.
- She says people who are threatened make very bad decisions.
- She says that after threats have been made it is hard to step back, and changing the person at the table is one way to defuse it.

### So-called irrational people

- She says the first step with a seemingly irrational party is to assume they have different information, and to ask questions to find the basis of their position.
- She reminds the class of the ultimatum game from the first session, where they themselves decided on emotion rather than on the numbers.
- She says people who look irrational are usually acting on high emotion or misinformation.
- She recommends Negotiation Genius by Deepak Malhotra and Max Bazerman as the one negotiation book to read.

### Using threats

- She says a threat must be one you are willing to execute and must be aimed at something that hurts the other side's interest.
- She says a threat should state what you will do, when and how. Without that it reads as empty.
- Her example is a leader who told air traffic controllers to report for work within 48 hours or lose their jobs, and then carried it out.
- She adds that a reputation for having carried out threats before makes a new threat credible.
- The class notes add two more points from her slide: leave the other side a way to save face, and expect reactance, revenge and escalation.

### Likability

- She says people do things for those they like, and that being likable takes little: be trustworthy, be polite and listen.
- She tells founders to make a good impression on every investor they meet, even one who is not investing.

### Negotiating from low power

- Her first recommendation for a low-power party is to gain more power before negotiating.
- She says a low-power party can control the process, and the class notes list the levers as multiple offers, the agenda, voting and decision rules.
- She says coalitions are another source of power. "I just need to be friends with somebody who is powerful."
- She says a party with no benchmark for its price has to argue from the value it brings and why it is better than the alternatives.

### Multi-party negotiation

- She says a negotiation with many parties needs decision rules agreed first, and the group has to stick to them.
- She says there are two levels: one-to-one talks before the meeting to build coalitions, and then the negotiation at the table.
- She advises the lead negotiator not to open the meeting, and to let a party who is on good terms with everyone speak first.
- She says unanimous voting is generally better but makes a deal harder, since every party has equal power.
- She says majority voting leaves the minority feeling left out, which is a problem when they still have to work on the project. For a one-time negotiation majority voting is acceptable.
- She says public voting lets everyone see who said yes and no, and that this changes how people vote.

### Emotions

- She says everyone feels emotional in a negotiation, and that asking for a moment or a break is fine.
- She says someone who gets nervous thinking on the spot should prepare more and think through as many scenarios as possible.
- She says you can answer an unexpected question by saying you have not thought about it and will come back.
- She says to learn your own triggers and plan how to avoid or handle them. Her own method for a difficult conversation is to have it while walking.

### Preparation

- She says preparation alone gives an edge, because many parties do not prepare.
- She says numbers will be there and everyone does that homework. How you deal with people is what matters.

### Influence principles

- She describes the obedience experiment in which ordinary people kept giving electric shocks because the experimenter told them to.
- Her use of it in negotiation is to look authoritative.
- She recommends the book Influence by Robert Cialdini, and names commitment and consistency and reciprocity among its principles.
- On reciprocity she says to label a concession so the other side notices it, and to do so subtly.
- She says to keep in mind favours you can do that cost you little.
- She says people attach more value to things presented as scarce.
- She says people like those who are similar to them, such as someone from the same school.

### Confirmation bias

- She runs the 2, 4, 6 exercise, where the class guesses the rule behind a number sequence.
- She says the real rule, any three numbers in ascending order, is so simple that people do not think of it.
- She says the Challenger launch decision is a case of confirmation bias: the data was there, but the decision makers looked only at the data that confirmed what they already believed.
- She uses a survey in which managers credited pet ownership for their management skills as an example of reading causation into a correlation.

### Social proof

- She plays a clip of the Asch line experiment, in which subjects went along with a wrong group answer on 37 percent of the critical trials.
- The clip reports that with one partner giving the right answer, yielding fell to 5 percent of the critical trials.
- She also plays a clip of people in a lift turning to face the back wall because everyone else does.
- She says that when people lack data they look for credibility cues.
- Her use of social proof in negotiation is to support an offer with outside evidence, such as a research study or a recommendation.
- Her everyday examples are laugh tracks in comedy shows and bestseller labels on Amazon.
- She says social proof matters a great deal in multi-party negotiations with public voting.
- Her closing rule is to accept an offer only when your interests are met.

## Examples and cases used

- A study of who stops to help a stranger: used to explain the fundamental attribution error.
- An ultimatum to air traffic controllers: used to show a threat that was exact and then executed.
- The Asch line experiment and a lift conformity clip: used to show social proof.
- The Challenger launch decision: used to show confirmation bias.
- Influence by Robert Cialdini and Negotiation Genius by Deepak Malhotra and Max Bazerman: recommended reading.

## Numbers given

- 37 percent: share of critical trials on which Asch's subjects went along with the group, from the clip she plays.
- 5 percent: the same measure when the subject had one partner giving the right answer.
- 48 hours: the deadline in her air traffic controller example.
