# New product development, marketing research and defining a market

What appears to be the first class of the marketing course, taught by a marketing professor who is not named in the recording. He says he earlier worked at Johnson and Johnson's surgical products business and has co-founded startups. It is a long class with one break. It moves from the new product development funnel, through how much testing a product needs, to the customer centric marketing process, growth hacking, customer needs and the definition of a market. He teaches through stories and takes many questions.

## What was said

### The new product funnel

- The instructor says every new product process starts at a "fuzzy front end" of idea generation or opportunity identification, wherever the idea comes from, and then narrows like a funnel.
- He names the stages as idea generation, concept development and testing, pre-test or test marketing, and launch followed by product management.
- He says most of the development work happens at the concept development and testing stage.
- A company's aim at that stage is to eliminate concepts with low potential and to learn how to revise the ones it takes forward, because taking every idea to the end would bankrupt it.
- He says companies moved from test marketing to in-house pre-test market systems so that they could get realistic market feedback without exposing the product to the market.
- He counts anything done to get information, analyse it and use it as marketing research, including asking friends for feedback, and says it informs every stage of the funnel.

### What to test: market newness and firm newness

- He places a product on two axes: how new it is to the market and how new it is to the firm.
- When both are low, as with a shampoo maker adding a shampoo, he says the main issue is cannibalisation of the firm's own products, so the concept test asks what people use now and how likely they are to buy.
- When the product is new to the firm, the issue becomes product and company fit, so the test asks whether people find it credible that this firm offers it.
- When market newness is high, the issue is whether people will adopt it at all.
- When both are high, his example being a home robot, he says it is very risky and suits someone with very deep pockets more than a founder with limited resources.
- He refuses to give a formula for each box, saying a formula everyone has stops working.
- He says a startup whose product needs serious market development, meaning educating customers about something they have never seen, should avoid being first unless it has a lot of funds, because a large company will see the opportunity and overrun it.
- His line on this is "First successful mover advantage is there".

### How fast to go: development risk and opportunity cost

- He defines development risk as the company's own assessment of how badly it will be hurt if the product goes to market and people react poorly.
- He describes opportunity cost as the potential loss of a fast moving window of opportunity.
- High development risk with low opportunity cost, his case being patented surgical products, means testing over and over and getting it right before launch.
- Low development risk with high opportunity cost, his case being an online community for doctors where network effects reward the first mover, means talking to a few doctors, launching an off the shelf product with basic functions in a week, and letting users vote on features.
- When both are high, his case being electric cars, the product has to be tested very thoroughly and very quickly, which takes a lot of resources.
- He says an MVP makes sense only when development risk is low.
- He says investors and commentators talk only about MVPs because they grew up working with products where development risk is almost always low.
- He says growth hacking can typically be done wherever an MVP makes sense.

### Choosing which battle to fight

- He says that across the hundreds of startups that have approached him in India, the most common problem is a poor targeting decision, which he describes as choosing which battle to fight.
- He says people in India are quick to enter a business and repent at leisure, and he advises taking as long as needed to prepare before entering.
- For his own first startup, he says he and two partners spent about a year and a quarter of weekend discussions deciding which business to enter.
- He says a founder cannot win every battle however good they are, and that deciding when to cut losses is very important.
- He says a platform business is a fight to the finish, because network effects leave room for only one or two players, so whoever enters is signing up for a war against players who can afford to lose money for years.
- He says founders have "a right to failure", meaning that doing everything correctly still leaves failure the most likely outcome.
- He says larger companies now hold a far greater advantage than they did, and that getting rich was far easier 30 years ago.
- He says disruption in Clayton Christensen's sense is the opposite of the popular idea. Big companies' products leave niches unserved because of their complexity, and a simpler version comes in and wins those niches.

### Doing marketing research on a budget

- He says marketing research exists to inform decisions, and if the decision will not change there is no point doing any.
- He says research is always done within a cost and time limit and is never perfect. A founder who knows the domain deeply can get by with far less, because their own insight supplements it.
- He says doing a survey does not make a product succeed.
- He says research never gives the answer. It gives information on which the founder still has to use judgement.
- He prefers a little high quality information to a lot of garbage.
- He says R&D and marketing should work closely together for the best chance of new product success.

### The customer centric marketing process

- He tells founders to strip away jargon such as needs and wants and think of a person with a problem that the company wants to solve, whether or not that person knows or can state the problem.
- He says customers differ in what they want, and that this heterogeneity is the timeless concept. Segmentation is only the most common way of handling it.
- One to one marketing satisfies everyone but is very costly, and mass marketing is cheap but leaves many people less satisfied. He calls segmentation the sweet spot between them.
- He defines mass customisation as a one to one solution at the cost of mass marketing, and cites Apple, where a few devices in an ecosystem let each user tailor their own solution.
- He distinguishes it from personalisation, which is segmenting more and more finely while still offering one solution within each segment.
- After targeting, he says marketing runs separately in each segment: study how competing solutions are positioned in customers' minds, then plan the position you want so that they choose you.
- He says the elements of the marketing mix are then used to design a strategy that creates a perception of superior value and leads to the planned position.
- He says the 4Ps are only one way of categorising whatever a company can do, and founders can categorise differently if that is more meaningful in their context.
- The elements must stay in sync. He says not to cut price just because the product is not selling, and to change an element only in a way that does not damage the planned position.
- He says the fuzzy front end of product development coincides with strategic marketing.
- He describes a solution as three layers: the core product, which is the need being met, the tangible product people can buy, and the augmented product. He says to always start with the core.
- He says "Good marketing is always ethical".

### Positioning as a mind game

- He says positioning happens in the customer's mind, and that what people call reality is a social agreement.
- His demonstration is a glass: everyone calls its mouth a circle although from almost every viewpoint they see an ellipse.
- He says this trained way of seeing becomes a hindrance when founders need to think differently, and that someone innocent and compassionate makes a better customer centric marketer.

### Growth hacking as experimentation

- He defines growth hacking as rapid experimentation for growth: experiment without waiting, and adopt whatever succeeds.
- He says experimenting for growth is age-old, and that fast food chains have long tested special items and moved those that meet a threshold onto the main menu.
- He traces the modern term to Microsoft in about 2011 or 2012, when an engineer spent two days coding an idea that would otherwise have waited months, and it became a large success.
- He sorts marketing research into three types: exploratory, descriptive and causal.
- Exploratory research explores an issue through reports, interviews and focus groups and relies on judgement.
- Descriptive research uses a proper statistical sample and a survey to describe a population. He counts survey based concept testing as descriptive.
- Causal research asks whether one thing causes another and is mostly done through experiments.
- He says causal research used to be expensive and difficult, and digital technology has made it cheap and easy.
- In his framing, causal research done inside the funnel before launch is ordinary marketing research, and the same thing done after a version has launched is what gets called growth hacking.
- He says founders should always experiment with communication. In his own startups radio advertising won in one case and influencers in another.
- For elements other than communication, he says to experiment only where development risk does not become a problem.

### Customer needs, existing and latent

- He says the single most important question is who uses the product and why.
- He says a solution is never about one dimension, and the product itself may not be the most important part.
- He says that in any product or solution the core need, the basic need, must be satisfied.
- He calls a need existing when the customer is willing and able to answer if asked directly, and latent when they are not.
- His latent examples are a five year old asked about higher education, who is unable to answer, and a patient with a condition they are embarrassed about, who is aware but unwilling.
- Whether a need is existing or latent is the company's judgement, and it decides whether asking directly is the right research method.
- To sharpen that judgement he tells founders to go and spend time with customers, including people on the ground at a B2B client, and says there is no substitute.
- He says survey answers shift with wording, so no survey figure means much alone. The same question should be repeated over time and benchmarked.
- He says people will answer a question even when they do not know the answer, so the asker has to judge whether the respondent is able to answer.

### Defining the market

- He defines a market as all potential customers who share a need, narrowed to those willing and able to enter an exchange to satisfy it.
- Under this definition the same product can sit in different markets and different products in the same market.
- He says a market can exist without any supplier.
- He says Blue Ocean Strategy rests on this need based definition, and that founders who think from the product and not the need will not make breakthrough innovations.
- He says deciding what market you are in is the same question as what business you are in and what problem you are solving, and nobody else can answer it for you.
- He calls market definition and segmentation the founder's battle plan.
- He warns B2B founders not to let a customer steer the product unless tailoring is the value proposition. Customers know a startup is desperate for their business and will keep it building for them at minimal margins, and it will never scale.

## Examples and cases used

- Johnson and Johnson surgical products: he says he launched a new surgical cutting instrument in India for the company. It was recommended for 30 surgeries. US surgeons stopped at 30, while Indian surgeons told him they had used one for 120. He says the company withdrew the product about a year later.
- The same case, his lesson: multinationals of that period brought US and European strategies to India unchanged and did not analyse the local context.
- Perplexity: he says it had a window because large players carry reputational risk and move slowly, citing Google's delay with Gemini, and it grew before anyone could overrun it.
- Insects as protein: his example of very high market newness, a cheap protein source that people will not adopt.
- Children's toothbrushes: he says large companies first made small, cute brushes and that this was what failed. The brushes on shelves now have big handles, and he says the same principle applies to patients with arthritis.
- Milkshakes at a US chain: a consultant sat in outlets to learn who bought them and why. Morning commuters wanted something quick to drink while driving that would last until a late lunch, so the drink was made bigger with fruit pieces. Children in the afternoon found it too thick, so theirs was made thinner.
- A beggar at a railway platform: he reads him as a business that solves the giver's need to feel better about themselves, targets three segments, and arrived at his look and position through trial and error.
- The Clinton survey: two groups got the same question with one saying Bill Clinton and the other President Clinton, and he says the disapproval ratings differed by 15 to 20%.
- Asking a street vendor the way to Honolulu: his illustration that people answer what they cannot know.
- Mineral water: the same product in a travellers' market and an office market, with a very different strategy in each.
- Tourist trips to the moon: a market of people willing and able to pay, with no supplier.
- TurboTax: he says about 40 tax software products together held under 10% of filers, and most people used a calculator, paper and pencil. TurboTax went after those people with software that led them through step by step, priced low.
- Airbnb: start from the traveller's problem with hotels, which he lists as expensive and intrusive, and not from the product.
- Politics in India: the need is good governance, the exchange is the vote, so the market is voters aged 18 and over.
- Starbucks: the class's answer for what business it is in is experience, and he says a shop that defines itself as selling coffee sees different competition.
- Credit card: the core product is fast, secure access to credit, the tangible product is the card, and the augmented product is the extras.
- Government tenders: the product is fixed by the tender, so vendors differentiate on the other elements of the mix.

## Numbers given

- TurboTax, his account: about 40 competing tax software products with a combined share under 10%.
- Clinton wording experiment, his account: a 15 to 20% difference in disapproval from one word.
- Surgical instrument, his account: recommended for 30 surgeries, used for 120 by a surgeon in India.
- His first startup: about a year and a quarter spent choosing which business to enter.

## Not covered

- Segmentation and targeting methods. He says he will explain the targeting decision systematically in a later session.
- Pricing, distribution and promotion in any detail. The 4Ps appear only as a framework.
- Product adoption and consumer psychology. He mentions both and deliberately holds back.
- Conjoint analysis and the pre-test market systems, which he names without explaining.
