# Operations, value indicators and process thinking

An operations professor gave the first session of an eight-session operations course for the cohort. He spent the whole session on basics: what operations is, three routes to market leadership, the value indicator, and process thinking. He ran a two-round drawing exercise to show what a process does, and assigned two articles, one on digital myopia and one on the three routes.

## What was said

### What operations is

- The instructor says operations is designing a process, executing it to reach the market, and continually improving it so the market stays with the company.
- He says it is a myth that operations means production. Anything that helps a company execute its strategy is operations.
- He separates strategy from execution with examples. Growing a hospital from 200 to 500 beds is strategy, and deciding where to put the new facility is an execution decision.
- His marketing example is that raising sales around a sporting event is strategy, while how often to post, which channels to use and how else to reach the same customer are operations.
- His hiring example is that adding four people is the goal, while how to shortlist from 100 CVs, who interviews and what rubric is used are execution decisions.
- He says a standard operating procedure does not fit every case, because some work, such as a marketing campaign, is a unique project.

### Value and stakeholders

- He defines value as a material or non-material offering through which a company satisfies a stakeholder.
- He names customers, employees and shareholders as the three primary stakeholders, and says what satisfies one may not satisfy another.
- He says a founder cannot satisfy stakeholders without operations.

### Market leadership inside a segment

- He says every organisation should aim for market leadership from day one, and that leadership is judged within a market segment, not across the whole market.
- He says market leadership is not a volume game alone.
- He says starting a firm is the starting point and not the achievement, and compares it to treating a proposal as the end of the story.
- He names three routes to market leadership: product leadership, customer intimacy and operational excellence.

### Route one: product leadership

- He says the first route is product leadership, and innovation is what enables it.
- He gives Apple as the example, for design, experience and continuous innovation that makes customers feel each release is new.

### Route two: customer intimacy

- He defines customer intimacy as customising and personalising for the customer, so the customer feels the thing was made for them.
- He contrasts a mass car showroom, which shows a menu of models, variants and colours already built, with a luxury brand that asks what features the buyer wants and builds the car for them over months.
- He says choosing from a menu of ready products is not customer intimacy.
- He says Dell entered a market held by giants such as HP by letting buyers configure their own computer, and that without customer intimacy Dell had no reason to offer HP's customers.
- He says customer intimacy cost Dell the buyers who wanted to touch the product or have it immediately, and when Dell later sold through retail stores its selling point in that channel was no longer customer intimacy.
- He says customisation is a way for an entrant in a competitive market to give customers an extra reason to try it over the incumbents.
- He says Subway is customer intimacy through co-creation, because the customer decides what goes in from a limited set of options.
- He says a handwritten note from a restaurant with a delivery is a form of customer intimacy, and using the customer's name would make it more personal.
- He says companies launching in a digital era have access to a lot of customer data, which makes customer intimacy a route most of them can take.
- He asks why quick commerce apps show every user the same nudge, and suggests varying the nudge by customer persona.
- He says customer intimacy involves trade-offs in cost, time, people and technology.
- He says a unique selling point can become saturated on any of the three routes.

### Route three: operational excellence and the value indicator

- He says the third route is operational excellence, which means mastering one value indicator.
- He defines the value indicator as the value customers perceive in the product, meaning what comes to mind when they hear the company's name.
- His examples are oral health for Colgate, speed for Domino's, being on time for IndiGo and affordability for Maruti Suzuki.
- He says a company that thinks it sells a product such as toothpaste is selling a commodity. Once it asks how else it can deliver the value indicator, innovation follows.
- He says a founder needs "crystal clear clarity" on how customers will identify the company in the market.
- He says mastering a value indicator means delivering it every time. A promise met only sometimes is not mastery.
- He says when a competitor in the same segment offers the same value indicator, the recognition a company gets from it falls. His example is Hyundai competing with Maruti Suzuki on low-priced cars.
- He says a competitor mastering a different value indicator is not taking the company's identity.
- He says a value indicator must be unique within a market segment. Two companies can hold the same one in different segments, and a company that finds its indicator taken in its segment should look for another segment.
- He gives three questions to find a value indicator, answered in order: what is my market segment, who are my competitors in that segment, and why should a customer come to me and not to them.
- He says an outcome such as job placement for a business school is not a value indicator, and some attributes are only hygiene.

### How large incumbents respond

- Asked how a giant responds to an entrant, he says large companies often let the entrant prove its selling point and then acquire it, because that is a quick win. He cites Facebook's many acquisitions.
- He says some large companies are slow to notice change because of organisational arrogance, and gives Nokia's slow response to Android.
- He says a sudden culture change after an acquisition leads to attrition and to practices nobody in the company likes.

### The drawing exercise and what a process does

- In round one the class had 60 seconds to draw a smiling fish facing right. The drawings all differed.
- In round two the customer and requirements were the same, but the class followed lettered step-by-step instructions on a grid. The drawings came out almost alike.
- He says inconsistent output confuses the customer, who cannot tell what to expect on the next visit.
- He says as a company grows the founder no longer does everything, so differences between people enter the product, and the customer does not care who made it.
- He says the voice of the customer was identical in both rounds, and the process made the difference, so the process matters as much as the voice of the customer.
- He says the simplest definition of operations management is everything about the process: its design, improvement, control and analysis.
- He defines a process as having inputs, a sequence of activities and an output.
- He says unique cases still follow a process. His examples are a call centre call, a doctor's diagnosis, issuing a credit card and matchmaking on a platform.
- He says a standard operating procedure is a way of communicating a process to a person, and an algorithm is the same thing communicated to a computer.
- He says customisation also runs on a process, as in the fixed order of choices at Subway.

### Process thinking and silos

- He says the boxes on the exercise grid stand for departments, and the finished fish shows that value is created across departments, not inside one.
- He says customers see one organisation. How it divides its departments is its internal problem.
- He says process design, improvement and control are everybody's responsibility.
- He calls job titles and departments a corporate myth that exists only to organise and govern the firm.
- He says founders must sow this culture from the start. If it is missing when the company becomes large, the result is disruption and internal politics.
- He says a company can organise by product when each product could stand alone, and gives Google as the example.

### Digital comes after process

- He says the idea comes first and digital is the means of realising it. A platform founder should first be clear that the business is matching supply with demand.
- He says technology has to be fitted onto a process, and a company should not think about digitalisation until it excels at its processes.
- He says organisations like easy technology that promises high impact, and gives robotic process automation sold as a way to replace staff as an example.
- He says 70% of organisations fail at digital transformation, and he puts the cause as a lack of process thinking.
- He describes process thinking as humility and says "Jumping the gun is not scientific".
- He calls fixing on a technology before the process digital myopia, the subject of the first assigned article.
- He says a leader should think about processes in light of people first.

## Examples and cases used

- Maruti Suzuki: customers associate it with affordability, and it has held its lead in its segment as many competitors arrived.
- White Castle and McDonald's: White Castle brought the hamburger to market and did not spread across the United States, while McDonald's copied the product with variations and scaled worldwide through operations.
- Apple: product leadership through design, experience and continuous innovation.
- Dell versus HP: customer intimacy as the entry route against a giant, with the trade-off of losing buyers who wanted to see the product first.
- Subway: the customer co-creates the product within a fixed sequence of choices.
- Marriott: every interaction with a guest is recorded, and the data is used to choose a welcome drink likely to impress that guest at the first touch point.
- Apollo 24x7: an experiment he took part in gave each customer a coupon customised on about 60 parameters beyond loyalty, and cross-selling of other products rose by 20%.
- Domino's: its 30-minute promise is built from a timed process, with order to oven in 4 minutes, baking in 6, cutting, packing and clean-up in 5, and delivery in 8, for 23 minutes and a 7-minute buffer.
- Colgate: defining itself by oral health instead of toothpaste opens other products that deliver the same value.
- Nokia and Android: a large company slow to respond to a shift.
- Google: products that can stand alone, so the company can be organised by product.

## Numbers given

- 23 minutes: the total process time he quotes for Domino's against a 30-minute promise, leaving 7 minutes spare.
- 20%: the rise in cross-selling he reports from the customised coupon experiment with Apollo 24x7.
- 60: the parameters beyond loyalty he says the coupon algorithm used.
- 70%: the share of organisations he says fail at digital transformation.

## Not covered

- Process design, improvement, control and analysis in detail. He lists them as the agenda for later sessions.
- When to use technology and when not to, which he promises for a later session.
- How to balance customer intimacy with scale. He raises the trade-offs and defers them.
- The class exercise on finding each startup's value indicator, which he moved to the next session.
