# Six Sigma and sigma capability

An operations management class in a multi-session series on lean and quality. The instructor, who is not named in the recording, builds the idea of sigma capability from a restaurant wait-time example, then covers defects per million opportunities, the DMAIC and DMADV roadmaps and Lean Six Sigma. He sets a delivery-time case as homework. The session ran as a lecture with many questions from the class.

## What was said

### Specification limits and the voice of the customer

- The instructor says specification limits are what the customer tells you counts as good performance. The lower specification limit is LSL and the upper specification limit is USL, and together they are the voice of the customer.
- He says that in any industry the specification limits are given by the customer.
- His restaurant example: a diner is unhappy if biryani takes more than 10 minutes, so 10 minutes is the upper specification limit. If the dish came in 30 seconds she would doubt it was made for her, so a lower limit also exists and the specification becomes two sided.
- He says a specification limit belongs to the market segment you have chosen, not to one customer.
- He says a metric where lower is better has only a USL and no LSL. A metric where higher is better has no USL.
- His sequence of thinking is: capture the voice of the customer, convert it into KPIs, prioritise the KPIs with the Kano model, then ask for each KPI whether higher or lower is better, then find the limit.
- To find the limit he keeps asking customers until he reaches the point where most of the segment says no. He then sets his internal limit tighter than that point to be safe. His example is customers who become unhappy beyond 20 minutes and an internal USL of 10.
- He says customers cannot always be asked directly, because some needs are hidden and have to be identified.

### What sigma means

- The instructor defines the sigma scale as the number of standard deviations you can fit between your mean and the specification limit on each side of the performance curve.
- His method: calculate the mean, calculate the standard deviation, get the specification limits from the customer, then step away from the mean one standard deviation at a time until you hit the limit, and count the steps.
- He says sigma means the health or capability of a process, and the capability in question is the capability to meet the customer's expectation. He writes process capability as Z.
- He says the higher the sigma value, the more capable the process is of meeting customer expectation.
- In his worked figure, two processes have the same mean and the same specification limits. Cutting the standard deviation from 2 to 1 moves the process from 3 sigma to 6 sigma.
- He says lowering the standard deviation raises the sigma value, so less variation means a healthier process.
- He says sigma compares your current performance with the customer's expectation. It is the deviation of your performance around its mean, read against what the customer expects.
- He says this way of measuring sigma is for continuous data such as weight and time, because standard deviation only applies to continuous data.

### Six sigma is a reference point, not a rule

- The instructor calls six an arbitrary reference point. "Who said stop at six? I said don't stop at six."
- He says what counts as a good sigma value depends on the context of the business and how critical the process is.
- His contrast: aircraft manufacturing may run a 12 sigma process and six sigma is not enough there, while a business selling mangoes can be fine at 3 or 4 sigma.
- He says improving a process costs money, so a business should not spend to raise sigma beyond what its context needs.
- He says a company that calls itself a Six Sigma company does not have every KPI at six sigma. It means the company follows the cycle of measuring process health and improving it.
- He says to measure the status quo before attempting any improvement.

### KPIs are measured one at a time

- Asked whether looking at KPIs in isolation is a problem, the instructor says each KPI is treated as a separate improvement opportunity, and you connect back to the value indicators of the business to decide which matters.
- His example is a restaurant with three processes such as procurement, cooking and billing, each with about five KPIs. He would calculate a sigma value for each of the 15 KPIs and would not take a cumulative average or minimum.
- He says an outcome like customer satisfaction comes from many causes such as taste and wait time, and each cause is attacked individually.
- He says a KPI should be defined from the customer's perspective. A customer feels delivery time from order to receipt, not the separate procurement, cooking and logistics times.
- He says when you cannot measure something directly, such as taste, you measure the customer's perception of it on a Likert scale.
- He says when data has an outlier, use the median and not the mean. He says not to delete the outlier, because every data point is a message about your performance.

### Discrete data and DPMO

- For discrete data the instructor uses a count: out of the opportunities you had to meet a customer expectation, how many times you met it is the yield, and how many times you missed is the defect count.
- He defines DPMO as defects per million opportunities. It is a theoretical value because you extrapolate from the opportunities you really had to one million.
- He gives a standard conversion table: about 690,000 defects per million is a one sigma process, roughly 300,000 is two sigma, and 3.4 defects per million is six sigma.
- He says a 3 sigma process is considered average, a process is considered good only after 4.5 sigma, and at 6 sigma you are getting a competitive advantage.
- He gives two reasons to use the sigma scale over a percentage scale. It is a more stringent scale, and it gives a common unit so that KPIs measured in different units can be compared.

### How much data is enough

- The instructor says the amount of data needed depends on how often the KPI is measured. With daily data he asks for 30 days where possible.
- His example: 200 data points a day over 30 days gives 6,000 data points, which he calls decently good data. A KPI measured once a quarter, such as audit time, needs many years.
- He says an early-stage business without data should run a test market, pilot or proof of concept before launch, and that this also helps convince investors.
- He says sample size for auditing depends on how much error you can accept and is worked out through hypothesis testing, which he did not teach in this class.

### DMAIC, DMADV and Lean Six Sigma

- The instructor says Six Sigma is also a methodology with two project roadmaps, DMAIC and DMADV, and that the real value of Six Sigma comes when it is used as a scale.
- In the roadmap you define the problem and measure it, then analyse the root causes and not the problem itself. His example is attrition that is called too much until it is measured at 40%.
- He says every data set has two characteristics to read separately: central tendency, which is average performance, and spread, which is consistency.
- His two-restaurant example: the first meets the customer expectation on average but is inconsistent, and the second is consistent but does not meet the expectation on average. Both need improvement, with different treatments.
- He says the inconsistent restaurant has a variation problem and gets a DMAIC project. The consistent restaurant that misses the expectation needs its whole process redesigned, which is a DMADV project.
- His dartboard picture: darts scattered around the bullseye mean the aim is right and the delivery is not, and darts tightly grouped away from the bullseye mean the execution is good and the aim is wrong.
- His order of questions is: first, on average am I meeting the customer expectation. If not, change the design. If yes, ask whether I meet it consistently.
- He says a process that meets the expectation on average is capable. The inconsistency then comes from causes such as demotivated or unskilled people or old equipment.
- He says lean brings rapid improvements because it is organised common sense, and that Lean Six Sigma is a popular method in the market.
- He says belt certifications are overrated. What matters is the knowledge and hands-on demonstrated skill a person carries.

## Examples and cases used

- Restaurant biryani wait time: used to build specification limits and the sigma scale.
- Aircraft manufacturing against a mango seller: the right sigma level depends on how critical the process is.
- A maternity ward: a second example of a setting where errors are not tolerable.
- Two restaurants and a dartboard: average performance and consistency are separate problems with separate treatments.
- A food delivery case with a 30 minute delivery promise: set as homework, with the mean, standard deviation and a histogram to be worked out in Excel.

## Numbers given

- 3.4 defects per million opportunities at six sigma, from the instructor's conversion table.
- About 690,000 defects per million at one sigma and roughly 300,000 at two sigma, from the same table.
- 3 sigma average, 4.5 sigma good, 6 sigma competitive advantage, as the instructor's reading of the scale.
- 12 sigma as what he says aircraft manufacturing may operate at.
- 30 days of daily data as his working minimum, with 200 points a day giving 6,000.
