# Bridge financing case and investor rights in a term sheet

The first half of a finance class, up to the break. The instructor, a woman who co-teaches the ivi finance course, ran a case discussion on whether a venture fund should put a 5 million dollar bridge round into a clean technology battery company whose management had lost focus. Student groups argued for and against, and the instructor then explained four investor rights that the case raised. The company name is garbled in the transcript. The second half of the same class is the note on tranching.

## What was said

### The case for the bridge round

- The instructor says the case for investing is that the good things about the company are unchanged, the bad things are being changed, and a new CEO will take it in a better direction.
- The instructor says the upside if the company succeeds is very large, and that is the big point in favour.
- The instructor says the best argument is to treat the 5 million as a new investment with large upside, in a company whose technology is largely validated.
- The instructor says the fund can cut the cord later if the company misses its milestones.
- The instructor says the real decision is what timeline and exit valuation can be reached with 5 million and a new CEO, leaving aside the money already spent.

### The case against

- The instructor says the venture fund's own board representative carries some of the blame, because the controls now being promised were not put in after Series A.
- The instructor says clean technology is capital heavy and needs several rounds, so follow-on investment is part of the business.
- The instructor says venture investing moves in waves of what is hot and what gets funded.
- The instructor says the fund in the case is not one that will finance the company all the way to exit, so other investors must come in later.
- The instructor says investors in this position ask two things: whether later funding will be available, and at what step-up in valuation it will happen.
- The instructor says capital-heavy companies dilute early investors more, because much more funding is needed relative to the pre-money value they hold.
- The instructor says a fund's duty is to its own investors and not to the sector.
- The instructor says she would vote no, mainly because the investment climate had changed, while agreeing with the case made for the company's direction.
- The instructor says there is no right or wrong answer and it comes down to judging the chance of a turnaround.

### What the company could have done differently

- The instructor says seeking commitments from customers earlier would have shown traction and would also have kept the team focused on meeting a need.
- The instructor says technology should not become an end in itself, and that is a slippery slope for a team excited by its technology.
- The instructor says the company lacked an audit committee and a CFO, and needed better governance.
- The instructor says some things are outside the founders' control, such as the investment climate, and the idea is to focus on what you can influence.
- The instructor says judging the company for being in the wrong sector carries hindsight bias, because the sector was in favour when it started.

### Investor rights explained

- The instructor says anti-dilution provisions exist because a later round can happen at a lower valuation, and they make the dilution in such a down round smaller for early investors than for founders.
- The instructor says a right of first refusal applies when a founder has an outside buyer for their own shares. The investors can buy that stake first by matching the same terms.
- The instructor says this right lets investors keep control and bring in new management, while the founder still gets the price offered.
- The instructor says the right covers founders selling their own shares and not a new funding round.
- The instructor says the right is common in term sheets but not often invoked, and it matters because founders in later rounds often sell some of their own shares.
- The instructor says a co-sale or tag-along right applies if the investors decline to buy. The outside buyer must then also buy from the investors in proportion, so they exit alongside the founder.
- The instructor says a drag-along right lets investors who have found a buyer force the founder to sell too, if certain conditions are met.
- The instructor says drag-along is usually exercised only in extreme cases of serious disagreement, and such provisions also get used to negotiate with a founder or keep them in line.
