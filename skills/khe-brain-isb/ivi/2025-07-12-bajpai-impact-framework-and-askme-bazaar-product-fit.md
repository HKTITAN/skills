# IMPACT growth framework and the Askme Bazaar product fit, Amardeep Bajpai

This is the morning of day two of Amardeep Bajpai's growth hacker bootcamp. He named his growth framework, IMPACT, described how a real-money rummy company he worked with ran on disciplined KPIs, and then began a case study of Askme Bazaar, an Indian e-commerce site whose growth he worked on. This part of the case covers finding the target audience and building the product for it. The session ran up to a break.

## What was said

### The IMPACT framework

- Bajpai names six parts of his growth framework: identify product-market fit, mobilise with digital and AI acquisition marketing, position through branding, analyse everything, continuously iterate, and transform through innovation.
- He says there are a thousand ways to fail and a thousand ways to succeed, so the framework is not a formula.
- He says the key to acquisition marketing is what consumes the target audience's time, and the forms of acquisition marketing change when the audience moves.
- He says most acquisition will be driven by artificial intelligence within the next 6 to 12 months, and that his own company has worked for six months on what he calls artificial intelligence engine optimisation.
- He says many digital companies stop spending on brand, and that brand spend should never be zero, though the split between brand and paid marketing depends on the case.
- He says every innovation becomes a commodity at some point, and "whenever you are fighting a battle of commodities, only the brand wins".
- He says to analyse data regularly and feed it into branding, execution and product decisions, not only when the company is in trouble.
- He says the last part means not being afraid to try something new in the growth strategy when you are convinced of it.

### Doing more with less: a disciplined gaming company

- Bajpai describes a real-money rummy company that competed with RummyCircle and says it raised very little money and never raised again after its first round.
- He says its founders were among the most disciplined people he has met: they worked Monday to Saturday and reviewed everything on Sundays.
- He says the company gave employees a large share of ownership, and many of them made significant money when it was sold.
- He says every employee got KPIs on joining, at least one of them personal in nature, and the company measured them and paid extra salary for hitting them.
- He says an employee who had beaten their KPIs could ask for an appraisal at any time without waiting for the cycle.
- He says an under-performer got three chances to improve, with the company working alongside them, for example by offering a course.

### Askme Bazaar: finding the audience

- Bajpai says Askme began as a search service and later launched Askme Bazaar to compete in e-commerce with Amazon and Flipkart, funded by Malaysian investor Ananda Krishnan through Astro.
- He says identifying product-market fit was the hardest part of the work.
- He says Flipkart and Amazon were chasing higher average order values and gross merchandise value, so their home pages showed laptops, desktops and microwaves aimed at well-off buyers in tier 1 cities.
- He says the reasoning behind that focus was that online shopping needed costly broadband or data, so the people online were the people who could afford high-value items.
- He says his team saw 4G arriving and phones getting cheaper, and chose the market the others neglected: young people aged 18 to 34 in tier 2 and tier 3 cities, plus some in tier 1, with limited spending power.
- He says the team surveyed about 1,000 people across India and did a psychographic analysis of the audience before designing the site.
- He says a founder can reach 1,000 people by standing outside malls over a weekend, and he strongly recommends founders go and talk to their target audience themselves.
- He says people cannot say exactly what they want, so the founder has to read the latent need from their answers.
- He says respondents kept saying they wanted something cheaper. The team concluded that cheap products already existed on the big sites and the real problem was discovering them.

### Askme Bazaar: building the product for that audience

- Bajpai says the first product change was "99 stores": stores for goods priced from 99 to 999 rupees, placed on the home page so that every visitor had a reason to shop.
- He says a home page full of expensive goods intimidates a first-time shopper with little money, who decides the site is not for them.
- He says home page space was divided by audience: most of it went to the young low-budget buyer, with a dedicated smaller space for high-value items.
- He says the site introduced endless browsing, a home page that keeps loading products as the user scrolls.
- He says the site, which had been divided into three parts, was combined into one bazaar where everything could be found, and search by discount percentage was added.
- He says cash on delivery was added because the audience lacked credit card limits, and that third-party vendors handle the cash for under 10 rupees an order.
- He says checkout was cut to a single step that asks only for contact number and address.
- He says the site was built mobile first, ahead of other sites, and rode the Jio rollout.
- He says the team expected that buyers who got used to shopping at 99 to 999 rupees would return for higher-value items, and that this was proven within six months.
- He says choosing an audience means making trade-offs in the product, and once the audience is identified the product has to be built for it.

### Analysing everything

- Bajpai says a 40-person analytics team measured conversion daily, and one person was responsible for conversion in each store, such as the 99 rupee store.
- He says heat maps of the site showed where people clicked, and A/B or multivariate tests then produced small product changes such as where a button should sit.
- He says the catalogue was split into category groups, each with a weekly target and a business head, whom the company called category managers.
- He says category managers could spot fast-moving items and build relationships with more suppliers, and that Amazon and Flipkart later adopted the same role.

### Testing the hypothesis at the Google Online Shopping Festival

- Bajpai says the team tested its audience hypothesis with one product, a sling bag modelled on a well-known sports brand's bag, priced at 299 rupees and pushed with Google AdWords.
- He says the team planned for about 1,500 units and received 25,000 to 30,000 orders in the three days of the festival.
- He says the result confirmed the hypothesis, since other sellers had featured high-value items, and it exposed a supply problem that led to the category manager structure.

## Examples and cases used

- Askme Bazaar: growth by serving young, low-budget buyers in smaller cities whom Amazon and Flipkart ignored.
- Amazon and Flipkart home pages of the time: full of high-value goods, which he says intimidated first-time buyers.
- A real-money rummy company competing with RummyCircle: his example of growing on little capital through KPI discipline and employee ownership.
- Cleartrip: he says its design process was similar to the survey-led process used for Askme Bazaar.
- Perplexity: his example of AI search starting to carry advertising models.

## Numbers given

- 6 to 12 months: Bajpai's forecast, made in July 2025, for when most acquisition would be driven by AI.
- 18 to 34: the age band of Askme Bazaar's target audience.
- About 1,000 people: the survey sample used to design Askme Bazaar.
- 99 to 999 rupees: the price range of the stores placed on the Askme Bazaar home page.
- Under 10 rupees an order: what he says vendors charge to manage cash on delivery.
- 40 people: the size of Askme Bazaar's analytics team.
- 299 rupees price and about 150 rupees cost in bulk: the sling bag used in the festival test.
- About 1,500 units planned against 25,000 to 30,000 orders in three days: the result of the festival test.
