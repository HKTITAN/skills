# The four questions investors ask of a startup, Varun Laul

Varun Laul, an ISB alumnus who works in private equity and founded a micro VC platform, spoke for about an hour on how investors evaluate a startup. He lectured for the first third on a four-question framework and the funding stages. Founders in the room then wrote one or two sentence answers to the four questions for their own ventures and some read them out. This note keeps only the general teaching.

## What was said

### The frame

- Varun Laul says that whatever framework an investor or textbook presents, investors are fundamentally trying to answer only four questions about a venture.
- The four are: how large is the market, does the founder fit the market, is the value proposition unique, and how will the business make money.
- He tells founders to use the four questions when designing the venture itself, not only when pitching for capital.
- He says a founder must be able to answer each of the four in one or two sentences.

### Question 1: how large can this become

- Varun Laul says an investor puts in capital for a return, a return comes only when the business scales, so the first thing they want to know is how large the business can become.
- He says many investments that go wrong fail on this first question, because the market was never properly estimated.
- He says founders, managers and investors still get the market question wrong at the growth stage, in businesses that have run for 10 to 15 years.
- His way of sizing a serviceable market is to slice it: how many people can afford the product, how many recognise they need it, how many you can reach, and what usage it creates.
- He says usage multiplied by price gives revenue, and working through that is when you see how different the serviceable market is from the headline addressable one.
- His illustration is a skin care brand, where treating everyone as the addressable market is the wrong way to think.
- Asked whether to size top down or bottom up, Varun Laul says it depends. Where industry data is missing you go bottom up, where you are creating a new category you go top down, and the short answer is to do both.
- He says a market defined as everyone who takes part in an activity is too loose and broad a definition, and compares it to a food company claiming that 7 billion people eat.
- He tells founders not to get caught up in definitions of TAM, SAM and SOM. Think logically about how many people your proposition addresses, how many of those you can serve, and how many of those will pay.
- He says the serviceable obtainable market changes each year, because distribution cannot be set up everywhere on day one.
- Varun Laul says sizing the market from your go-to-market plan is "the tail wagging the dog". First decide who and how many you are targeting, then build the go-to-market for that.
- He says the market number should reflect how large the business can truly aim to become, and that you cannot assume 100% market share.

### Question 2: founder-market fit

- Varun Laul says founder-market fit is the question of whether the founder or team has the knowledge, experience and skills for this particular business, plus initiative, drive and entrepreneurship.
- His example is a procurement platform for restaurants and hotels. The founder needs to know how to reach those businesses, who the buyer inside them is, how to build the technology, and how money is made and lost in procurement.
- He says it is sometimes more important to know where you lose money in a business before you start making money.
- He says investors are checking for two things in the team: functional skills and certain behavioural attributes.
- Varun Laul says founder-market fit is the variable most investors discount, and also the one that decides whether a venture succeeds.
- He says people gloss over a weak fit by assuming the founder will learn or hire the talent, but that fit is what really makes the difference.
- Asked how an investor judges soft skills, he says the investor first has to know what it takes to build that specific business, then ask whether this team has it. For a B2B software company that means prior experience selling to enterprises and someone who understands the technology.
- He puts a startup's probability of succeeding at 1 in 100, and says a founder has to be "borderline delusional" to try.

### Question 3: a unique value proposition

- Varun Laul says that outside areas such as healthcare where investors back creation or invention, a business almost never creates something new, because people have been eating, wearing clothes and buying software and raw materials for a long time.
- He says that for 99.9% of businesses the question is therefore whether the value proposition is unique or special, or a me-too.
- His example is Dollar Shave Club and Harry's against Gillette. He calls Gillette one of the strongest consumer incumbents, and says the product was the same but the subscription model was the unique proposition.
- He says shaving is a routine activity and people in some markets have no problem with subscriptions, which is why the model worked.
- Varun Laul says uniqueness is the hardest of the four questions to answer, and that thinking deeply enough about the problem you care about will produce an answer.
- He says a founder must be able to state the proposition itself, such as the specific format or offering, and what is special about it compared with the many others attempting the same thing.

### Question 4: monetisation

- Varun Laul says the fourth question is how you will charge, how you will make money and how much.
- He says the point to be careful about is the difference between a customer's ability to pay and their willingness to pay.
- His examples: a person who can afford a Mercedes is not necessarily willing to buy one, and an Indian bank can easily pay for new software but is not necessarily willing to, because of the friction of replacing what it already runs.
- He says B2C founders think a lot about whether consumers will pay, and B2B founders do not give willingness to pay enough thought.
- He says that when a product or service is not yet common, the founder has to address ability and willingness to pay directly.
- He asks the room whether anyone would invest in a venture that does not know what it will charge, and says the premise is to take capital and generate a return on it.
- He says investors ask what proof or validation sits behind a claimed price, and in some ventures the founder has to do primary work to get it.

### Evidence and execution

- Varun Laul says that at no stage will an investor accept "I think so" as the answer to one of the four questions. They want hard data, such as evidence from another market or bottom-up consumer behaviour.
- He says execution beats strategy 99 times out of 100, and that execution is founded on detail.
- He tells founders to bring as much quantification as they can into their thinking, even if it never goes on a slide.
- His tip for pitching is that the first answer to every question is "it depends", followed by the variables it depends on.

### Funding stages and who invests when

- Varun Laul says the idea stage, a few people with an idea, is funded by friends, family, angel investors or angel syndicates, who ask whether the founder is good and the problem large enough.
- He says that once there is an offering to build, a founder can approach micro VC platforms, and the question shifts to the value proposition and the business model.
- He says VC firms come in when there is an offering, some customers and early validation, and they look for customer validation and early signs of monetisation.
- At the following rounds, he says, investors ask whether product-market fit is turning into scale, monetisation and unit economics that show in a real P&L.
- He says this journey takes five to ten years, and the cases he has personally seen took five to six.
- He says private equity comes much later, when no one is asking whether there is a market or whether people will buy. The question is how to replicate a proven success with more customers.
- Varun Laul says private equity puts far more capital into a business than venture capital does and can tolerate far fewer failed investments, so it looks for more certainty.
- He says it is a fallacy that needing a lot of capital makes a business a private equity case. What matters is the maturity of the product. Building a new foundational AI model would cost a great deal and is still a venture bet.
- He says asset-heavy businesses are usually not what venture investors back, and that there are ways to restructure a business so the core proposition is asset-light.
- He distinguishes a micro VC from a later VC by cheque size and purpose: about $500,000 is what you need to develop an offering, and about $3 million is what you need to establish product-market fit.
- He tells founders not to worry about the names of rounds. Know how much capital you need and who gives capital at your stage.

## Examples and cases used

- Gillette, Dollar Shave Club and Harry's: a subscription model as a unique proposition against a very strong incumbent selling the same product.
- A skin care brand: the addressable market is not everyone with skin.
- A procurement platform for restaurants and hotels: the knowledge and skills that founder-market fit demands.
- Mercedes, and software sold to Indian banks: ability to pay is different from willingness to pay.
- A foundational AI model: capital intensity does not turn a venture bet into a private equity deal.
- A food company saying 7 billion people eat: a market definition too broad to use.

## Numbers given

- 1 in 100: Varun Laul's figure for a startup's probability of succeeding.
- 99.9%: his figure for the share of businesses that are not inventing something new and must show a unique proposition.
- 99 out of 100: how often he says execution beats strategy.
- Five to ten years: his estimate of the journey from idea through the venture rounds.
- About $500,000 and about $3 million: his cheque sizes for developing an offering and for establishing product-market fit.
