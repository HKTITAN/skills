# Money, legal and runway

This file answers how SAFEs and priced rounds work, what legal setup a company needs, how to manage the company's money, and what to do when runway is short or the market turns. The people speaking are Kirsty Nathoo, Carolynn Levy, Jon Levy, Jason Kwon, Paul Graham, Dalton Caldwell and Michael Seibel. Read every figure with the year attached to it, read the legal lines as US and Delaware practice, and note that two of the transcripts label their speakers unreliably, so a few lines name the conversation instead of one person.

## How does a SAFE work?

- Nathoo, in a talk the library dates to 2018, describes a SAFE as an instrument where the investor gives the company money now and the company promises shares at a later date, when it raises a priced round. (Understanding SAFEs and priced equity rounds, https://www.ycombinator.com/library/6m-understanding-safes-and-priced-equity-rounds)
- Nathoo, in a talk the library dates to 2018, says a SAFE involves minimal negotiation, with two points to settle, the amount invested and the valuation cap, while a priced round has many more terms and is harder to close. (Understanding SAFEs and priced equity rounds, https://www.ycombinator.com/library/6m-understanding-safes-and-priced-equity-rounds)
- Carolynn Levy, in a talk the library dates to 2019, says valuation is the one term a founder has to negotiate in a SAFE, and that a version with no valuation in it also exists. (Modern startup funding, https://www.ycombinator.com/library/8F-modern-startup-funding)
- Nathoo, in a talk the library dates to 2018, says a SAFE is not debt: convertible debt generally carries an interest rate and a maturity date when it must be repaid, and a SAFE has neither, though the two convert in similar ways in a priced round. (Understanding SAFEs and priced equity rounds, https://www.ycombinator.com/library/6m-understanding-safes-and-priced-equity-rounds)
- Carolynn Levy, in a talk the library dates to 2019, says YC created the SAFE because selling equity through debt made no sense to them: angel investors are not lenders, and startups do not want to think about accruing interest or when a note falls due. (Modern startup funding, https://www.ycombinator.com/library/8F-modern-startup-funding)
- Carolynn Levy, in a talk the library dates to 2019, describes the SAFE as one document that is available online and says founders do not need to hire lawyers to do one. (Modern startup funding, https://www.ycombinator.com/library/8F-modern-startup-funding)
- Nathoo, in a talk the library dates to 2018, says YC had announced a move to post-money SAFEs in the couple of weeks before the talk, where post-money means after all the SAFEs have converted, so that founders can more easily see the dilution they are taking. (Understanding SAFEs and priced equity rounds, https://www.ycombinator.com/library/6m-understanding-safes-and-priced-equity-rounds)
- Nathoo, in a talk the library dates to 2018, gives one formula for both instruments: an investor's ownership is the amount invested divided by the post-money valuation in a priced round or by the post-money valuation cap in a SAFE, so $1 million at a $6 million post-money valuation is 16.67%. (Understanding SAFEs and priced equity rounds, https://www.ycombinator.com/library/6m-understanding-safes-and-priced-equity-rounds)
- Nathoo, in a talk the library dates to 2018, says a SAFE round needs no lead investor, since each investor can sign and send money once the amount and the cap are agreed, while a priced round needs a lead who sets the terms the other investors follow. (Understanding SAFEs and priced equity rounds, https://www.ycombinator.com/library/6m-understanding-safes-and-priced-equity-rounds)
- Nathoo, in a talk the library dates to 2018, advises against holding both SAFEs and convertible notes because the mix complicates the calculations, suggests staying with debt if the company started on it, and says there is nothing wrong with the convertible debt that companies outside the US often raise on. (Understanding SAFEs and priced equity rounds, https://www.ycombinator.com/library/6m-understanding-safes-and-priced-equity-rounds)
- Carolynn Levy, in a talk the library dates to 2019, says a SAFE converts only if the company raises a priced round, is sold or goes public, and that a company which never needs more money is a corner case she left out on purpose to keep the document simple. (Modern startup funding, https://www.ycombinator.com/library/8F-modern-startup-funding)
- Carolynn Levy, in a talk the library dates to 2019, says the SAFE is the wrong instrument for giving someone equity in return for services, because it is built for money up front and stock later. (Modern startup funding, https://www.ycombinator.com/library/8F-modern-startup-funding)
- Carolynn Levy, in a talk the library dates to 2019, says a founder offered five million dollars by a VC as a first fundraise in a priced round should take it if it makes sense for the company, and one whose investor insists on convertible promissory notes should take that money too if it is needed: "It's better than dying." (Modern startup funding, https://www.ycombinator.com/library/8F-modern-startup-funding)
- Carolynn Levy, in a talk the library dates to 2019 and given in Boston, says SAFEs and convertible securities are fully normal on the west coast, expects some investors elsewhere to ask for promissory notes or preferred stock instead, and for the most part still recommends approaching a raise with convertible securities because they are fast and flexible. (Modern startup funding, https://www.ycombinator.com/library/8F-modern-startup-funding)

## How much of the company am I selling, and what happens at the priced round?

- Nathoo, in a talk the library dates to 2018, says founders must know at every stage how much of the company they have sold, which is hard to see on convertible instruments because those are not yet shares. (Understanding SAFEs and priced equity rounds, https://www.ycombinator.com/library/6m-understanding-safes-and-priced-equity-rounds)
- Nathoo, in a talk the library dates to 2018, says understanding the cap table is the job of the CEO or founder and calls leaving it to the lawyers a dangerous statement, and adds that at the beginning a spreadsheet of who owns how many shares is enough. (Understanding SAFEs and priced equity rounds, https://www.ycombinator.com/library/6m-understanding-safes-and-priced-equity-rounds)
- Carolynn Levy, in a talk the library dates to 2019, says holders of convertible securities are not on the cap table, so a founder who has sold only those still looks like a 100% owner until the priced round, and that nothing excuses being surprised to find 30% of the company sold to angel investors. (Modern startup funding, https://www.ycombinator.com/library/8F-modern-startup-funding)
- Nathoo, in a talk the library dates to 2018, works an example on post-money SAFEs: $200,000 at a $4 million cap is 5% and $800,000 at an $8 million cap is 10%, so the founders have sold 15% even though the cap table has not changed. (Understanding SAFEs and priced equity rounds, https://www.ycombinator.com/library/6m-understanding-safes-and-priced-equity-rounds)
- Nathoo, in a talk the library dates to 2018, says that with post-money SAFEs a later SAFE investor does not dilute an earlier one, only the existing shareholders, who at that stage are the founders. (Understanding SAFEs and priced equity rounds, https://www.ycombinator.com/library/6m-understanding-safes-and-priced-equity-rounds)
- Nathoo, in a talk the library dates to 2018, shows in her example a cap table with the founders at 92.5% after an option pool is created, and says that with 15% sold on SAFEs they really own 85% of that, about 78.6%. (Understanding SAFEs and priced equity rounds, https://www.ycombinator.com/library/6m-understanding-safes-and-priced-equity-rounds)
- Nathoo, in a talk the library dates to 2018, says a Series A term sheet usually asks for an option pool of about 10%, sometimes up to about 15%, with more than that fairly non-standard, and that the increase dilutes existing shareholders and SAFE holders but not the new money. (Understanding SAFEs and priced equity rounds, https://www.ycombinator.com/library/6m-understanding-safes-and-priced-equity-rounds)
- Nathoo, in a talk the library dates to 2018, ends her example, a $5 million Series A at a $15 million pre-money valuation, with the founders at 51.5%, and says little can be done about it then because most of that dilution came from the SAFEs. (Understanding SAFEs and priced equity rounds, https://www.ycombinator.com/library/6m-understanding-safes-and-priced-equity-rounds)
- Nathoo, in a talk the library dates to 2018, describes a fairly standard cap table after a Series A, with about 25% to the Series A investors, about 10% in the option pool and the founders just over 50%, and says selling about 15% on SAFEs is about the range founders want. (Understanding SAFEs and priced equity rounds, https://www.ycombinator.com/library/6m-understanding-safes-and-priced-equity-rounds)
- Seibel, in a talk the library dates to 2020, says that when a pre-seed investor, an accelerator and a seed investor each need something like 7 to 12% it is easy to give up 30% before the Series A, and that his best answer is to fund the company with revenue, which makes each round smaller and its valuation higher. (The 5 things that kill startups post seed rounds, https://www.ycombinator.com/library/8a-the-5-things-that-kill-startups-post-seed-rounds)
- Nathoo, in a talk the library dates to 2018, says a priced round converts the SAFEs whatever its price, so a round priced below the cap, which she calls pretty rare, converts them at the round price and gives the holders more than the founder counted on, and her example is a $100 million cap followed by a $25 million priced round. (Understanding SAFEs and priced equity rounds, https://www.ycombinator.com/library/6m-understanding-safes-and-priced-equity-rounds)
- Nathoo, in a talk the library dates to 2018, says founders who raise too much on caps that are too low find at the priced round that they own very little, and that not much can be done then because the contracts with investors are already signed. (Understanding SAFEs and priced equity rounds, https://www.ycombinator.com/library/6m-understanding-safes-and-priced-equity-rounds)
- Nathoo, in a talk the library dates to 2018, tells founders not to over-optimize the cap: rerunning her example with the $800,000 at a $10 million cap instead of $8 million moves the founders from 51.5% to 52.7% after the Series A, a gain she does not think is worth the pain of negotiating. (Understanding SAFEs and priced equity rounds, https://www.ycombinator.com/library/6m-understanding-safes-and-priced-equity-rounds)
- Nathoo, in a talk the library dates to 2018, says voting power at the Series A comes from the composition of the board, and that founders generally want to keep a majority of it, for example two founders and one representative of the lead investor. (Understanding SAFEs and priced equity rounds, https://www.ycombinator.com/library/6m-understanding-safes-and-priced-equity-rounds)
- Carolynn Levy, in a talk the library dates to 2019, says a startup cannot raise a meaningful amount by selling common stock, which founders typically buy for a fraction of a penny, so investors are sold a separate and more expensive class called preferred stock. (Modern startup funding, https://www.ycombinator.com/library/8F-modern-startup-funding)
- Carolynn Levy, in a talk the library dates to 2019, says priced rounds still happen but are no longer how most startups raise their first money, and that the priced round documents are more standard than they were and available online, though everyone still tends to hire a lawyer for them. (Modern startup funding, https://www.ycombinator.com/library/8F-modern-startup-funding)
- Carolynn Levy, in a talk the library dates to 2019, says raising in small flexible amounts can leave a company with 25 or 35 angel investors where there used to be six to ten, which she calls not a bad thing but an administrative challenge, because all of them become stockholders at the priced round and their consents are needed. (Modern startup funding, https://www.ycombinator.com/library/8F-modern-startup-funding)

## What legal setup do I need: incorporation, founder stock and vesting?

- Carolynn Levy, in a talk the library dates to 2018, strongly recommends incorporating in Delaware, mainly because the filing process there is easy and the service fast, and adds that most public companies are Delaware companies and some investors require it. (Startup legal mechanics, https://www.ycombinator.com/library/7R-startup-legal-mechanics)
- Carolynn Levy, in a talk the library dates to 2018, recommends incorporating through an online platform built for startups and names Clerky, which the transcript spells Clerkie, and Stripe Atlas, because they also provide the post-incorporation documents, and without those founders can fail to buy their stock. (Startup legal mechanics, https://www.ycombinator.com/library/7R-startup-legal-mechanics)
- Carolynn Levy, in a talk the library dates to 2018, says the right moment to incorporate differs by startup but that in general sooner is better, because a corporation shields founders from personal liability, holds the intellectual property they create, and is needed to take professional investment, run payroll and sign contracts. (Startup legal mechanics, https://www.ycombinator.com/library/7R-startup-legal-mechanics)
- Carolynn Levy, in a talk the library dates to 2018, calls forming an LLC for the tax advantages usually a mistake for a startup that plans to raise beyond a small group of friends and family, because most angels and VC firms, in her words the vast majority, will not put money into one, and she does not think the tax benefit outweighs the inconvenience of converting later. (Startup legal mechanics, https://www.ycombinator.com/library/7R-startup-legal-mechanics)
- Kwon, in a talk the library dates to 2018, says a company filed in Delaware is a C corporation by default and should stay one, because that is the kind of entity investors are used to and outside investment will undo an S corporation election most of the time anyway. (Startup legal mechanics, https://www.ycombinator.com/library/7R-startup-legal-mechanics)
- Carolynn Levy, in a talk the library dates to 2018, says founders should split stock more or less equally if all of them will work hard, without giving too much weight to whoever had the idea, because all the hard work and all the value lie ahead. (Startup legal mechanics, https://www.ycombinator.com/library/7R-startup-legal-mechanics)
- Carolynn Levy, in a talk the library dates to 2018, says founder shares should vest, gives about four years as the standard period, and explains that if a founder quits before then the company automatically buys back the unvested shares. (Startup legal mechanics, https://www.ycombinator.com/library/7R-startup-legal-mechanics)
- Carolynn Levy, in a talk the library dates to 2018, calls skipping vesting because the team has worked together for years a big mistake: a founder who burns out can leave with a giant stake while the rest do the work, and fixing it is painful because the stock price may have risen. (Startup legal mechanics, https://www.ycombinator.com/library/7R-startup-legal-mechanics)
- Carolynn Levy, in a talk the library dates to 2018, says a single founder should have vesting too, because investors find no vesting unattractive and because the employees hired later will have vesting on their options. (Startup legal mechanics, https://www.ycombinator.com/library/7R-startup-legal-mechanics)
- Kwon, in a talk the library dates to 2018, explains the 83-B election as a tax election: without it a founder is taxed on stock as it vests at its value then, and with it the founder is taxed at purchase, when founder stock costs a nominal amount, so he says making it is in most cases the best thing for a startup founder to do. (Startup legal mechanics, https://www.ycombinator.com/library/7R-startup-legal-mechanics)
- Kwon, in a talk the library dates to 2018, says the thirty days for filing the 83-B election run from the day the founder actually buys the stock, count calendar days and are a hard deadline, and that little can be done if it is missed. (Startup legal mechanics, https://www.ycombinator.com/library/7R-startup-legal-mechanics)
- Carolynn Levy, in a talk the library dates to 2018, adds that the date on the restricted stock purchase agreement should be the day the founder pays for the stock, and that the company must keep its own signed and dated copy of each 83-B election form. (Startup legal mechanics, https://www.ycombinator.com/library/7R-startup-legal-mechanics)
- Jon Levy, in a talk the library dates to 2018, says the standard people think of saving for employees is, by his estimate, ten to twenty percent, and that the right figure is specific to the company. (Startup legal mechanics, https://www.ycombinator.com/library/7R-startup-legal-mechanics)
- Carolynn Levy, in a talk the library dates to 2018, says granting stock options requires a 409A valuation, which most startups do not want to get until after a fundraising event, and that promised employee stock should be granted sooner instead of later because the stock gets more expensive with time. (Startup legal mechanics, https://www.ycombinator.com/library/7R-startup-legal-mechanics)

## Which legal mistakes cost founders later?

- Carolynn Levy, in a talk the library dates to 2018, says employment agreements do not suit startups, because employment is at-will by default and a court may read an agreement's language as changing that to for-cause, which makes people harder to terminate. (Startup legal mechanics, https://www.ycombinator.com/library/7R-startup-legal-mechanics)
- Carolynn Levy, in a talk the library dates to 2018, says every founder and everyone who later works for the company should sign a confidential information and invention assignment agreement, which protects trade secrets and makes the company the owner of the intellectual property created. (Startup legal mechanics, https://www.ycombinator.com/library/7R-startup-legal-mechanics)
- Carolynn Levy, in a talk the library dates to 2018, says paying a friend only in shares is generally a mistake because workers have to be paid in money, with an exception for a properly classified independent contractor under a consulting agreement, and notes that California's classification rules had just become stricter. (Startup legal mechanics, https://www.ycombinator.com/library/7R-startup-legal-mechanics)
- Kwon, in a talk the library dates to 2018, says unpaid interns are generally illegal because interns are generally treated as employees, and mentions a YC company whose fourteen unpaid interns became a big mess. (Startup legal mechanics, https://www.ycombinator.com/library/7R-startup-legal-mechanics)
- Carolynn Levy, in a talk the library dates to 2018, says founders need to understand every provision of their founder stock purchase agreement even if they skim the bylaws, and that the larger problem is fundraising documents, since many founders have told her they will not read a five-page SAFE. (Startup legal mechanics, https://www.ycombinator.com/library/7R-startup-legal-mechanics)
- Carolynn Levy, in a talk the library dates to 2018, says the company's legal documents should be kept signed and dated in a shared place such as a shared Dropbox folder and not in one co-founder's email, so that no single person controls access, and that blanks in formation documents look amateur when investors do due diligence. (Startup legal mechanics, https://www.ycombinator.com/library/7R-startup-legal-mechanics)
- Carolynn Levy, in a talk the library dates to 2018, says a young company that receives a cease and desist letter over its name from a more established, better-funded company should drop the name and pick a new one, because it has built little value in the name and a trademark dispute is expensive and distracting. (Startup legal mechanics, https://www.ycombinator.com/library/7R-startup-legal-mechanics)
- Carolynn Levy, in a talk the library dates to 2018, says a founder who still has a full-time job should keep the startup separate, working on their own time with their own resources, and should research the rules first because they differ by state and country and a non-compete agreement may apply. (Startup legal mechanics, https://www.ycombinator.com/library/7R-startup-legal-mechanics)
- Carolynn Levy, in a talk the library dates to 2018, says founders paying themselves, vesting on founder shares and a signed invention assignment agreement significantly reduce the legal drama when co-founders split. (Startup legal mechanics, https://www.ycombinator.com/library/7R-startup-legal-mechanics)
- In a talk the library dates to 2018 with Carolynn Levy, Jon Levy and Jason Kwon, the answer on shareholder agreements among founders, which a later remark credits to Jon Levy, calls them a non-US kind of document and overkill for a startup, since chances are a startup that goes wrong has no assets to divide. (Startup legal mechanics, https://www.ycombinator.com/library/7R-startup-legal-mechanics)

## How do I manage the company's money?

- Nathoo, in a talk the library dates to 2019, says a founder needs three numbers to judge the company's health, the bank balance, the money coming in and the money going out, all available from online banking or bank statements without a bookkeeper or financial software. (Managing startup finances, https://www.ycombinator.com/library/8I-managing-startup-finances)
- Nathoo, in a talk the library dates to 2019, defines runway as the bank balance divided by average burn, so 150K in the bank at a 15K burn is 10 months, and says pretending the burn is lower changes nothing, because the money still runs out on the same day. (Managing startup finances, https://www.ycombinator.com/library/8I-managing-startup-finances)
- Nathoo, in a talk the library dates to 2019, says these numbers should be checked at least every week, and sometimes daily when runway is getting low. (Managing startup finances, https://www.ycombinator.com/library/8I-managing-startup-finances)
- Nathoo, in a talk the library dates to 2019, gives as a rule of thumb that an employee costs about 25 to 50 percent more than the salary, depending on location, once equipment, desk space and health insurance are counted. (Managing startup finances, https://www.ycombinator.com/library/8I-managing-startup-finances)
- Nathoo, in a talk the library dates to 2019, warns against assuming paid acquisition costs stay constant, because the earliest users are the most motivated and it gets harder and more expensive to find and convert users over time. (Managing startup finances, https://www.ycombinator.com/library/8I-managing-startup-finances)
- Nathoo, in a talk the library dates to 2019, says hiring a bookkeeper once money is raised is normal, but the founders stay responsible for the numbers, because an outside bookkeeper works from bank statements with a best guess and will not always be right, so founders must read the monthly reports and question anything strange. (Managing startup finances, https://www.ycombinator.com/library/8I-managing-startup-finances)
- Nathoo, in a talk the library dates to 2019, says the ratio of revenue to employees is a better measure than headcount, that every hire is an investment that should return, and that founders should be prepared to fire fast when someone is not working out. (Managing startup finances, https://www.ycombinator.com/library/8I-managing-startup-finances)
- Nathoo, in a talk the library dates to 2019, says a company still looking for product market fit should spend as little as possible, because adding employees will not get it there faster. (Managing startup finances, https://www.ycombinator.com/library/8I-managing-startup-finances)
- Nathoo, in a talk the library dates to 2019, tells founders to assume the last money they raised is the last they will ever raise and to aim for profitability on it, because investors who are expected to put in more do not always do so. (Managing startup finances, https://www.ycombinator.com/library/8I-managing-startup-finances)
- Nathoo, in a talk the library dates to 2019, says the time to decide between raising and reaching profitability is at twelve months of runway, because at six months a raise could take three more months while leverage falls with the cash balance, and a failed raise then leaves little time to turn around. (Managing startup finances, https://www.ycombinator.com/library/8I-managing-startup-finances)
- Nathoo, in a talk the library dates to 2019 and speaking of US practice, says a full-time CFO comes surprisingly late and that even after a Series A a company, in her estimate, does not need one, with a bookkeeper needed earlier, a CPA once a year for tax returns, and the founders doing the finance work before that. (Managing startup finances, https://www.ycombinator.com/library/8I-managing-startup-finances)
- Nathoo, in a talk the library dates to 2019, says her inclination is against putting forecasts in a seed deck before product market fit, reads a seed investor who asks for monthly growth predictions as a sign of inexperience with early-stage companies, and says that by Series A a company should have some plans. (Managing startup finances, https://www.ycombinator.com/library/8I-managing-startup-finances)

## Am I default alive or default dead?

- Graham's 2015 essay says the first thing he wants to know about a startup that has operated for more than 8 or 9 months is whether, with expenses constant and revenue growth as it has been in recent months, it reaches profitability on the money it has left. (Default Alive or Default Dead?, https://paulgraham.com/aord.html)
- Graham's 2015 essay says half the founders he talks to do not know whether they are default alive or default dead. (Default Alive or Default Dead?, https://paulgraham.com/aord.html)
- Graham's 2015 essay proposes asking the question too early instead of too late, because worrying early is not very dangerous and worrying late is, and late realisation is how founders land in what he calls the fatal pinch: default dead, slow growth and not enough time to fix it. (Default Alive or Default Dead?, https://paulgraham.com/aord.html)
- Graham's 2015 essay says investor interest is as a rule a function of growth, and that steep revenue growth, which he puts at over 5x a year, lets a founder only start to count on investors while not yet profitable. (Default Alive or Default Dead?, https://paulgraham.com/aord.html)
- Graham's 2015 essay says fundraising can never safely be more than plan A, and that founders should write down exactly what they would do to survive without new money and exactly when they would switch to that plan B. (Default Alive or Default Dead?, https://paulgraham.com/aord.html)
- Graham's 2015 essay says spending and growth are, to his surprise, barely connected: fast growth usually comes from a product that meets a big need directly, and high spending from a product that is expensive to develop or sell, or from waste. (Default Alive or Default Dead?, https://paulgraham.com/aord.html)
- Graham's 2015 essay says the way to avoid being default dead is not to hire too fast, which he calls by far the biggest killer of startups that raise money. (Default Alive or Default Dead?, https://paulgraham.com/aord.html)
- Graham's 2015 essay says founders who have just raised are often encouraged to overhire by their VCs, for whom kill-or-cure strategies are optimal because a portfolio protects them, while the founder wants above all to survive. (Default Alive or Default Dead?, https://paulgraham.com/aord.html)
- Nathoo, in a talk the library dates to 2019, says being profitable gives freedom because the company does not need to raise, that not needing money makes it easier to raise, and that knowing one specific expense could be turned off to reach profit is also a safety net. (Managing startup finances, https://www.ycombinator.com/library/8I-managing-startup-finances)
- Caldwell, in the 2022 conversation with Seibel, defines default alive as different from profitable: the company may be burning money today, but its revenue growth is high enough that it becomes profitable before the bank account reaches zero, with no further investor money. (Dalton & Michael: Save your startup during an economic downturn, https://www.ycombinator.com/library/Ed-dalton-michael-save-your-startup-during-an-economic-downturn)

## What do I do with less than a year of runway?

- Caldwell, in an undated post for founders who have raised an angel or institutional round and have 12 months or less of runway, says a founder who is Default Dead is responsible for becoming Default Alive immediately, by growing revenue faster, cutting costs, or both. (Advice for companies with less than 1 year of runway, https://www.ycombinator.com/library/3Z-advice-for-companies-with-less-than-1-year-of-runway)
- Caldwell, in an undated post, lists beliefs that keep low-runway founders from acting, among them that investors will keep funding the company, that it can be sold if fundraising fails, and that cutting costs will put off acquirers and new investors or sink employee morale. (Advice for companies with less than 1 year of runway, https://www.ycombinator.com/library/3Z-advice-for-companies-with-less-than-1-year-of-runway)
- Caldwell, in an undated post, says delaying a cut in burn is a bad strategy, because an investor or acquirer is best served by stalling until the founder has no leverage left, and an opportunistic one is unlikely to say no outright. (Advice for companies with less than 1 year of runway, https://www.ycombinator.com/library/3Z-advice-for-companies-with-less-than-1-year-of-runway)
- Caldwell, in an undated post, says that when revenue cannot rise fast enough the founder must cut costs, and that leases are binding agreements that are very hard to exit, with real estate obligations often what kills later stage companies. (Advice for companies with less than 1 year of runway, https://www.ycombinator.com/library/3Z-advice-for-companies-with-less-than-1-year-of-runway)
- Caldwell, in an undated post, says payroll is the most likely source of high burn and over-hiring usually its root cause, and that a founder who cuts staff must treat the people who leave well and be transparent with those who stay. (Advice for companies with less than 1 year of runway, https://www.ycombinator.com/library/3Z-advice-for-companies-with-less-than-1-year-of-runway)
- Caldwell, in an undated post, says the easiest things to cut are PR and marketing expenses and incidental spending on perks and parties. (Advice for companies with less than 1 year of runway, https://www.ycombinator.com/library/3Z-advice-for-companies-with-less-than-1-year-of-runway)
- Caldwell, in an undated post, says a company with less than three months of cash has to face the issue and account for its liabilities and for the scenario of shutting down. (Advice for companies with less than 1 year of runway, https://www.ycombinator.com/library/3Z-advice-for-companies-with-less-than-1-year-of-runway)
- Caldwell, in an undated post, says that in many cases less than two months of cash is the point of no return, where the founder must lay off employees with severance, pay down obligations and use the remaining cash for shutdown costs. (Advice for companies with less than 1 year of runway, https://www.ycombinator.com/library/3Z-advice-for-companies-with-less-than-1-year-of-runway)
- Caldwell, in an undated post, writes "Do not become insolvent. Pay your debts." and adds that tax and payroll obligations must be paid, that especially messy cases can end in personal liability, and that founders should consult their lawyers on an orderly shutdown. (Advice for companies with less than 1 year of runway, https://www.ycombinator.com/library/3Z-advice-for-companies-with-less-than-1-year-of-runway)
- In the 2022 conversation between Caldwell and Seibel, an acqui-hire is described as no fallback for a company low on runway, because nobody on the buy side wants a company that is out of money and may carry legal liability, and 12 or 18 months of runway leaves flexibility on an acquisition that three to six months does not. (Dalton & Michael: Save your startup during an economic downturn, https://www.ycombinator.com/library/Ed-dalton-michael-save-your-startup-during-an-economic-downturn)

## What changes when the market turns?

- In the 2022 conversation between Caldwell and Seibel, founders are told to expect the next round to differ from the last and to plan for a wider range of outcomes, since there are fewer Series A rounds than seed rounds and fewer Series B rounds than Series A rounds, and the economy and interest rates are outside a founder's control. (Dalton & Michael: Save your startup during an economic downturn, https://www.ycombinator.com/library/Ed-dalton-michael-save-your-startup-during-an-economic-downturn)
- In the 2022 conversation between Caldwell and Seibel, most fundraises are said to fail, which reading only about the successful ones hides, so a default alive company can fail to raise and carry on while one run with about three months of runway dies if its raise does not work. (Dalton & Michael: Save your startup during an economic downturn, https://www.ycombinator.com/library/Ed-dalton-michael-save-your-startup-during-an-economic-downturn)
- In the 2022 conversation between Caldwell and Seibel, being default alive is said to give leverage twice in a fundraise: the founder who does not need the deal pitches better, and the investor knows the company does not need them, while a good investor can tell when a company is about to die and offers worse terms. (Dalton & Michael: Save your startup during an economic downturn, https://www.ycombinator.com/library/Ed-dalton-michael-save-your-startup-during-an-economic-downturn)
- In the 2022 conversation between Caldwell and Seibel, citing Graham's essay, founder and investor incentives on burn are called opposite: an investor with a portfolio does fine when companies either grow explosively or die fast, while the founder is choosing between zero and more time to find product market fit. (Dalton & Michael: Save your startup during an economic downturn, https://www.ycombinator.com/library/Ed-dalton-michael-save-your-startup-during-an-economic-downturn)
- In the 2022 conversation between Caldwell and Seibel, it is called extremely rare for an investor to demand that a company burn heavily, and the problem is said to be that founders need very little push to do it. (Dalton & Michael: Save your startup during an economic downturn, https://www.ycombinator.com/library/Ed-dalton-michael-save-your-startup-during-an-economic-downturn)
- In the 2022 conversation between Caldwell and Seibel, the advice given at the end of a YC batch is put as "Stay lean until you have something, and then spend money on growing it." and founders are said to conclude wrongly that spending more brings product market fit sooner. (Dalton & Michael: Save your startup during an economic downturn, https://www.ycombinator.com/library/Ed-dalton-michael-save-your-startup-during-an-economic-downturn)
- In the 2022 conversation between Caldwell and Seibel, the tough moves for a default dead company are listed as headcount first, then ad spend whose payback period keeps lengthening, then raising prices so each sale stops losing money, with the remark that the best fix is never to over-hire. (Dalton & Michael: Save your startup during an economic downturn, https://www.ycombinator.com/library/Ed-dalton-michael-save-your-startup-during-an-economic-downturn)
- In the 2022 conversation between Caldwell and Seibel, taking a deliberate hit to growth to become default alive is compared to a bankruptcy that a startup recovers from quickly, because at any time a startup is judged on its last six to eighteen months. (Dalton & Michael: Save your startup during an economic downturn, https://www.ycombinator.com/library/Ed-dalton-michael-save-your-startup-during-an-economic-downturn)
- Seibel, in the 2022 conversation with Caldwell, says Justin.tv was making about $750,000 a month in revenue against a million dollars a month in expenses, with half a million in the bank, when he told employees the options were to die in two months, reach break-even or become profitable. (Dalton & Michael: Save your startup during an economic downturn, https://www.ycombinator.com/library/Ed-dalton-michael-save-your-startup-during-an-economic-downturn)
- Seibel, in the 2022 conversation with Caldwell, says Justin.tv let some people go and put video ads on everything, that the meeting was in August, that the company broke even by October and that by the end of December it had generated $1.2 million in profit. (Dalton & Michael: Save your startup during an economic downturn, https://www.ycombinator.com/library/Ed-dalton-michael-save-your-startup-during-an-economic-downturn)
- In the 2022 conversation between Caldwell and Seibel, the founders are said to have to push for survival, not the board, the VPs or the CFO, and Amazon is given as a company that always knew it was in a low margin business and ran itself that way. (Dalton & Michael: Save your startup during an economic downturn, https://www.ycombinator.com/library/Ed-dalton-michael-save-your-startup-during-an-economic-downturn)

## How should I spend after a seed round?

- Seibel, in a talk the library dates to 2020, says the vast majority of the companies YC sees raise money at demo day still die, and that about 70% of them never find any form of product market fit. (The 5 things that kill startups post seed rounds, https://www.ycombinator.com/library/8a-the-5-things-that-kill-startups-post-seed-rounds)
- Seibel, in a talk the library dates to 2020, tells companies before product market fit to cap their burn: fix a monthly amount, hold to it, and pay for anything above it from revenue instead of from the money raised. (The 5 things that kill startups post seed rounds, https://www.ycombinator.com/library/8a-the-5-things-that-kill-startups-post-seed-rounds)
- Seibel, in a talk the library dates to 2020, suggests considering a smaller seed round, because less money means less dilution and makes magical thinking and aggressive spending harder. (The 5 things that kill startups post seed rounds, https://www.ycombinator.com/library/8a-the-5-things-that-kill-startups-post-seed-rounds)
- Seibel, in a talk the library dates to 2020, says that all things equal raising more than a company needs leads to less success, because a larger seed raises the Series A investor's expectations: a company at $1 million ARR after a $4 million seed is compared with one at the same ARR that raised a million or a million and a half. (The 5 things that kill startups post seed rounds, https://www.ycombinator.com/library/8a-the-5-things-that-kill-startups-post-seed-rounds)
- Seibel, in a talk the library dates to 2020, says he has yet to consistently meet founders who can keep from spending more when they have more money in the bank. (The 5 things that kill startups post seed rounds, https://www.ycombinator.com/library/8a-the-5-things-that-kill-startups-post-seed-rounds)
- Seibel, in a talk the library dates to 2020, lists signs that a team has talked itself into product market fit it does not have: a team that goes from 4 to 12, more business people than engineers, no metrics dashboards, spending on nice things, and changing the KPI when it flatlines. (The 5 things that kill startups post seed rounds, https://www.ycombinator.com/library/8a-the-5-things-that-kill-startups-post-seed-rounds)
- Seibel, in a talk the library dates to 2020, proposes a three-month rule for early hires, under which an employee whose resignation three months in would not leave the founder distraught is one he suggests letting go, and says an early salesperson must pay for themselves. (The 5 things that kill startups post seed rounds, https://www.ycombinator.com/library/8a-the-5-things-that-kill-startups-post-seed-rounds)
- Seibel, in a talk the library dates to 2020, warns founders against making an investor their boss, and gives as signs hiring a recruiter before product market fit, burn rising each month while the main KPI does not, and believing the investor will backstop the company if it follows their plan. (The 5 things that kill startups post seed rounds, https://www.ycombinator.com/library/8a-the-5-things-that-kill-startups-post-seed-rounds)
