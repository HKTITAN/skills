# Pitch deck bootcamp on investor gaps and framing, Mohit Bansal

Mohit Bansal of DeckRooster ran a full-day pitch bootcamp for the cohort. The recording has three blocks: what investors look for and a worksheet that compares the founder's view with the investor's view, founders reading out their worksheets with his comments, and a session on reframing a business with a "framing canvas". This note keeps the teaching only. The transcript is noisy, so only points that are clear in the recording are kept.

## What was said

### Start from what the investor wants

- Mohit Bansal says a pitch is a sale, so the founder has to know what the listener is motivated by and align the pitch with what that listener is looking for.
- He says weak metrics, missing features or a missing story are often not the root cause of a weak pitch. They are what founders worry about after seeing someone else's deck.
- He says investors are looking for a return, and the question for a venture investor is whether one company can return enough to cover the other bets in the fund.
- He says scalability is part of how an investor judges whether that return is possible.

### Two things an investor judges: opportunity potential and capability

- He uses a car race analogy. The car, engine, terrain and fuel set the maximum mileage, which he calls the opportunity potential. The driver's ability to get that mileage out is the capability.
- He says opportunity potential is how big the opportunity can be, and capability is how much of that potential this team and company can extract.
- He says the platform a startup sits on, for example being in Y Combinator, gives access to people, resources and markets. He files that under capability, because it raises the ability to extract the potential.
- He says the earlier the stage, the more weight the opportunity potential carries, and the later the stage, the more weight capability carries.
- He says an investor's own abilities can add to capability: it becomes the founder's capability plus the investor's if they come on board.
- He says a pitch often fails because the investor does not see the potential the way the founder sees it, either because it is not conveyed or because it does not exist.

### The worksheet: why this, why now, why us, for now and for the future

- He says most businesses have two parts, the now and the future, and for some the future business looks very different from the current one.
- He structures the pitch around three questions: why invest in this kind of opportunity, why invest right now, and why invest in this company.
- He says a sales pitch answers the same three questions for a customer, and that without a strong "why now" the buyer feels no urgency and puts the decision off.
- For the short term, his worksheet asks about confidence in the current product-market fit, whether the opportunity is at an inflection point, and whether the company will be among the winners.
- For the long term, it asks about confidence in the future product-market fit, whether this will be huge, whether it will be massively profitable, whether the chance to invest will still be there later, and whether the company will be among the winners.
- He says he asks whether the company will be "among the winners", not the only winner.
- Founders mark on a five-step scale, from strongly negative through neutral to strongly positive, where they stand and where they think investors stand on each question.
- He says a founder who has not pitched much should still mark a best guess of the investor's view, because working out what the listener thinks is part of the founder's job.
- He says the founder's own mark is the upper limit of what the pitch can achieve. If the founder is neutral or negative on a point, the pitch cannot do better than that and there is no point faking it.
- He says the gaps should be discussed in a group, because every founder is biased towards their own business and others can question the assessment.
- He says the exercise breaks a vague "this will not work" into specific points that founder and investor can discuss.
- He says if investors do not believe the current business, a strong belief in the future business does not help, because they cannot see how the future will be reached.
- He says "among the winners" on the opportunity side is about whether the specific approach to the solution is the right one. The founder's experience and track record belong on the capability side.
- He says the answer to "why will you win when others enter" usually comes from moats being built, such as partnerships or a distribution network, and less from the team's confidence or commitment.
- He says the worksheet is built for the mindset of venture investors and angels. Strategic investors and acquirers look for different things, so it has to be adapted for them.
- He says the aim of the workshop is the skill of seeing your own business as an investor sees it, not his opinion on any one business.

### Risk, value and insight

- He says it is the investor's job to take risk, and risk is where value gets unlocked.
- He says each funding round creates value by de-risking part of the business. Once a part is proven, there is no further value for a new investor to gain from that part.
- He says an early-stage founder without proof can still build a case with logic, a hypothesis, data, anecdotes and examples of similar companies.
- He says different investors enter at different points. Some take risk before product-market fit, and others want product-market fit first and then bet on growth.
- He says when a founder feels strongly positive and others do not, the founder should dig into the source of that feeling, because it usually holds an insight the founder knows but has never articulated.
- He calls these "unknown knowns", things we know without knowing that we know them.
- He says he does not take customers' stated opinions at face value, because people say one thing and do another. What counts is whether they buy when the product is in front of them and they are asked to pay.

### Framing and context

- He says if a founder does not frame the business, the listener puts it in a default category.
- He says once a listener accepts a framing, they attach assumptions to it about market size, scale and margins, and it is hard to move them afterwards.
- He separates framing from context. Framing is how you position what the business is. Context is the background that makes the framing believable.
- His framing canvas has the target group, the broad need area and the specific need within it, the broad solution category and the specific solution within it, and the context.
- He says the founder is free to change any of these, but has to prove each one, and the framing must be true and consistent with the business's numbers.
- He says changing one box changes the others. If a reframing weakens one answer, the founder has to fix that answer or add context.
- He says a new framing changes who the business is compared with, and the founder then has to argue why it differs from those players and why the market is moving its way.
- He says a unique selling proposition is for the customer choosing between you and someone else. It follows from the framing and is not the framing.
- He says, "if you don't build your arguments, then they are building their arguments themselves."
- He says an argument rests on two things, logic and data, and both have to be there. Data strengthens an argument but is not the argument.
- He says where a startup has no data of its own, it can show reasons and outside examples where people already behave the way the thesis needs.
- He says adding context can change what people believe, and that when founder and investor believe different things the cause is usually a different understanding that the pitch has to bridge.

### Narrative

- He says the story of how the founder started the company shows passion and drive, but that is all it does.
- He says the business narrative comes from what you do, who you do it for, what need you serve, how the world is shifting and where the company sits in that shift.

### How to work with the canvas

- He says building a pitch deck is problem solving. Find the biggest gap between your view and the investor's view first and start there.
- He says when the biggest gap is fixed by reframing or adding context, other answers usually move in the right direction too, because something about the business is finally being said.
- He says not every box needs changing. Change only what is required.
- He says finding the right framing takes introspection and repeated articulation. User research can feed it. He says he has no shortcut or framework for that part.
- He says AI tools produce options for a target group or need and can act as a trigger, but each option has to be checked against what is true for the business.
- He says a startup has one narrative from an investor's point of view. Two products or two target groups need a higher-level reason for sitting in one business.
- He suggests describing two different user groups by what they share, for example calling children and elders "dependents", instead of by age.
- He says when the future business differs from the current one, investors need a clear path from one to the other, built on something the current business creates, such as a brand, data or access to users.
- He says framing can be changed at any stage of a company, and an early-stage company can also change the business itself if the exercise shows a misfit that framing cannot fix.

## Examples and cases used

- A coffee shop, hypothetical: framed as a meeting place or an alternative workplace for working professionals, it is compared with co-working players such as WeWork instead of cafes. Wi-Fi and charging become the core product, and context has to explain the economics of people staying longer.
- A language learning app, hypothetical: framed as a daily micro-learning habit builder, it is no longer confined to the language education market.
- An edtech company, hypothetical: framed as an entertainment-first learning platform.
- A regional-language entertainment platform his firm worked with: the audience was first described as "Bharat", which made investors assume users with little ability to pay. It was redefined as people attached to their regional language, including those living outside the home state or abroad, and the product as identity expression through entertainment.
- A platform that was being compared with ShareChat: reframed from social media to a local network, with a narrative on why people in smaller towns look for schools, jobs and marriage matches close to home.
- A cloud telephony company: no reframing, only added context. Customer calls were moving outside office premises, as with a Swiggy delivery partner calling a customer, and office-based call tracking does not cover that.
- A wired broadband company: its focus was articulated as "unstructured urban" areas, as opposed to gated communities and high-rises. Context on homes moving from one connected device to many answered the doubt about mobile internet replacing wired lines.
- Seekho: good traction in short educational videos but investors were not coming in. The deck defined a new learning category and avoided the edtech app label.
- A company first seen as "pay now, buy later": reframed as "save now, buy later" and placed on a two-by-two of small to large spends and planned to unplanned spends. Large planned spends are where the product fits.
