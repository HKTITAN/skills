# Building products and startups

This theme collects what he tells builders: how to test an idea, what a first version should be, when to listen to users, what to refuse, how small a company can stay, why fun and craft pay off, what games teach, and why old tech does not get fixed. Most of it comes from his 2021 to 2023 startup videos, written while he worked as a product manager, and from his 2024 to 2026 essays, written while he was building his own product, Flask. Product facts are his research as of each video's date, his views on listening to users and on ideas against execution shifted over the years, and the Lovable, Notion and Figma episodes were made with those companies' teams.

## Is the idea or the execution what matters?

- In 2021 he opened a video on startup ideas by saying "ideas are worthless and execution is what really matters", and mocked the friend who claims he thought of Uber or Airbnb before they were founded. (Reacting to Innovative Ideas From Reddit at 0:00, https://www.youtube.com/watch?v=82E7VoxCPc0&t=0s)
- He still thinks ideas are worth playing with because they are free and anyone can have one, and he closed the same video saying execution matters but you can still have fun with ideas. (Reacting to Innovative Ideas From Reddit at 0:00, https://www.youtube.com/watch?v=82E7VoxCPc0&t=0s; Reacting to Innovative Ideas From Reddit at 9:45, https://www.youtube.com/watch?v=82E7VoxCPc0&t=585s)
- In November 2023 he called the idea of Humane's AI Pin great but said it leaves many questions unanswered about how it would work in daily life, and that "this is not it". He said the goal of products is to turn the idea into meaningful execution, which is the hard part: the actual design and engineering, working within the technical limitations you have. (We Need To Talk About The AI Pin at 7:30, https://www.youtube.com/watch?v=E2-L-Gv0wr0&t=450s)
- He says designing and building tech products is a rabbit hole of seven levels, starting at level one with the smallest visual details and running up to the product strategies of the biggest tech companies, and that these levels separate products people hate using from ones that make billions. (The 7 Levels of Tech Design at 0:45, https://www.youtube.com/watch?v=FQVRfadJkJk&t=45s)
- He says vision alone fails: General Magic had the same touchscreen phone vision as the iPhone and flopped because it lacked the levels underneath, and those lower levels are the difference between Steve Jobs's team and everyone else. (The 7 Levels of Tech Design at 15:45, https://www.youtube.com/watch?v=FQVRfadJkJk&t=945s)
- He says most people think great products are built around great technology, but most times you first know the perfect interaction and then go and find the technology that makes it possible. (The 7 Levels of Tech Design at 7:30, https://www.youtube.com/watch?v=FQVRfadJkJk&t=450s)
- He says one good structural decision in an app's logic makes everything after it simpler. In Flask he turned a fixed done toggle into a rule that everything is a tag, which later gave him filters and a new comments feature almost for free. (The 7 Levels of Tech Design at 12:00, https://www.youtube.com/watch?v=FQVRfadJkJk&t=720s)
- He says you can build a company by reframing what something is for without new technology: Loom reframed video as a replacement for meetings and was bought for $975 million, and Superhuman reframed email as the fastest email and charges $30 a month. (The 7 Levels of Tech Design at 14:15, https://www.youtube.com/watch?v=FQVRfadJkJk&t=855s)
- In May 2025, in his video on Lovable, he said that building apps this way still has limits but is moving so fast that it is starting to change one of the golden rules of tech, that ideas are nothing and execution is everything. (Behind The Scenes Of Lovable's Insane Design at 12:00, https://www.youtube.com/watch?v=ZoVLS-Wxr1s&t=720s)
- In the 2025 Lovable video he said cheap building also breaks tech's need for scale: an app that costs $300,000 and solves a problem for three people made no sense before, since each would have to pay $100,000. (Behind The Scenes Of Lovable's Insane Design at 6:45, https://www.youtube.com/watch?v=ZoVLS-Wxr1s&t=405s)
- He qualified his 2025 praise of vibe coding in the same video: you cannot prompt "build me GTA 6" and expect to get it, tools like Lovable have a ceiling on the complexity of what you can build, and the people making the most advanced products with Lovable are developers who could already build before and now do it much faster. (Behind The Scenes Of Lovable's Insane Design at 6:00, https://www.youtube.com/watch?v=ZoVLS-Wxr1s&t=360s; Behind The Scenes Of Lovable's Insane Design at 9:45, https://www.youtube.com/watch?v=ZoVLS-Wxr1s&t=585s)

## How do you check a problem before you build?

- He says in 99 percent of cases a shower idea fails because nobody wants it, and that most startups die after spending years and a lot of cash on something people do not want. (Do You Have a Genius Startup Idea at 0:00, https://www.youtube.com/watch?v=HABFEs90MZ8&t=0s)
- His validation advice starts with two moves: step back from your solution to the problem you are trying to solve, then ask who has that problem in its most severe form and narrow your audience to them. (Do You Have a Genius Startup Idea at 0:45, https://www.youtube.com/watch?v=HABFEs90MZ8&t=45s)
- He says your idea is a solution, so go back to the problem. His own example is a Tinder-style app for matching creators, where the real problem might be having nobody to give feedback on a video or feeling lonely making content. (Do You Have a Genius Startup Idea at 0:45, https://www.youtube.com/watch?v=HABFEs90MZ8&t=45s)
- He calls jobs to be done one of the most important frameworks in design: people hire a product to solve a problem, and working backwards from the drill to the hole to the picture you want to hang leads to better solutions, such as an adhesive hook. (Airbnb Just Hacked The Travel Industry Again at 3:00, https://www.youtube.com/watch?v=OwiztfnZwE4&t=180s)
- He says to pick the audience that has the problem in its most severe form, and narrows creators down to YouTubers with 2,000 to 10,000 subscribers who upload regularly. (Do You Have a Genius Startup Idea at 1:30, https://www.youtube.com/watch?v=HABFEs90MZ8&t=90s)
- Against the objection that Facebook and Amazon serve billions, he says Facebook began by connecting students at universities and Amazon began by selling books, popular for rare ones you could not find in shops. (Do You Have a Genius Startup Idea at 3:00, https://www.youtube.com/watch?v=HABFEs90MZ8&t=180s)
- He calls asking friends and family whether your idea is good the worst mistake you can make, because they will say it is great and that hurts you. Only the audience you defined counts. (Do You Have a Genius Startup Idea at 3:45, https://www.youtube.com/watch?v=HABFEs90MZ8&t=225s)
- He says to spend as much time as possible where your audience is, such as their subreddits, communities and meetups, and he rates interviews as one of the best practical ways to validate. (Do You Have a Genius Startup Idea at 4:30, https://www.youtube.com/watch?v=HABFEs90MZ8&t=270s)
- He says the first interview mistake is talking about your idea. Talk about their lives and problems and show the prototype only in the last few minutes. (Do You Have a Genius Startup Idea at 5:15, https://www.youtube.com/watch?v=HABFEs90MZ8&t=315s)
- He says the second interview mistake is asking for opinions about the future. Ask about real past behaviour, for example by having people walk you through the last time they did the task. (Do You Have a Genius Startup Idea at 5:15, https://www.youtube.com/watch?v=HABFEs90MZ8&t=315s)
- He offers two ways to test demand without building: a fake door test, which is a landing page for a product that does not exist yet, and a Wizard of Oz test, where the front end is real and you do the work behind it by hand. (Do You Have a Genius Startup Idea at 6:00, https://www.youtube.com/watch?v=HABFEs90MZ8&t=360s)
- His **claw machine** model says starting a startup is like trying to win at an arcade claw machine: you can keep trying until you win, but one way to improve your odds is knowing which item to pick up so it does not slip, which in product building means validating the problem. (Do You Have a Genius Startup Idea at 3:45, https://www.youtube.com/watch?v=HABFEs90MZ8&t=225s)
- He says you probably do not need designers, developers and two mobile apps to solve the problem in its most basic form. He lists Google Sheets, Airtable, Typeform, Bubble and Zapier as enough for a first version. (Do You Have a Genius Startup Idea at 6:45, https://www.youtube.com/watch?v=HABFEs90MZ8&t=405s; Do You Have a Genius Startup Idea at 7:30, https://www.youtube.com/watch?v=HABFEs90MZ8&t=450s)
- Against his own interview advice, he also says that if you can build in a weekend instead of a year, you can skip the interviews, launch and see whether people use it, and try a new startup every week. (Do You Have a Genius Startup Idea at 7:30, https://www.youtube.com/watch?v=HABFEs90MZ8&t=450s)
- Of the Reddit ideas he reviewed, he called the shops-and-influencers app the most difficult to build because it has the classic two-sided platform problem: one side cannot exist without the other. He thinks you would need either a lot of capital to promote it or an existing network of influencers to kick-start that side, and he would pilot it locally, in one city and maybe one category. (Reacting to Innovative Ideas From Reddit at 9:00, https://www.youtube.com/watch?v=82E7VoxCPc0&t=540s)

## What should a first version look like?

- After showing the first versions of famous products such as Amazon's early online-bookstore site, he says these companies all started from something very basic, very simple and sometimes really bad, and that this gives every builder permission to take the first step. (Famous Apps Looked Like This at 6:45, https://www.youtube.com/watch?v=_Sn8Glj5NW4&t=405s)
- He says the users who will take your product to the next level are sometimes already there. Gaming was one niche category on Justin.tv, it grew fast, and the team spun it off as Twitch. (Famous Apps Looked Like This at 0:45, https://www.youtube.com/watch?v=_Sn8Glj5NW4&t=45s)
- He says Spotify beat a free rival, music piracy, by being more convenient: pirating cost nothing but was painful enough that a few dollars a month was the better deal. (Famous Apps Looked Like This at 2:15, https://www.youtube.com/watch?v=_Sn8Glj5NW4&t=135s)
- He says reaching the mainstream means shifting the product away from what early adopters wanted: early users enjoy the technology, while the majority needs it hidden and the value up front. (Famous Apps Looked Like This at 3:45, https://www.youtube.com/watch?v=_Sn8Glj5NW4&t=225s)
- In 2021 he said posting his terrible first videos was a good thing, and backed it with a line he attributes to the founder of LinkedIn: if you are not ashamed of your first version, you launched too late. (How I'm Saving My Dying YouTube Channel at 0:45, https://www.youtube.com/watch?v=PaGAVBBCv6s&t=45s)
- He says a first-time WhatsApp user saw just one thing, messaging, which flattened the learning curve and got people fast to the aha moment where they feel the product's value. (Why Half The World Uses WhatsApp at 4:30, https://www.youtube.com/watch?v=1O1u0UO99NE&t=270s)
- He also records a case against launching fast. He says the playbook is to launch fast and iterate, with GitHub out in 6 months and Canva in a year, while Figma worked in private for 3 years, and that this became a secret of its success. (Why Everyone Is Obsessed With Figma at 9:00, https://www.youtube.com/watch?v=0jze5RbyoW4&t=540s)
- He says making controls digital changed how technology is built: in the past any tech product, software or hardware, was thoroughly planned and tested before release, but today we ship much more and much faster because a bug can be fixed by pushing an update, and that this is also why many newly released products are a buggy mess. (What Happened To Physical Tech at 4:30, https://www.youtube.com/watch?v=TIFF-rinlbo&t=270s)
- He says behind every shipped product there are dozens or hundreds of alternatives that did not make it, and that his own product has hundreds of concepts and designs behind it. (The Ridiculous Engineering Of Notion at 6:45, https://www.youtube.com/watch?v=b00sgRR_Vc0&t=405s)

## Should you listen to users or watch them?

- In 2021 his position was that you can only win by listening to the people who use your product, and that anyone who thinks they already know everything about what they are making is destined to fail. (The Spotify Shuffle Button is a Lie at 3:45, https://www.youtube.com/watch?v=NhAIA-o7f4M&t=225s)
- His 2021 case for listening was Spotify's shuffle: the answer that seems obvious, just randomise it, is not what Spotify does. He plays a clip of another company's boss saying people complained that true random put two songs by the same artist next to each other, and then explains that Spotify spreads each artist's songs across the playlist so it seems random. He says a detail that small can be worth billions at Spotify's scale. (The Spotify Shuffle Button is a Lie at 3:45, https://www.youtube.com/watch?v=NhAIA-o7f4M&t=225s; The Spotify Shuffle Button is a Lie at 4:30, https://www.youtube.com/watch?v=NhAIA-o7f4M&t=270s)
- By 2024 he had reversed the wording and called this the most important and counterintuitive rule in product management: "Don't listen to users but watch them as carefully as possible." (How Your Tech Tricks You at 15:45, https://www.youtube.com/watch?v=7nnyv4yJGJc&t=945s)
- He says Microsoft listened to feature requests until Word was bloated, while Notion and similar tools observed that 95% of the time people use Word for notes and basic to-dos and struggle with sharing and organising. (How Your Tech Tricks You at 15:45, https://www.youtube.com/watch?v=7nnyv4yJGJc&t=945s)
- He says users are bad at innovating but already struggle with the problems the next billion-dollar company will solve. His examples of features found by observation are Gmail's undo send and iOS filling in SMS codes. (How Your Tech Tricks You at 16:30, https://www.youtube.com/watch?v=7nnyv4yJGJc&t=990s)
- In 2026 he repeated it as the golden rule of product management, never listen to your users, and applied it to people who want old YouTube back: the features they dislike are there because they work. (The Ridiculous Engineering Of YouTube Videos at 3:45, https://www.youtube.com/watch?v=giP1iIbSVh0&t=225s)
- In June 2025, with the Notion Mail team, he said the advice to talk to users is true but building for yourself looks like an even better way. (The Ridiculous Engineering Of Notion at 9:00, https://www.youtube.com/watch?v=b00sgRR_Vc0&t=540s)
- He says the hardest thing about building tech is not the clever hack or the complex build. It is humans and their weird psychology, the most unpredictable part. (How Your Tech Tricks You at 7:30, https://www.youtube.com/watch?v=7nnyv4yJGJc&t=450s)
- He says good design is studying how people really use the product. One-handed zoom in Google Maps exists because people on bikes cannot pinch, and many people preview a route without ever pressing start. (The Most Dangerous App That Everyone Loves at 6:45, https://www.youtube.com/watch?v=P2qOiKA56tI&t=405s; The Most Dangerous App That Everyone Loves at 7:30, https://www.youtube.com/watch?v=P2qOiKA56tI&t=450s)

## How do you decide what to build and what to refuse?

- He says a new feature has to pass three questions: do people want it, can we do it, and should we do it, meaning does it fit the product strategy or company vision. (I Redesigned YouTube's Worst New Feature at 0:00, https://www.youtube.com/watch?v=ELoNeW7GKog&t=0s)
- He says the best products are built by saying no. Once things go well, users ask for more features, views and customisation, and that is how tools get complicated. (The Ridiculous Engineering Of Figma at 12:45, https://www.youtube.com/watch?v=5t8dh3DSdBk&t=765s)
- He says the biggest problem all professional tools face is that each starts simple, advanced users ask for advanced features, and basic and advanced get mixed into a scary interface; he calls the result cognitive overload. Letting users rearrange panels only moves the complexity around. (Why Everyone Is Obsessed With Figma at 0:45, https://www.youtube.com/watch?v=0jze5RbyoW4&t=45s; Why Everyone Is Obsessed With Figma at 2:15, https://www.youtube.com/watch?v=0jze5RbyoW4&t=135s)
- He says Figma solved cognitive overload in professional software with progressive disclosure: basic options show by default and an advanced one is disclosed only in the context where it is needed. He says this is extremely hard to design correctly. (Why Everyone Is Obsessed With Figma at 3:00, https://www.youtube.com/watch?v=0jze5RbyoW4&t=180s; Why Everyone Is Obsessed With Figma at 3:45, https://www.youtube.com/watch?v=0jze5RbyoW4&t=225s)
- He says the real genius of Figma was being lazy: when people asked for very advanced features, it let the community build them as plugins instead of cluttering the app. (Why Everyone Is Obsessed With Figma at 3:45, https://www.youtube.com/watch?v=0jze5RbyoW4&t=225s)
- He says that when competitors keep adding features, focusing on the basics is the most difficult thing you can do. In 2012 rivals added games, calls and social features while WhatsApp stayed a chat app. (Why Half The World Uses WhatsApp at 3:45, https://www.youtube.com/watch?v=1O1u0UO99NE&t=225s)
- He says obvious features can hide so much complexity that even Google and Apple pass on them. His method is to build the feature in versions in his head, as he did with an unsafe areas layer for Maps, and watch what breaks each time. (Why Google Maps Doesn't Show You Unsafe Areas at 6:00, https://www.youtube.com/watch?v=4i66ht6YHkw&t=360s; Why Google Maps Doesn't Show You Unsafe Areas at 0:45, https://www.youtube.com/watch?v=4i66ht6YHkw&t=45s)
- His **1% dilemma** says any product reaches 99% of use cases fairly easily, while the last 1% of edge cases takes exponential resources, and the more often a product is used, the more often users hit that 1%. (The Most Dangerous App That Everyone Loves at 1:30, https://www.youtube.com/watch?v=P2qOiKA56tI&t=90s)
- He describes how tech products are built as a loop: keep a list of feature ideas, score each against the team's objective and key result, build, A/B test on half the users, and ship what moves the metric. (The Real Reason Your Tech Makes You Unhappy at 1:30, https://www.youtube.com/watch?v=Vbg5TeoLTW0&t=90s)
- He says the loop is not the problem. What decides whether a product delivers value or addiction is the objective and how it is measured, and engagement took over because it is the easiest thing to measure. (The Real Reason Your Tech Makes You Unhappy at 2:15, https://www.youtube.com/watch?v=Vbg5TeoLTW0&t=135s; The Real Reason Your Tech Makes You Unhappy at 6:45, https://www.youtube.com/watch?v=Vbg5TeoLTW0&t=405s)
- His **law of the marginal user** says products get worse as they get popular because companies stop building for the people who love them and optimise for the next user they have not acquired yet. (Why Tech Gets Terrible When It Gets Popular at 0:00, https://www.youtube.com/watch?v=BQLVgmhgtpQ&t=0s; Why Tech Gets Terrible When It Gets Popular at 9:00, https://www.youtube.com/watch?v=BQLVgmhgtpQ&t=540s)
- He says A/B tests are the mechanism behind the marginal user problem: a feature the loyal users begged for confuses newcomers, lowers engagement and gets buried in a menu, so people who do not care end up deciding for people who do. (Why Tech Gets Terrible When It Gets Popular at 7:30, https://www.youtube.com/watch?v=BQLVgmhgtpQ&t=450s)
- In May 2024 he said chatbots were bolted onto everything because they are the easiest way to add AI to an app, and that the fix is to start from a real problem and apply AI inside the interface people already know. (AI Has A Huge Problem at 3:00, https://www.youtube.com/watch?v=CY_b8w8u9NY&t=180s; AI Has A Huge Problem at 12:00, https://www.youtube.com/watch?v=CY_b8w8u9NY&t=720s)

## Do you need venture money and a big team?

- He says the garage, the millions raised and the billionaire founder are not the only way tech works, and that the other side has more interesting stories than the next startup raising $100 million. (The Weird Rise Of Anti-Startups at 0:00, https://www.youtube.com/watch?v=xL1MOOD5Ox8&t=0s)
- He says Craigslist shows the power of keeping things simple: its founder took no big funding, added no features and never redesigned, and the site gets 6.2 billion page views a month and makes $500 million to $1 billion a year. (The Weird Rise Of Anti-Startups at 2:15, https://www.youtube.com/watch?v=xL1MOOD5Ox8&t=135s)
- He says Silicon Valley hates linear growth. Since 90% of startups fail, investors need the survivors to return 50, 100 or 1,000 times, so a chart that would thrill a normal company disappoints them. (The Weird Rise Of Anti-Startups at 3:45, https://www.youtube.com/watch?v=xL1MOOD5Ox8&t=225s)
- He says Gumroad's founder kept a startup that had failed by those standards, shrank the team from 20 to five to himself, and the growth chart does not even show the moment everyone left. (The Weird Rise Of Anti-Startups at 4:30, https://www.youtube.com/watch?v=xL1MOOD5Ox8&t=270s; The Weird Rise Of Anti-Startups at 5:15, https://www.youtube.com/watch?v=xL1MOOD5Ox8&t=315s)
- His lesson from Gumroad is that freedom and full control can matter more than more resources. (The Weird Rise Of Anti-Startups at 6:00, https://www.youtube.com/watch?v=xL1MOOD5Ox8&t=360s)
- In February 2024 he said Midjourney had fewer than 40 employees, no outside investment and $200 million a year in revenue, and was completely profitable, less than three years after it was founded. (The Weird Rise Of Anti-Startups at 6:00, https://www.youtube.com/watch?v=xL1MOOD5Ox8&t=360s)
- He says running Midjourney on Discord was forced by a lack of resources and became one of its greatest assets: feedback was instant, and seeing what others generate was the best tutorial. (The Weird Rise Of Anti-Startups at 9:00, https://www.youtube.com/watch?v=xL1MOOD5Ox8&t=540s)
- He draws a rule for builders from Midjourney: "By being limited, your limitations become your biggest asset." (The Weird Rise Of Anti-Startups at 9:45, https://www.youtube.com/watch?v=xL1MOOD5Ox8&t=585s)
- He describes indie hackers as people who want freedom, decent money and a good life and not a unicorn. They build small profitable products for specific problems, sometimes dozens at once, with no employees. (The Weird Rise Of Anti-Startups at 9:45, https://www.youtube.com/watch?v=xL1MOOD5Ox8&t=585s)
- He lists how indie hackers ship: stick to the basics, skip fancy design and most marketing, do everything yourself, recycle code between projects and launch often. (The Weird Rise Of Anti-Startups at 10:30, https://www.youtube.com/watch?v=xL1MOOD5Ox8&t=630s)
- He says the key idea of indie hacking is building in public: document the journey as you go, share revenue numbers and share failures as well as wins. (The Weird Rise Of Anti-Startups at 11:15, https://www.youtube.com/watch?v=xL1MOOD5Ox8&t=675s)
- In February 2024 he said venture funding peaked at the end of 2021 and that building something big without asking anyone for money, and keeping full control of it, can sometimes be your secret weapon. He added that funded companies will still happen. (The Weird Rise Of Anti-Startups at 12:00, https://www.youtube.com/watch?v=xL1MOOD5Ox8&t=720s)
- He says there is always a way to start beside your main job, and that a tech company does not need $10 million of venture capital: learn to code and build the first version yourself. (I Quit My 6-Figures Dream Job In Tech at 11:15, https://www.youtube.com/watch?v=UbFufWfJPZ8&t=675s)
- His **ramen period** is the stretch where a stable job out-earns your own project. He says to shrink it by building the project beside the job, as he did with his channel for three years. (I Quit My 6-Figures Dream Job In Tech at 9:45, https://www.youtube.com/watch?v=UbFufWfJPZ8&t=585s; I Quit My 6-Figures Dream Job In Tech at 10:30, https://www.youtube.com/watch?v=UbFufWfJPZ8&t=630s)
- After visiting Lovable in 2025 he said small, very successful teams work because they attract people who really want to be there, and that the people inside are regular people with a mission who work hard and have fun. (Behind The Scenes Of Lovable's Insane Design at 21:00, https://www.youtube.com/watch?v=ZoVLS-Wxr1s&t=1260s)

## Why are fun, craft and imperfection an edge?

- He says some of the biggest products in history came from unplanned fun. In his telling Gmail began as an engineer's side idea in 2001, and Google's 20% time produced Gmail, Google News and AdSense. (How Silicon Valley Killed Fun at 0:45, https://www.youtube.com/watch?v=DYdLq9icgrI&t=45s)
- He says those products were not designed by focus groups or validated by A/B tests. They were built by people who thought it would be fun to make them. (How Silicon Valley Killed Fun at 2:15, https://www.youtube.com/watch?v=DYdLq9icgrI&t=135s)
- He splits tech people into creators, who build things because it is fun, and operators (MBAs, management consultants, managers of managers), who build by testing numbers. He says that around 2011 to 2013 tech stopped being the era of creators and became the era of operators. (How Silicon Valley Killed Fun at 4:30, https://www.youtube.com/watch?v=DYdLq9icgrI&t=270s)
- He says usability testing and A/B testing strip out personality, because under that method textures, quirks and Easter eggs are just noise. His example is Spotify replacing the heart with a plus that is clearer and soulless. (How Silicon Valley Killed Fun at 5:15, https://www.youtube.com/watch?v=DYdLq9icgrI&t=315s; How Silicon Valley Killed Fun at 6:00, https://www.youtube.com/watch?v=DYdLq9icgrI&t=360s)
- He grants that boring is profitable, and that Apple made much more money in Tim Cook's operator era than in Steve Jobs's creator era. (How Silicon Valley Killed Fun at 9:00, https://www.youtube.com/watch?v=DYdLq9icgrI&t=540s)
- He also gives a case where building for fun backfired: Gmail's 2016 April Fools button sat next to send, millions clicked it by accident, and people missed important emails. (How Silicon Valley Killed Fun at 8:15, https://www.youtube.com/watch?v=DYdLq9icgrI&t=495s)
- In November 2025 he said the pendulum swung back to creators when ChatGPT launched on November 30th, 2022, and that Google's Bard was the product of the operator era. (How Silicon Valley Killed Fun at 12:00, https://www.youtube.com/watch?v=DYdLq9icgrI&t=720s; How Silicon Valley Killed Fun at 9:45, https://www.youtube.com/watch?v=DYdLq9icgrI&t=585s)
- He says fun features are worth shipping even when they move no metric. Of YouTube's topic-based like animations he says they are not necessary and will not lift engagement, and they shipped because they are fun. (How Silicon Valley Killed Fun at 15:45, https://www.youtube.com/watch?v=DYdLq9icgrI&t=945s)
- He says he spent 5 hours redesigning an effect on Flask's home page only because it was cool, and that the people tech needs now ask what would be cool, not what the return is. (How Silicon Valley Killed Fun at 16:30, https://www.youtube.com/watch?v=DYdLq9icgrI&t=990s)
- In July 2024 he said that because making functional software keeps getting easier, beauty and craft are now a way to stand out from competitors. (What Happened To User Interfaces at 10:30, https://www.youtube.com/watch?v=HdozqSVF-VM&t=630s)
- He agrees that no single detail matters on its own. A hundred or a thousand of them, each with hours of work, are what make a product feel good. (The Ridiculous Engineering Of Notion at 16:30, https://www.youtube.com/watch?v=b00sgRR_Vc0&t=990s)
- His **commoditized perfection** argument, from October 2024: technology made flawless output cheap, so everyone has it, and imperfection became the scarce sign that a human made something. (Why Ugly Things Are Cool Now at 3:00, https://www.youtube.com/watch?v=q1SWw7i9LQc&t=180s; Why Ugly Things Are Cool Now at 9:45, https://www.youtube.com/watch?v=q1SWw7i9LQc&t=585s)
- His proof that imperfect beats polished is that Casey Neistat's grainy 720p Nike ad has 32 million views against Nike's polished commercials, and that fully AI-generated videos fail because nothing is wrong with them. (Why Ugly Things Are Cool Now at 0:00, https://www.youtube.com/watch?v=q1SWw7i9LQc&t=0s; Why Ugly Things Are Cool Now at 5:15, https://www.youtube.com/watch?v=q1SWw7i9LQc&t=315s)

## What do games teach product builders?

- In 2026 he argued that all products are secretly games: "Game design and product design is actually the same thing." (The Weird Way Tech Steals From Games at 0:45, https://www.youtube.com/watch?v=GypAKofWGBk&t=45s)
- He says **real gamification** is building the whole product to feel like a game, not adding mascots, points, goals or levels. (The Weird Way Tech Steals From Games at 5:15, https://www.youtube.com/watch?v=GypAKofWGBk&t=315s)
- He says the best tutorial is one players do not notice. The first level of Plants vs. Zombies shows no instructions, and its designers rebalanced the game so the sunflower is the only choice at the start. (The Weird Way Tech Steals From Games at 1:30, https://www.youtube.com/watch?v=GypAKofWGBk&t=90s)
- His two tutorial rules are to speak only when the user is stuck or doing something wrong, as Gmail does when you write attached and attach nothing, and to use visuals instead of text. (The Weird Way Tech Steals From Games at 3:00, https://www.youtube.com/watch?v=GypAKofWGBk&t=180s)
- From the book Game Feel he takes a loop of context, input, response, metaphor, rules and polish, and says you can build any game and any product with it. (The Weird Way Tech Steals From Games at 3:45, https://www.youtube.com/watch?v=GypAKofWGBk&t=225s)
- He says what game developers call juice, tech people call craft: colour, easing, sound, particles and screen shake make the same game feel good. (The Weird Way Tech Steals From Games at 6:00, https://www.youtube.com/watch?v=GypAKofWGBk&t=360s)
- He cites a law (captioned "Borel's law") that all the best games are easy to learn and difficult to master, and dates it to Pong: Computer Space flopped because nobody understood its four buttons, so the next game had a single gameplay instruction printed on the cabinet. (The Weird Way Tech Steals From Games at 8:15, https://www.youtube.com/watch?v=GypAKofWGBk&t=495s)
- He says great products reveal themselves step by step like Civilization, which starts you with one settler and about three possible actions. His fix for Arc's crowded first screen was the same: reveal features one at a time. (The Weird Way Tech Steals From Games at 9:00, https://www.youtube.com/watch?v=GypAKofWGBk&t=540s; Why Everyone Is Obsessed With Arc Browser at 3:45, https://www.youtube.com/watch?v=UdopamaLJC8&t=225s)
- He says the hard part of multiplayer is predicting behaviour, not the technology. Journey's designers built physics so players could help each other, and players used it to push each other to their deaths. (The Weird Way Tech Steals From Games at 9:45, https://www.youtube.com/watch?v=GypAKofWGBk&t=585s)
- He says products sell at the moment of loss, as games do: Google Meet ends the meeting and offers the upgrade a few minutes before, and Temple Run sells a continue the second you lose. (The Weird Way Tech Steals From Games at 12:45, https://www.youtube.com/watch?v=GypAKofWGBk&t=765s)
- In April 2026 he said Claude Code hooked people because, by accident, it follows principles from The Art of Game Design: a short feedback loop, juiciness, visible progress and chance. (Why Everyone is Suddenly Obsessed With Claude Code at 1:30, https://www.youtube.com/watch?v=gzt52Trk9w0&t=90s; Why Everyone is Suddenly Obsessed With Claude Code at 4:30, https://www.youtube.com/watch?v=gzt52Trk9w0&t=270s)

## Why does broken tech stay broken?

- In February 2026 he gave four reasons annoying tech stays broken: standards, chasing the next big thing, backwards compatibility, and users' own resistance to change. He argued this is mostly a good thing. (The Weird Reason Your Tech Stays Broken at 11:15, https://www.youtube.com/watch?v=-51ocAD6zBI&t=675s; The Weird Reason Your Tech Stays Broken at 0:00, https://www.youtube.com/watch?v=-51ocAD6zBI&t=0s)
- His first reason is "Being standardized always beats being cutting-edge." He says better technology does not win and never has. (The Weird Reason Your Tech Stays Broken at 2:15, https://www.youtube.com/watch?v=-51ocAD6zBI&t=135s)
- His proof that the standard beats the better technology is Betamax against VHS: he says Betamax had better picture and sound, VHS recorded 2 hours instead of 1 and was an open standard anyone could make, and by 1987 VHS had 90% of the market. (The Weird Reason Your Tech Stays Broken at 2:15, https://www.youtube.com/watch?v=-51ocAD6zBI&t=135s)
- His second reason is that tech companies neglect current products to chase the next one. He says Apple spent an estimated $20 billion on the Vision Pro while Siri stayed bad, and calls that the cost of being a tech company, not incompetence. (The Weird Reason Your Tech Stays Broken at 3:45, https://www.youtube.com/watch?v=-51ocAD6zBI&t=225s; The Weird Reason Your Tech Stays Broken at 7:30, https://www.youtube.com/watch?v=-51ocAD6zBI&t=450s)
- He says the instinct to just fix the list of user complaints is wrong, and that this is why most people are not the chief product officer of Apple or Google. (The Weird Reason Your Tech Stays Broken at 5:15, https://www.youtube.com/watch?v=-51ocAD6zBI&t=315s)
- He says products we use daily looked like distractions at first: Google Maps came from two startups bought in 2004 while search was stretched thin, and Discord began as the chat of a failed game. (The Weird Reason Your Tech Stays Broken at 6:00, https://www.youtube.com/watch?v=-51ocAD6zBI&t=360s)
- His third reason is backwards compatibility. He says Windows keeps both Settings and Control Panel because millions of scripts and IT workflows depend on Control Panel. (The Weird Reason Your Tech Stays Broken at 9:45, https://www.youtube.com/watch?v=-51ocAD6zBI&t=585s)
- He says any clean replacement for Word would rebuild Word: start with simple Markdown, add what courts need, then what law firms need, and feature by feature you have the same bloated product. (The Weird Reason Your Tech Stays Broken at 10:30, https://www.youtube.com/watch?v=-51ocAD6zBI&t=630s)
- He sums this up as "The bigger something gets, the more dangerous it is to touch." He says touching it is almost always worse than leaving it alone. (The Weird Reason Your Tech Stays Broken at 11:15, https://www.youtube.com/watch?v=-51ocAD6zBI&t=675s)
- His fourth reason is the user. He says QWERTY dates from the 1870s and Dvorak's 1932 layout still lost, because throwing away years of learning hurts more than the gain. (The Weird Reason Your Tech Stays Broken at 12:00, https://www.youtube.com/watch?v=-51ocAD6zBI&t=720s)
- He says people fight to keep what they know: Snapchat's 2018 redesign drew a petition with 1.2 million signatures in a week and a 7.2% drop in the stock in a day, and in his view the redesign was not wrong. (The Weird Reason Your Tech Stays Broken at 12:45, https://www.youtube.com/watch?v=-51ocAD6zBI&t=765s)
- He says users are participants and not victims: we complain about what does not work and then reject attempts at something new. (The Weird Reason Your Tech Stays Broken at 13:30, https://www.youtube.com/watch?v=-51ocAD6zBI&t=810s)
