# Design Thinking part 3 of 3, authority bias and the five minute pitch

This is part 3 of 3 of a design thinking class recorded on 14 May 2025. The instructor is not named in this slice. The slice holds only one short stretch of the class: the end of his point on authority bias in customer interviews, a student question about investors, and his story about pitching a senior leader in five minutes. The transcription tool then repeated that stretch until the file ended, so nothing later in the class was captured.

## What was said

### Authority bias in interviews

- The instructor describes a senior person who answers a new idea with "I have been in this company 15 years, I know how this works". He says that person is showing authority, not listening to the idea.
- He says someone's 15 years in a company does not mean your idea has no merit.
- His advice on an opinion from someone in authority is to consider it and not discount it, but not to assume it is 100% right.
- He says data from other customers can show that the world does not work the way the person in authority says it does.
- He says a founder should not assume someone is right just because they are in authority, and should take their view for what it is worth.
- He tells founders to take what an authority figure says as data and validate it against the other data points they have.
- He says the important thing is to interview multiple people and not rely on one person.

### Incumbents and newcomers can both be right

- He says he works closely with automobile companies, and names TVS and Ather.
- He contrasts TVS, which he says has been in the automobile industry for more than 100 years, with Ather, which he says has been in it for less than 8 years. Asked which of the two is right, he says both are.
- He says that TVS saying something does not mean the automobile world will move that way.
- He says TVS did not predict electric vehicles and should have entered them three years earlier.

### Investors and short attention

- A student asks how to counter this with an investor who is giving you money. The instructor says investors are a separate topic that someone else will cover with the class.
- He says investors often have little patience, so a founder must find a way to convey the most important thing in the time the investor has.
- He says investors listen to a few words and assume the rest.
- For a VC he lists reaching them through a trusted network and giving them what they want in two minutes.
- He says a cold approach to a VC has a very low chance of winning ten minutes of attention, while an introduction through someone the VC knows has a high chance of winning eight minutes.
- He says the investor case is a communication strategy question, not authority bias. It is about how you communicate your value.

### The five minute pitch

- He tells of leading a division that incubated emerging products inside a large technology company, where he needed a 50 million dollar investment from a senior leader whose time was impossible to get.
- The leader's assistant gave him five minutes: he had to walk with the leader between two campuses and make his case on the way.
- He says the leader was not convinced and gave him 10 million dollars, not the 50 million he asked for.
- His point from the story is that he had to say everything he needed in those five minutes, and he calls doing so "a really important art".
- He says that if you cannot explain what you are building in three sentences, you need to clarify what you are building.
- He says top executives always ask for it in three sentences, and treat a person who cannot do that as someone who does not know what they are doing.

## Examples and cases used

- TVS and Ather: an old automobile company and a young one think differently and both can be right, so an incumbent's view is one data point and not the answer.
- TVS and electric vehicles: his example that long experience in an industry did not let a company predict the shift to electric vehicles.
- His own five minute walk with a senior leader: an ask for 50 million dollars had to be made in five minutes and came back as 10 million.

## Numbers given

- More than 100 years: how long he says TVS has been in the automobile industry.
- Less than 8 years: how long he says Ather has been in the automobile industry.
- Three years: how much earlier he says TVS should have entered electric vehicles.
- 50 million dollars asked and 10 million dollars given: his own internal funding request for an incubated product division.
- Five minutes: the time he was given to make that request.
- Two minutes: the time in which he says a VC must get what they want.
- Ten minutes and eight minutes: his contrast between a cold approach to a VC and an approach through someone the VC knows.
- Three sentences: the length in which he says a founder must be able to explain what they are building.
