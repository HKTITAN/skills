# Digital ad ratios and the branding S-curve, Amardeep Bajpai

This is the afternoon part of day one of Amardeep Bajpai's growth hacker bootcamp. He picked up his closing line from the morning, that marketing is measurable but not replicable or scalable, and explained it through the ratios inside a Google search campaign. He then gave ways to make growth steadier and described how brand campaigns produce an S-curve.

## What was said

### Why marketing is hard to predict

- Bajpai says demand is among the hardest things in business to predict, unlike manufacturing or delivery times, which can be planned.
- He says many e-commerce businesses built their plans on a fixed customer acquisition cost, and that this assumption is fundamentally wrong.
- He says acquisition cost will change from day to day, that the swings stay small only when volumes are very low, and that they become large at even fairly high volumes.
- He says markets are never static, so copying a model that worked once does not reproduce its result.
- He says people want predictable scale because it looks good on a spreadsheet, and a founder who raises money on a fixed acquisition cost starts losing money rapidly when the cost does not hold.
- He says a planned acquisition cost of 50 rupees can become 500 rupees when a few heavily funded competitors enter and outspend everyone, because they are playing to create a monopoly and can lose money for a long time.

### The ratios inside a search campaign

- Bajpai says the first ratio is click-through rate, the share of people who saw the ad and clicked, and it decides how many visitors reach the product page.
- He says Google's algorithm gives ads with a higher click-through rate a lower cost per click, so a better rate also lowers acquisition cost.
- He says cost per click is set by a second-price auction on the keywords the advertiser bids for.
- He says a high quality score lets the advertiser lower the bid.
- He says the second key ratio is conversion rate, the share of visitors who buy, and it is the product of the ratios at every step of the funnel from landing page to payment.
- He says bounce rate, people leaving the page within a few seconds of landing, is the first of those funnel ratios, and a high bounce rate raises cost.
- He gives under 30% as the bounce rate to aim for and says under 50% is decent in some cases, depending on the industry and on whether the ad runs on exact match or on broad or phrase match.
- He says a landing page can either show several products to choose from or take the visitor straight to one product and a buy button with fewer clicks.
- He says that because click-through, bounce, conversion and page-to-page funnel rates all move every day, the final cost per conversion moves every day.
- He says the Google search example will lose relevance because AI search is rising and classic search is declining, though search is still the dominant model for now.

### Ways to make growth steadier

- Bajpai says the first is to find where the customer spends most of their time, which keeps changing, and to target them there, whether that is a cafe or Instagram.
- He says to split the customer's time into buckets and then work out how to influence them in each one.
- He says the second is to treat digital marketing like day trading and branding like long-term investing in fundamentals: most day traders do not make much money, and long-term investors have a far higher chance.
- He says both marketing and stock markets are heavily shaped by human behaviour and daily choices.
- He says to put some time, effort and money into branding and to start early, because depending only on acquisition cost gets harder and harder.
- He says the growth plan should be non-linear and made of many elements, so each element can be studied to see which part to reduce.

### The S-curve of a brand campaign

- Bajpai says a good branding campaign produces an S-curve: visits, footfall or inquiries rise after the campaign and later come back down.
- He says companies space out campaigns, letting the S-curve play out for about two months and launching the next one before the numbers fall back to the start.
- He says large consumer goods companies keep measuring the curve and act when inquiries start to come down.
- He says more spend does not raise inquiries without limit, and bombarding the audience can turn negative, to the point that buyers pick any other brand.
- He says that if the market stays flat even at very high gross rating points, the product is not good enough and needs another look.
- He says some products need no new brand campaign for a long time because people already know them and there are few alternatives at the point of purchase.
- He says content has to be either educational or entertaining.
- He says influencer marketing is paid video, and in that sense it is the equivalent of a Google ad.
- He says repeat purchase is a good measure of whether the product is working.

## Examples and cases used

- A brass cup sold through Google search ads: his worked example for click-through rate, conversion rate and bounce rate.
- A consumer goods company in Kerala run by a friend: it stayed in a single state, spent very little on Google and Facebook ads, and relied on branding.
- Hindustan Unilever and Procter and Gamble: companies that measure the S-curve and time campaigns by it.

## Numbers given

- 50 rupees rising to 500 rupees: Bajpai's illustration of how far a planned customer acquisition cost can move when funded competitors outspend you.
- Under 30%: the bounce rate he says to aim for, with under 50% acceptable in some industries.
- About two months: how long he says companies let a brand campaign's S-curve run before the next campaign.
