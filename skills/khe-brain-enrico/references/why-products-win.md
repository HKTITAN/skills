# Why products win

This theme is his habit of taking one product apart and naming the few moves that made it win or lose, usually a psychology principle, a product decision and a technical foundation. It comes mostly from the obsessed-with videos on Notion, Figma, Arc and Claude Code, the engineering videos made with the Notion and Figma teams, and the single-product videos on WhatsApp, Google Maps, Spotify, Airbnb, YouTube, the AI Pin and Olivetti. Product facts and figures are his research as of each video's date, verdicts on new products carry their year, and the Lovable video was made in partnership with Lovable.

## What does he think separates products that win from products that lose?

- A big vision only wins when every level under it is done well. He says General Magic had the same touchscreen smartphone vision as the iPhone and flopped because it lacked the levels below. (The 7 Levels of Tech Design at 15:45, https://www.youtube.com/watch?v=FQVRfadJkJk&t=945s)
- Great products start from the experience and then find the technology, and the technology that matters is the kind that disappears. His case is Spotify, which he says made songs start instantly by rebuilding its delivery as peer-to-peer after buying uTorrent to get its engineer. (The 7 Levels of Tech Design at 7:30, https://www.youtube.com/watch?v=FQVRfadJkJk&t=450s; The 7 Levels of Tech Design at 6:45, https://www.youtube.com/watch?v=FQVRfadJkJk&t=405s)
- One good structural decision in a product's logic makes everything after it simpler. He says Notion decided everything is a page, Slack decided everything is a channel, and in his own product Flask he made everything a tag. (The 7 Levels of Tech Design at 13:30, https://www.youtube.com/watch?v=FQVRfadJkJk&t=810s; The 7 Levels of Tech Design at 12:00, https://www.youtube.com/watch?v=FQVRfadJkJk&t=720s)
- Reframing what a thing is for can build a company with no new technology. He says Loom reframed video as a replacement for meetings and was bought by Atlassian for $975 million, and Superhuman reframed email as the fastest email and charges $30 a month. (The 7 Levels of Tech Design at 14:15, https://www.youtube.com/watch?v=FQVRfadJkJk&t=855s)
- Every device is defined by its **core input**, the one interaction everything else builds on. He says that once you understand it you can predict which future products will succeed and which will fail before they even launch. (The Most Important Tiny Decision In Tech History at 0:45, https://www.youtube.com/watch?v=7EVZpW9Rdwk&t=45s; The Most Important Tiny Decision In Tech History at 10:30, https://www.youtube.com/watch?v=7EVZpW9Rdwk&t=630s)
- He says smartphones won because scrolling comes first and tapping second. The phone waits before registering a tap, 100 milliseconds on Android with 8 dp of finger travel before a touch counts as a scroll, and about 150 milliseconds on the iPhone. (The Most Important Tiny Decision In Tech History at 4:30, https://www.youtube.com/watch?v=7EVZpW9Rdwk&t=270s; The Most Important Tiny Decision In Tech History at 5:15, https://www.youtube.com/watch?v=7EVZpW9Rdwk&t=315s)
- A new interface has to be easier than the one before it, and he says that has been true of every successful one. Touchscreens won because tapping was easier than buttons and keyboards. (We Need To Talk About The AI Pin at 4:30, https://www.youtube.com/watch?v=E2-L-Gv0wr0&t=270s)
- His **1% dilemma**: any product reaches 80, 90 or 99% of use cases fairly easily, but the last 1% of weird edge cases takes an exponential amount of resources, and frequent users hit it sooner or later. (The Most Dangerous App That Everyone Loves at 1:30, https://www.youtube.com/watch?v=P2qOiKA56tI&t=90s)
- Most popular products began as something specialized for a specialized group. He names Levi's jeans built for miners, Airbnb as an air bed and breakfast for a 2007 design conference, and the Rolex Daytona made for 1950s racers. (Why Tech Gets Terrible When It Gets Popular at 0:45, https://www.youtube.com/watch?v=BQLVgmhgtpQ&t=45s)
- Trying to skip straight to the mass audience he calls the **love zone** is almost always a disaster. He says CNN spent $300 million on CNN Plus in 2022 and got 10,000 users, and Apple managed it with AirPods only because they were an iPhone accessory. (Why Tech Gets Terrible When It Gets Popular at 3:45, https://www.youtube.com/watch?v=BQLVgmhgtpQ&t=225s)
- He says the iPod became a smash hit only once it was opened up: after iTunes for Windows, Apple sold 4.4 million iPods the next year. His lesson is that a great product limited to a small group does not sell and an open, compatible one does. (How Tech Secretly Killed Choice at 0:45, https://www.youtube.com/watch?v=UdzT1YxeZsY&t=45s)
- The products we use every day often looked like distractions at the start. He says Google Maps exists because Google bought two startups in 2004 while stretched thin on search, and Discord began as a failed video game whose chat outlived it. (The Weird Reason Your Tech Stays Broken at 6:00, https://www.youtube.com/watch?v=-51ocAD6zBI&t=360s)

## Why does he say people are obsessed with Notion?

- Notion broke the startup rule of winning one niche first and still worked. He says it is a note app, a to-do list, a CRM, a project tool and a collaboration space, and "It's simply not great at any of those things." (Why Everyone Is Obsessed With Notion at 0:45, https://www.youtube.com/watch?v=aWGviOMQqSw&t=45s)
- He says Notion set out to replace Word, Evernote, Todoist and Asana and avoided feature bloat by keeping only what was absolutely needed: no A4 pages because people do not print, no extra fonts, only 10 colors. (Why Everyone Is Obsessed With Notion at 1:30, https://www.youtube.com/watch?v=aWGviOMQqSw&t=90s)
- The **IKEA effect** is his main reason for the obsession. People give far higher value to what they build themselves, so the template and database you spent hours on beats a normal to-do app that is not truly yours. (Why Everyone Is Obsessed With Notion at 2:15, https://www.youtube.com/watch?v=aWGviOMQqSw&t=135s)
- Building the system is the peak moment, like assembling Lego. He calls it the **paradox of Notion** that you spend more time tweaking the system than being productive, and he says that is not a bad thing because it is fulfilling. (Why Everyone Is Obsessed With Notion at 3:00, https://www.youtube.com/watch?v=aWGviOMQqSw&t=180s)
- He uses Maslow's hammer, the bias Abraham Maslow made famous in 1966, to explain why people run every part of life in a Notion database even when it is the wrong tool. He adds that it took him months to see Notion was bad for his to-dos, and he moved them to TickTick. (Why Everyone Is Obsessed With Notion at 3:45, https://www.youtube.com/watch?v=aWGviOMQqSw&t=225s)
- Notion built a **meta game** that works as free marketing: tens of thousands of YouTube videos about setups, thousands of people selling templates and whole companies built around it. (Why Everyone Is Obsessed With Notion at 4:30, https://www.youtube.com/watch?v=aWGviOMQqSw&t=270s)
- Notion gets into companies through product-led growth, not salespeople. People use it free for themselves, then want it at the startup they found or join. (Why Everyone Is Obsessed With Notion at 5:15, https://www.youtube.com/watch?v=aWGviOMQqSw&t=315s)
- He says Notion started in 2015, when its two co-founders shut themselves in a room in Kyoto and worked 18 hours a day in Figma, and it was valued at $10 billion in January 2023. (Why Everyone Is Obsessed With Notion at 0:00, https://www.youtube.com/watch?v=aWGviOMQqSw&t=0s; Why Everyone Is Obsessed With Notion at 1:30, https://www.youtube.com/watch?v=aWGviOMQqSw&t=90s)
- In 2025 he said Notion did not start from one user problem but from the elements common to all tools, such as databases, lists, board views and links, and exposed them as Lego blocks from which many specialized apps can be built. (The Ridiculous Engineering Of Notion at 2:15, https://www.youtube.com/watch?v=b00sgRR_Vc0&t=135s)
- The same IKEA effect has a cost: because every user builds a different system, users want completely different features and deciding what to build next gets very hard. (The Ridiculous Engineering Of Notion at 4:30, https://www.youtube.com/watch?v=b00sgRR_Vc0&t=270s)
- As of June 2025 he said Notion and Notion Mail had so far avoided getting worse as they got popular. (The Ridiculous Engineering Of Notion at 21:00, https://www.youtube.com/watch?v=b00sgRR_Vc0&t=1260s)

## Why does he say Figma took over design?

- In 2025 he said he had never seen a piece of software completely take over an industry so quickly, and that three key psychological principles sit behind Figma's design. (Why Everyone Is Obsessed With Figma at 0:00, https://www.youtube.com/watch?v=0jze5RbyoW4&t=0s)
- The biggest problem of every professional tool is feature creep into a scary interface, where basic and advanced options get mixed. His examples are After Effects and the Photoshop Edit menu, where copy and paste sit next to puppet warp. (Why Everyone Is Obsessed With Figma at 0:45, https://www.youtube.com/watch?v=0jze5RbyoW4&t=45s)
- Progressive disclosure is what he calls the secret of Figma's success. Rectangle, circle and text are up front, while the corner smoothing that iOS icons use appears only inside the rounded-corners panel and goes away when you deselect. (Why Everyone Is Obsessed With Figma at 3:00, https://www.youtube.com/watch?v=0jze5RbyoW4&t=180s)
- "The true genius of Figma is that they were lazy." When users asked for very advanced features, he says Figma let the community build them as plugins instead of cluttering the app. (Why Everyone Is Obsessed With Figma at 3:45, https://www.youtube.com/watch?v=0jze5RbyoW4&t=225s)
- What sets Figma's plugins apart is that they are instant: no install and no reload, you type what you want and run it. He calls these **instant plugins** one of his favorite lazy solutions. (Why Everyone Is Obsessed With Figma at 4:30, https://www.youtube.com/watch?v=0jze5RbyoW4&t=270s)
- He credits Figma with inventing **team flow state**. Flow used to be reached alone and then broke into file versions and comments in another app, and Figma let a whole team be absorbed in one live file. (Why Everyone Is Obsessed With Figma at 6:45, https://www.youtube.com/watch?v=0jze5RbyoW4&t=405s)
- Figma did the opposite of the launch-fast playbook. He says GitHub took 6 months and Canva 1 year to release, while Figma worked in private for 3 years, which he calls the exact opposite of what a startup should do and also a reason it won. (Why Everyone Is Obsessed With Figma at 9:00, https://www.youtube.com/watch?v=0jze5RbyoW4&t=540s)
- Those technical foundations are why Figma went so long without a rival. He says even Adobe was not up to the challenge, so it tried to buy Figma for $20 billion, about 50 times yearly revenue, and antitrust stopped the deal. (Why Everyone Is Obsessed With Figma at 9:45, https://www.youtube.com/watch?v=0jze5RbyoW4&t=585s; Why Everyone Is Obsessed With Figma at 10:30, https://www.youtube.com/watch?v=0jze5RbyoW4&t=630s)
- He does not think Figma cracked everything. The layer tree turns into an unnavigable mess quickly, and pages have confused him many times. (Why Everyone Is Obsessed With Figma at 6:00, https://www.youtube.com/watch?v=0jze5RbyoW4&t=360s)
- With millions of users, sometimes the best move is to do nothing and let users build it. He says Figma's plugins became one of its best sources for deciding what to build natively, with Apple's Liquid Glass as the example. (The Ridiculous Engineering Of Figma at 17:15, https://www.youtube.com/watch?v=5t8dh3DSdBk&t=1035s)
- In 2026 he said Figma's edge in AI is its canvas, not the model. He does not expect any tool to be dramatically better at producing a design in one shot, but Figma has a canvas built over 10 years where a wrong result is fixed quickly. (The Ridiculous Engineering Of Figma at 27:45, https://www.youtube.com/watch?v=5t8dh3DSdBk&t=1665s)

## What is his verdict on Arc?

- Arc did the most difficult thing by questioning how the basic parts of a browser work. He says most rivals are a browser for something, such as privacy or crypto, while Arc challenged assumptions everyone had followed for 15 years. (Why Everyone Is Obsessed With Arc Browser at 1:30, https://www.youtube.com/watch?v=UdopamaLJC8&t=90s)
- He says Chrome, with 63% of internet users, wins because it just works. (Why Everyone Is Obsessed With Arc Browser at 0:45, https://www.youtube.com/watch?v=UdopamaLJC8&t=45s)
- Arc treats the browser as a home screen because people mostly use web apps, not websites, and it puts tabs on the left because vertical space is the precious one on a horizontal screen. (Why Everyone Is Obsessed With Arc Browser at 2:15, https://www.youtube.com/watch?v=UdopamaLJC8&t=135s; Why Everyone Is Obsessed With Arc Browser at 1:30, https://www.youtube.com/watch?v=UdopamaLJC8&t=90s)
- Arc's colour picker works through the IKEA effect, the same principle he sees in Notion: you spend an hour tweaking your browser colour and then show it off. (Why Everyone Is Obsessed With Arc Browser at 3:00, https://www.youtube.com/watch?v=UdopamaLJC8&t=180s)
- Old browsers all look alike because of familiarity bias. He says Arc got past it with a very good onboarding sequence and a personal card that makes you feel part of a club, then showed too much at once, and his suggested fix is to reveal features one at a time as video games do. (Why Everyone Is Obsessed With Arc Browser at 3:45, https://www.youtube.com/watch?v=UdopamaLJC8&t=225s)
- Artificial scarcity is underused in software, and he calls Arc's invite system genius. An extra install costs nothing, yet the invite controlled growth so the team was not flooded and built a sense of belonging. (Why Everyone Is Obsessed With Arc Browser at 4:30, https://www.youtube.com/watch?v=UdopamaLJC8&t=270s)
- Seeding the first invites with tech people kept the user base in tech. In November 2023 he said about half the people at his company used it. (Why Everyone Is Obsessed With Arc Browser at 7:30, https://www.youtube.com/watch?v=UdopamaLJC8&t=450s)
- "The biggest problem I have with Arc is that it's free." In November 2023 he estimated about $12 million a year in costs for a company with 66 employees, zero revenue and no clear plan to monetize. (Why Everyone Is Obsessed With Arc Browser at 6:00, https://www.youtube.com/watch?v=UdopamaLJC8&t=360s; Why Everyone Is Obsessed With Arc Browser at 6:45, https://www.youtube.com/watch?v=UdopamaLJC8&t=405s)
- He saw Arc's secret weapon as a niche audience that is higher earning, influential in tech and in the browser 10 hours a day. He proposed three ethical ways to earn from it: paid defaults and supported apps, a premium subscription modelled on Superhuman, and an affiliate model where software companies pay users to try products. (Why Everyone Is Obsessed With Arc Browser at 8:15, https://www.youtube.com/watch?v=UdopamaLJC8&t=495s; Why Everyone Is Obsessed With Arc Browser at 9:00, https://www.youtube.com/watch?v=UdopamaLJC8&t=540s)
- By May 2025 he listed Arc, which he had used for 3 years, among products that got worse as they got popular, because its company stopped working on it to build something else. (Why Tech Gets Terrible When It Gets Popular at 0:00, https://www.youtube.com/watch?v=BQLVgmhgtpQ&t=0s)

## Why did WhatsApp win, and what decides which chat app wins?

- He calls WhatsApp a boring app that should have failed, and says a chat app is probably one of the simplest kinds of applications you can build. (Why Half The World Uses WhatsApp at 1:30, https://www.youtube.com/watch?v=1O1u0UO99NE&t=90s)
- He says WhatsApp made three crucial moves behind its success; the first was using your phone number as the account identifier instead of email, and the second was focusing on the basics while every competitor kept adding features. (Why Half The World Uses WhatsApp at 2:15, https://www.youtube.com/watch?v=1O1u0UO99NE&t=135s)
- The phone number worked because the founders thought globally. He says for billions of people in countries like India the smartphone was their first access to the internet, so they had a phone number and not necessarily an email address. (Why Half The World Uses WhatsApp at 3:00, https://www.youtube.com/watch?v=1O1u0UO99NE&t=180s)
- The giants missed the opening. He says Skype was focused on video calls, Apple on its walled garden, Facebook on its social site, and Google had so many chat apps it became a meme. (Why Half The World Uses WhatsApp at 1:30, https://www.youtube.com/watch?v=1O1u0UO99NE&t=90s)
- Staying basic was the hardest choice when every rival in 2012 was adding mini games, video calls and social features and WeChat grew into a monster app. He says one thing on first login gets a new user to the aha moment faster. (Why Half The World Uses WhatsApp at 3:45, https://www.youtube.com/watch?v=1O1u0UO99NE&t=225s; Why Half The World Uses WhatsApp at 4:30, https://www.youtube.com/watch?v=1O1u0UO99NE&t=270s)
- "Keeping things simple is exactly what made it possible." He says about 50 people were managing 465 million users when Facebook bought WhatsApp in 2014 for $19 billion. (Why Half The World Uses WhatsApp at 6:45, https://www.youtube.com/watch?v=1O1u0UO99NE&t=405s)
- The same simplicity and scale has a price: he says every feature must be built for five platforms and rolled out to two billion people, which is why reactions took so long to arrive. (Why Half The World Uses WhatsApp at 5:15, https://www.youtube.com/watch?v=1O1u0UO99NE&t=315s)
- He says WhatsApp never took hold in the US because SMS was cheap there and the iPhone has 57% of the market, so people use iMessage. Globally iOS has only 25%. (Why Half The World Uses WhatsApp at 7:30, https://www.youtube.com/watch?v=1O1u0UO99NE&t=450s)
- Small chat decisions decide which app people choose. He says WhatsApp shipped read receipts as a default in 2014, added an option to hide them 10 days later after the backlash, and made hiding reciprocal so that not everyone would turn them off at no cost. (The Insane Design Of Messaging at 0:00, https://www.youtube.com/watch?v=6DSW1rN-ZV4&t=0s; The Insane Design Of Messaging at 0:45, https://www.youtube.com/watch?v=6DSW1rN-ZV4&t=45s)
- He calls Instagram DMs a **sharing machine**, not a chat: the paper plane under every post exists to feed the algorithm. WhatsApp limits the same forward button to five people at a time because it optimizes for everyday messaging and forwarding there means spam and scams. (The Insane Design Of Messaging at 6:00, https://www.youtube.com/watch?v=6DSW1rN-ZV4&t=360s; The Insane Design Of Messaging at 6:45, https://www.youtube.com/watch?v=6DSW1rN-ZV4&t=405s)
- Slack and Discord look the same and serve opposite goals. A Slack reply goes into a thread that never closes, so Slack is built to be found later, while a Discord reply lands in the stream because Discord is built for now. (The Insane Design Of Messaging at 7:30, https://www.youtube.com/watch?v=6DSW1rN-ZV4&t=450s; The Insane Design Of Messaging at 9:00, https://www.youtube.com/watch?v=6DSW1rN-ZV4&t=540s)
- He uses Jakob's law to explain why all chat apps look alike: people spend most of their time in other products and want yours to work the same way. He thinks a better chat design probably exists and is almost impossible to move people to. (The Insane Design Of Messaging at 15:00, https://www.youtube.com/watch?v=6DSW1rN-ZV4&t=900s)

## Why does he rate Google Maps so highly?

- A better product was not enough at launch because the habit did not exist. He says Google Maps got over 10 million visits the day it leaked in February 2005 and then took a year to get back to that traffic. (Why Google Maps Is The Most Important Tech Product Of The 2020s at 0:00, https://www.youtube.com/watch?v=bCvpoHxSVIE&t=0s)
- He says the iPhone, where it was the default maps app, is what pushed Google Maps into the mainstream. (Why Google Maps Is The Most Important Tech Product Of The 2020s at 0:45, https://www.youtube.com/watch?v=bCvpoHxSVIE&t=45s)
- The real value is the **deep map** underneath, not the app. He says you could rewrite the app in a few weeks, while the single map fed by satellites, Street View, business data, user photos and live traffic is one of the most valuable assets in the world. (Why Google Maps Is The Most Important Tech Product Of The 2020s at 1:30, https://www.youtube.com/watch?v=bCvpoHxSVIE&t=90s; Why Google Maps Is The Most Important Tech Product Of The 2020s at 2:15, https://www.youtube.com/watch?v=bCvpoHxSVIE&t=135s)
- Street View turned out to be a data source and not just a viewer. In 2022 he said it covered over 124 countries against 11 for Apple's equivalent. (Why Google Maps Is The Most Important Tech Product Of The 2020s at 1:30, https://www.youtube.com/watch?v=bCvpoHxSVIE&t=90s)
- Crowdsourcing is how Google solves the last 1%. Its users are already everywhere, so reviews, confirmed opening hours and reported closures feed the map, and he says Google captures 50 million updates a day. (The Most Dangerous App That Everyone Loves at 5:15, https://www.youtube.com/watch?v=P2qOiKA56tI&t=315s)
- One 1% failure is enough to lose a user. A friend in London was shown a bar entrance on the wrong side of the block by Apple Maps and deleted it on the spot. (The Most Dangerous App That Everyone Loves at 2:15, https://www.youtube.com/watch?v=P2qOiKA56tI&t=135s)
- He says iPhone users start on Apple Maps, hit one to three such failures and go back to Google Maps, which he estimated in 2024 at two to nine times more users worldwide. (The Most Dangerous App That Everyone Loves at 11:15, https://www.youtube.com/watch?v=P2qOiKA56tI&t=675s)
- Good design here means studying real use. He says the one-finger zoom exists because people on bikes cannot pinch, and the pin was invented so the marker would not cover what is around the target. (The Most Dangerous App That Everyone Loves at 6:45, https://www.youtube.com/watch?v=P2qOiKA56tI&t=405s)
- People who worry about social media data still leave Location History on because of his **data-value balance**: a free product works while the data it takes is matched by value given back, and he says Maps still gives back far more than it takes. (The Most Dangerous App That Everyone Loves at 9:00, https://www.youtube.com/watch?v=P2qOiKA56tI&t=540s; The Most Dangerous App That Everyone Loves at 10:30, https://www.youtube.com/watch?v=P2qOiKA56tI&t=630s)
- In April 2024 he called Google Maps the embodiment of the perfect tech product because it uses the latest technology to solve hard problems, such as fuel-efficient routing that he says removes 250,000 cars' worth of pollution. In the same breath he warned Google could ruin it with aggressive monetisation. (The Most Dangerous App That Everyone Loves at 12:00, https://www.youtube.com/watch?v=P2qOiKA56tI&t=720s)
- In April 2022 he said Google Maps has a good chance of becoming the most important tech product of the next decade, because augmented reality needs a shared 3D map of the whole world as its canvas and he sees Google as best positioned, given the amount and variety of its map data and Android's 70 percent share of mobile devices. (Why Google Maps Is The Most Important Tech Product Of The 2020s at 0:00, https://www.youtube.com/watch?v=bCvpoHxSVIE&t=0s; Why Google Maps Is The Most Important Tech Product Of The 2020s at 12:00, https://www.youtube.com/watch?v=bCvpoHxSVIE&t=720s)
- An obvious feature can hide so much trouble that even Google and Apple pass on it. His worked case, which he calls a hypothetical, is showing unsafe areas: it would help some people and save some lives, but discriminate against crime-heavy areas, drive people and tourism away, hurt local businesses, make the app a constant source of anxiety and put the company at the centre of controversy, media backlash and government scrutiny. (Why Google Maps Doesn't Show You Unsafe Areas at 6:00, https://www.youtube.com/watch?v=4i66ht6YHkw&t=360s; Why Google Maps Doesn't Show You Unsafe Areas at 5:15, https://www.youtube.com/watch?v=4i66ht6YHkw&t=315s)

## What made Spotify, Airbnb, YouTube and other consumer hits work?

- Spotify's real rival was music piracy, and it beat a free competitor by being more convenient. Pirating cost nothing but was painful, so a few dollars a month for unlimited streaming on your phone was the better deal. (Famous Apps Looked Like This at 1:30, https://www.youtube.com/watch?v=_Sn8Glj5NW4&t=90s; Famous Apps Looked Like This at 2:15, https://www.youtube.com/watch?v=_Sn8Glj5NW4&t=135s)
- He says Spotify's shuffle was truly random at first and was changed after users complained of hearing the same artist again and again. The new method spreads each artist's songs across the playlist, because people are bad at judging randomness. (The Spotify Shuffle Button is a Lie at 1:30, https://www.youtube.com/watch?v=NhAIA-o7f4M&t=90s; The Spotify Shuffle Button is a Lie at 3:45, https://www.youtube.com/watch?v=NhAIA-o7f4M&t=225s)
- The obvious choice that seems not worth discussing is where great products differ from mediocre ones. He says a detail as small as how one button works can be worth billions at Spotify's scale, since a subscriber deciding whether to stay just knows the drive home sounded better. (The Spotify Shuffle Button is a Lie at 3:45, https://www.youtube.com/watch?v=NhAIA-o7f4M&t=225s; The Spotify Shuffle Button is a Lie at 4:30, https://www.youtube.com/watch?v=NhAIA-o7f4M&t=270s)
- He says the spotlight effect is what powers the third ingredient behind Spotify Wrapped, sharing, and that Spotify has mastered all three ingredients but has to keep raising its game every year to stay relevant. (The Reason Everyone's Obsessed With Spotify Wrapped at 9:45, https://www.youtube.com/watch?v=EuzRWd5U6_I&t=585s)
- Spotify's first recap in 2015 had the same numbers and a good design and did not catch on, because it had only the data. (The Reason Everyone's Obsessed With Spotify Wrapped at 0:45, https://www.youtube.com/watch?v=EuzRWd5U6_I&t=45s)
- He says Spotify withholds listening stats all year on purpose so that you wait for Wrapped, and changes the story every year so the question becomes what the story will be, not what the data is. (The Reason Everyone's Obsessed With Spotify Wrapped at 2:15, https://www.youtube.com/watch?v=EuzRWd5U6_I&t=135s; The Reason Everyone's Obsessed With Spotify Wrapped at 5:15, https://www.youtube.com/watch?v=EuzRWd5U6_I&t=315s)
- Copies of Wrapped fail when the data is not something people want to see. He says Peloton's recap reminded people they had done three workouts all year and Monzo recapped expenses before Christmas. (The Reason Everyone's Obsessed With Spotify Wrapped at 6:45, https://www.youtube.com/watch?v=EuzRWd5U6_I&t=405s)
- Airbnb's 2022 redesign was a strategy move made through product design alone. He says it reframed its job to be done from booking a known stay to discovering where to go, while every other travel site still starts by asking where and when. (Airbnb Just Hacked The Travel Industry Again at 1:30, https://www.youtube.com/watch?v=OwiztfnZwE4&t=90s; Airbnb Just Hacked The Travel Industry Again at 3:45, https://www.youtube.com/watch?v=OwiztfnZwE4&t=225s)
- He ties this to Brian Chesky being one of the few CEOs with a design background. Big companies usually expand by building or buying, as Uber did with Uber Eats, and Airbnb used only the listings it already had. (Airbnb Just Hacked The Travel Industry Again at 5:15, https://www.youtube.com/watch?v=OwiztfnZwE4&t=315s)
- YouTube optimizes for playing time, not time on the app. He says browsing hurts how a product is perceived, which is why thumbnails play on hover and the video keeps playing while you browse. (The Ridiculous Engineering Of YouTube Videos at 2:15, https://www.youtube.com/watch?v=giP1iIbSVh0&t=135s)

## What do first versions, small companies and the AI-era hits show?

- The first versions of famous products were basic and sometimes bad, and he says that gives everyone permission to start. (Famous Apps Looked Like This at 6:45, https://www.youtube.com/watch?v=_Sn8Glj5NW4&t=405s)
- The users who will take a product to the next level are sometimes already inside it. His case is Twitch, which began as one fast-growing gaming category on Justin.tv and was spun off into its own company. (Famous Apps Looked Like This at 0:45, https://www.youtube.com/watch?v=_Sn8Glj5NW4&t=45s)
- Coinbase succeeded by hiding the complexity of Bitcoin. He says the 2012 site looked like PayPal, explained nothing complicated and framed Bitcoin as a way to send and receive money. (Famous Apps Looked Like This at 3:00, https://www.youtube.com/watch?v=_Sn8Glj5NW4&t=180s)
- Reaching the mainstream, which he calls the moment a product must **cross the river**, means shifting away from what early adopters wanted. Early users love the technology, and the majority needs it hidden with the real value up front. (Famous Apps Looked Like This at 3:45, https://www.youtube.com/watch?v=_Sn8Glj5NW4&t=225s)
- Craigslist shows the power of keeping things simple. He says it added no features and never redesigned, and still gets 6.2 billion page views a month and makes 500 million to 1 billion dollars a year. (The Weird Rise Of Anti-Startups at 2:15, https://www.youtube.com/watch?v=xL1MOOD5Ox8&t=135s)
- Gumroad's lesson is freedom over resources. He says the team went from 20 people to its founder alone, the growth chart does not show the moment everyone left, and it now processes over a hundred million in payments while profitable. (The Weird Rise Of Anti-Startups at 4:30, https://www.youtube.com/watch?v=xL1MOOD5Ox8&t=270s; The Weird Rise Of Anti-Startups at 5:15, https://www.youtube.com/watch?v=xL1MOOD5Ox8&t=315s)
- Midjourney's lesson is that limits become the biggest asset. In 2024 he said it had fewer than 40 employees, no outside investment and $200 million a year in revenue, and that running on Discord for lack of resources gave it instant feedback and a public feed that works as the best tutorial. (The Weird Rise Of Anti-Startups at 6:00, https://www.youtube.com/watch?v=xL1MOOD5Ox8&t=360s; The Weird Rise Of Anti-Startups at 9:00, https://www.youtube.com/watch?v=xL1MOOD5Ox8&t=540s)
- Superhuman is one of the few luxury apps because it has all three ingredients of luxury. He lists quality, with keyboard shortcuts for everything and a one-on-one onboarding call, exclusivity through an invite queue, and a price of $30 a month when email is free. (Why Luxury Apps Don't Exist at 8:15, https://www.youtube.com/watch?v=X1HqzLbGznc&t=495s)
- He calls the Teenage Engineering OP-1 his favourite piece of tech because it is constrained: no Wi-Fi, no notifications, only creating. He says it has the best software design he has used and that he learned it from a 30 minute tutorial. (The Best Tech Product Ever Designed at 2:15, https://www.youtube.com/watch?v=n1cMrkMqmnw&t=135s; The Best Tech Product Ever Designed at 6:00, https://www.youtube.com/watch?v=n1cMrkMqmnw&t=360s)
- In a 2025 video made in partnership with Lovable, he said the team is obsessed with getting a new user to the aha moment quickly and builds apps in reverse order, starting with the visual. (Behind The Scenes Of Lovable's Insane Design at 13:30, https://www.youtube.com/watch?v=ZoVLS-Wxr1s&t=810s; Behind The Scenes Of Lovable's Insane Design at 15:00, https://www.youtube.com/watch?v=ZoVLS-Wxr1s&t=900s)
- In 2026 he said Claude Code won without anything consumer products usually obsess over: it is hard to install, terminal-only and was never built for retention. He says three psychology and design principles explain why it hooked people, and the first is that it follows the most powerful game design principles by complete accident. (Why Everyone is Suddenly Obsessed With Claude Code at 0:00, https://www.youtube.com/watch?v=gzt52Trk9w0&t=0s; Why Everyone is Suddenly Obsessed With Claude Code at 0:45, https://www.youtube.com/watch?v=gzt52Trk9w0&t=45s)
- He maps Claude Code onto principles from the book The Art of Game Design: each prompt is a short feedback loop, the growing file tree is visible progress, and results that only sometimes work act like a slot machine. He says nobody at Anthropic planned this. (Why Everyone is Suddenly Obsessed With Claude Code at 2:15, https://www.youtube.com/watch?v=gzt52Trk9w0&t=135s; Why Everyone is Suddenly Obsessed With Claude Code at 4:30, https://www.youtube.com/watch?v=gzt52Trk9w0&t=270s)
- Great products offer **perceived simplicity**, with the complexity moved away from the user. He says Claude Code is simple because it solves its own problems, as when a failed connection to Notion led it to find another route on its own. (Why Everyone is Suddenly Obsessed With Claude Code at 6:00, https://www.youtube.com/watch?v=gzt52Trk9w0&t=360s; Why Everyone is Suddenly Obsessed With Claude Code at 6:45, https://www.youtube.com/watch?v=gzt52Trk9w0&t=405s)
- ChatGPT was an accident. In 2025 he said OpenAI put a bare web interface on its model, did not expect the result, and that the product people use is where the money is: 75% of OpenAI's revenue in October 2024 came from ChatGPT paid plans. (People Still Don't Understand AI at 3:45, https://www.youtube.com/watch?v=r87NGV1Lgao&t=225s; People Still Don't Understand AI at 6:00, https://www.youtube.com/watch?v=r87NGV1Lgao&t=360s)
- "They're not building the next Google. They're building the next Facebook." In December 2025 he said there are three main reasons OpenAI might be forced into this, and the first is that ChatGPT's economics are broken: an interaction costs far more than a Google search, and Google funds everything else from search ads while owning its whole stack. (How ChatGPT Is Weirdly Turning Into Facebook at 0:00, https://www.youtube.com/watch?v=C7Z11ghwevQ&t=0s; How ChatGPT Is Weirdly Turning Into Facebook at 3:00, https://www.youtube.com/watch?v=C7Z11ghwevQ&t=180s)
- In July 2024 he said Apple had effectively stolen AI from everyone else, or rather stolen access to users and apps: most people will reach AI through Siri, and App Intents will be the only route to personal data and actions in apps, and Apple controls both. He left open whether developers would adopt App Intents. (How Apple Stole AI From Everyone Else at 8:15, https://www.youtube.com/watch?v=gV1f-lS0C9g&t=495s; How Apple Stole AI From Everyone Else at 9:00, https://www.youtube.com/watch?v=gV1f-lS0C9g&t=540s)

## Why does he say products and companies fail?

- In November 2023 he called Humane's AI Pin a great idea with poor execution. It depended on someone else's model, could not keep its camera running for context, and presented itself as a phone replacement when it should have been an accessory. (We Need To Talk About The AI Pin at 2:15, https://www.youtube.com/watch?v=E2-L-Gv0wr0&t=135s; We Need To Talk About The AI Pin at 6:45, https://www.youtube.com/watch?v=E2-L-Gv0wr0&t=405s)
- He called the pin's projector its stupidest part, since double tapping an earbud to skip a song is easier than holding out a hand and pinching. By 2026 he said the device, which raised $230 million, had become one of the biggest tech fails. (We Need To Talk About The AI Pin at 4:30, https://www.youtube.com/watch?v=E2-L-Gv0wr0&t=270s; The Weird Future Of User Interfaces at 0:45, https://www.youtube.com/watch?v=f32W5BEzWN0&t=45s)
- In 2026 he said every attempt to kill the user interface has failed because those products attacked what graphical interfaces already do well: simple, habitual tasks like booking an Uber or sending a message. (The Weird Future Of User Interfaces at 0:45, https://www.youtube.com/watch?v=f32W5BEzWN0&t=45s; The Weird Future Of User Interfaces at 2:15, https://www.youtube.com/watch?v=f32W5BEzWN0&t=135s)
- On the Rabbit R1 in January 2024: "It's completely useless, and this is why it's genius." He believed the real product was the model that operates websites and the $199 device was a PR vehicle for software that is hard to explain. (Is This Useless at 6:00, https://www.youtube.com/watch?v=dWEjDIsMFB0&t=360s)
- He says the main reason Google Glass failed is that the hardware was too intrusive on the face, and that its camera was seen as creepy. He still called it, in 2023, probably the closest thing so far to the future of computing. (Apple Built The Vision Pro To Fail And It's Genius at 9:00, https://www.youtube.com/watch?v=fNEni_DP4fY&t=540s; Smartphones Are Dead But We Finally Know What's Next at 12:45, https://www.youtube.com/watch?v=pB6TdukUsAg&t=765s)
- In June 2023 he said the Vision Pro is not an iPhone moment but another Apple Lisa, a deliberately over-specified and overpriced product whose ideas matter more than its sales. He said Apple does not really care how many it sells. (Apple Built The Vision Pro To Fail And It's Genius at 2:15, https://www.youtube.com/watch?v=fNEni_DP4fY&t=135s; Apple Built The Vision Pro To Fail And It's Genius at 3:00, https://www.youtube.com/watch?v=fNEni_DP4fY&t=180s)
- Smart TVs are hated because the interface and the core input do not match: rows built for scrolling are driven by four arrows and a button. He says Apple shipped a touch remote in 2015, everyone hated it, and it went back to buttons. (The Most Important Tiny Decision In Tech History at 12:00, https://www.youtube.com/watch?v=7EVZpW9Rdwk&t=720s; The Most Important Tiny Decision In Tech History at 12:45, https://www.youtube.com/watch?v=7EVZpW9Rdwk&t=765s)
- Companies that only perfect the present die. He says BlackBerry had the best phones and keyboards, kept polishing them when the iPhone arrived, and no longer makes phones. (The Weird Reason Your Tech Stays Broken at 7:30, https://www.youtube.com/watch?v=-51ocAD6zBI&t=450s)
- Olivetti is his case of a lead thrown away. He says it completed the first fully transistor-based computer in the world in 1959, and lost the chance because the family who inherited the company after Adriano Olivetti's death in 1960 preferred the reliable mechanical business, and in 1964 a group of industrialists, politicians and family members decided to sell the electronics division to General Electric. (How Italy Almost Became Silicon Valley at 5:15, https://www.youtube.com/watch?v=86kXVggNQbo&t=315s; How Italy Almost Became Silicon Valley at 8:15, https://www.youtube.com/watch?v=86kXVggNQbo&t=495s)
- He says Olivetti showed the Programma 101 in a corner of a 1965 New York show while typewriters took centre stage, and that Hewlett-Packard copied it closely enough to pay Olivetti $900,000 for the patent. (How Italy Almost Became Silicon Valley at 13:30, https://www.youtube.com/watch?v=86kXVggNQbo&t=810s)
- His lesson from Olivetti is that visionaries are **sparks** and it takes an environment of open minds to turn a spark into a fire. Italy had several sparks and a culture that looked to the past and feared change. (How Italy Almost Became Silicon Valley at 14:15, https://www.youtube.com/watch?v=86kXVggNQbo&t=855s; How Italy Almost Became Silicon Valley at 15:00, https://www.youtube.com/watch?v=86kXVggNQbo&t=900s)
- Seeing the future is not enough either. He says the experts who watched Douglas Engelbart's 1968 demo did almost nothing with it for 15 years, and Xerox PARC built the graphical interface without grasping its potential. (The Man Who Made Apple Possible In 1968 at 3:00, https://www.youtube.com/watch?v=BXSHJXHeaqM&t=180s)
- Japan bet everything on physical high-tech and missed software and the internet. In 2023 he said its trains and toilets feel like the future while every website he used there felt like the 90s. (Why Japan Is Living In A Weird Retro Future at 5:15, https://www.youtube.com/watch?v=zgV8bJy5s34&t=315s; Why Japan Is Living In A Weird Retro Future at 6:00, https://www.youtube.com/watch?v=zgV8bJy5s34&t=360s)
- He says Google Now did not work because combining unstructured data from very different sources and apps is incredibly hard: the ecosystem holds his messages, location, documents and emails, but in silos inside one app or another, and large language models are what can finally combine them. (How Tech Secretly Killed Choice at 10:30, https://www.youtube.com/watch?v=UdzT1YxeZsY&t=630s)
- He says 2018 was the year that killed YouTube Rewind: that edition was one of the most disliked videos in YouTube's history, because it was cringe and had also gone stale, the same recipe year after year. (The Reason Everyone's Obsessed With Spotify Wrapped at 4:30, https://www.youtube.com/watch?v=EuzRWd5U6_I&t=270s)
- Products that win can still get worse because of what he calls the marginal user, the next user the company has not acquired yet: as a product grows, people who barely care about it end up driving the decisions for those who love it. His example is YouTube Shorts, which loyal users find an annoying distraction but which won the A/B test. (Why Tech Gets Terrible When It Gets Popular at 9:00, https://www.youtube.com/watch?v=BQLVgmhgtpQ&t=540s; Why Tech Gets Terrible When It Gets Popular at 8:15, https://www.youtube.com/watch?v=BQLVgmhgtpQ&t=495s)
- In the end, he says, the goal of a product is to turn an idea into meaningful execution, and that design and engineering inside real limits is the hard part. (We Need To Talk About The AI Pin at 7:30, https://www.youtube.com/watch?v=E2-L-Gv0wr0&t=450s)
