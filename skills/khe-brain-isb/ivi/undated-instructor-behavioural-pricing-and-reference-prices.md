# Behavioural pricing and reference prices

A short stretch of a marketing class on pricing, continuing from the value proposition and Calyx case session with the same instructor, who is not named in the recording. He introduces behavioural biases in how people read prices: reference prices, judging a saving against the item's price, framing as gain or loss, and fairness. He takes questions on surge pricing and virtual currencies before a break.

## What was said

### Biases and reference prices

- The instructor says that if you understand the systematic manner in which people behave, their behaviour becomes predictable, and that is essentially what a bias is.
- He says the first behavioural pricing concept is the reference price.
- In his opening example, what people said they were willing to pay at a fancy resort was almost double what they were willing to pay in the other setting he showed.
- He says this work comes from Daniel Kahneman, who won the Nobel Prize, and that much of it is about behavioural biases of this kind.
- He says the advertised price has a large effect in negotiations.

### A saving is judged against the item's price

- His example is a campus store purchase of a laptop and a sweatshirt, where the cashier says one of the items is cheaper at a mall some distance away. He polls the class on whether they would make the trip, once with the discount on the laptop and once on the sweatshirt.
- He says the buyer's reference for the sweatshirt is its 650 price, so a saving of 400 is read against 650.
- He says a low-cost provider should have a reference point that makes it explicitly the best deal available.

### Framing as a gain or a loss

- The instructor runs a two-round choice. In the first, you are given 1,000 and choose between winning another 1,000 with probability 0.5 and winning 500 for sure. In the second, you are given 2,000 and choose between losing 1,000 with probability 0.5 and losing 500 for sure.
- He says he has done nothing but frame the same outcomes differently, and people behave substantially differently.
- He says in the domain of gains people go for certainty and are very risk averse. In the domain of losses they start taking riskier gambles.
- He reports that in the gain frame 16 chose the gamble against 84 who did not, and in the loss frame 69 chose the gamble against 31.
- He applies this to pay. An employer can quote a total package that includes a variable part conditional on performance, or quote a base salary and present the variable part as extra. The amounts are the same and only the framing differs.

### Utility and fairness

- The instructor says that in the real world the fairness of the transaction also counts, alongside its economic value.
- He says if fairness is missing, people will pay less than the product is worth.
- He says the same happens when customers feel you are taking advantage of them.
- He defines acquisition utility as perceived value minus price.
- He defines transaction utility as the price set against the reference price.

### Where reference prices come from

- The instructor says one source of a reference price is past experience. His example is a student who expects yoghurt to cost what it cost the last time he bought it.
- He says other sources are looking around and looking at the competition.
- He says much of a reference price comes from recall.
- He says sellers can influence reference prices. One way is advertising, constantly putting prices in front of people.
- He says a seller whose value proposition is low cost starts by showing discounts on low-cost items, and a premium player starts by showcasing its high-end products.
- His example is an Apple store, which has lower-end products and does not put them first. The most expensive items come first, so that the prices further in feel low.
- He says salespeople in real estate, textiles and jewellery show the most expensive product first for the same reason. It sets the reference point, and the negotiation on other products happens against it.
- He says the environment is one way reference points are set.
- He says you are not setting prices based on the customer's reference point, but you must be aware of what it is.
- Asked whether a seller can charge different prices at different locations, he says you can, and that if customers find out you are exploiting them on that basis there will be a big backlash.

### Surge pricing and virtual money

- Asked why peak-hour pricing does not feel like cheating, the instructor says it rests on simple demand and supply: more demand at peak hours with the same supply.
- He says customers normalise it over time and come to accept paying double.
- He says the seller is also leveraging uncertainty.
- Asked why platforms use virtual coins, he compares them to credit cards. Once money becomes virtual, the pain of paying is lower.
- He calls this inter-temporal bias: with a credit card the loss is only felt when the statement arrives.

## Examples and cases used

- Daniel Kahneman's work: the source he names for reference prices and framing.
- An Apple store layout: expensive products first to set a high reference point.
- Real estate, textile and jewellery sales: the most expensive item is shown first.
- Peak-hour surge pricing: accepted because it is explained by demand and supply and becomes normal.
- Credit cards and virtual coins: paying hurts less when the money is virtual.

## Numbers given

- 16 against 84 choosing the gamble in the gain frame and 69 against 31 in the loss frame, as the instructor reports the result of the choice experiment.
- 650 for the sweatshirt and 400 for the saving, in his campus store example.
