# Brand positioning with category, tension, promise and reason to believe, Parvesh Debuka

This is the third recorded part of Parvesh Debuka's marketing bootcamp. Founders took turns presenting their positioning using a four-part model, and he and the room critiqued each one. Almost all of the recording is feedback on individual ventures, so this note is short. It keeps only the model and the general principles he stated while critiquing.

## What was said

### The model

- Parvesh Debuka's positioning model has four parts in order: category, tension, promise, and reason to believe.
- The class example for the model is boAt: the category is audio, the tension is that the category was dull and techie, the promise is youth lifestyle, and the reasons to believe are that it is stylish, Indian and affordable.
- He says once the tension is sharp, the promise, the product, the messaging and the reasons to believe all become clear.
- He says the reason to believe answers the customer's question of who you are and why you can deliver the promise better than anyone else.
- His illustration is a gifting service. If the tension is bad gifting, the promise is that the buyer will never go wrong, and the reason to believe is a proprietary way of matching the giver and the receiver.
- He says for a promise of guaranteed placement, the reason to believe would be the placement record of the last batch, or for a first batch, tie-ups with named employers.

### One promise

- He says a brand should pick one promise, because the return on spending is highest when it picks one thing and chases it.
- He says a brand that says two or three things confuses people and stops knowing who it is for.
- He says a brand that tries to stand for several things will see sales at the start and begin to struggle later.
- He says iconic brands have usually stood for one promise, and one promise lets the whole organisation rally behind it.
- He says choosing one promise is the tough thing to do, which is why many businesses avoid it.
- He says a brand can go wrong with its one promise, and can then change to another.
- He says a promise has to hit the customer hard, and a line that merely claims to be better than incumbents does not do that.

### Choosing who to go after

- He says market size matters when the market is 50 or 100 crore. If it is thousands of crore, the founder should not let that worry drive the decision.
- He says when customers have lost trust in a brand built around a trusted personality, replacing that hero means bringing in a hero of one's own, and that can be very expensive.
- He says customers who already believe in the category but have lost some trust in existing brands are easier to reach than customers who need awareness and education first.
- He says a new entrant does not need to take on established institutions directly. It can serve a group they do not serve, such as people who are too old for the conventional route or already have a job.
- He says a platform built around chance discovery is weak as a positioning, because people do not choose a platform because they feel like serendipity that day.
- He says a promise that sounds grand can be far wider than what the product does, and the positioning should describe what the product actually is.
- He says a founder's own professional background should sit at the centre of the brand story when it is the reason customers would believe the promise. The alternative, bringing in a well-known face, is expensive.
- He says one good industry name backing a new venture is enough for a good start.

### Promise versus feature

- In a debate on whether an education venture should promise jobs, he sets a promise to find someone a job against a promise to give them the tools to find a job anywhere, and says the second is more powerful.
- He says he does not deny that learners want the financial outcome. His question is which promise is the most powerful persuasion to enrol.
- He says every institute promises jobs, so the customer's next question is why this one can deliver, and that is the reason to believe.
- He says a person changing careers asks whether they will be as good in fundamentals as someone who trained the conventional way for years, and the positioning has to answer that.

## Examples and cases used

- boAt: used to fill in the four-part model.
- IndiGo: he says IndiGo was all about being on time, while also being low cost.
- Tanishq solitaires: he says Tanishq ran an ad campaign built on the insight that men are terrible at gifting.
- Mia by Tanishq: he says the launch positioning was aimed at women who love going to work, and came from the understanding that women in modern workplaces found traditional jewellery designs too gaudy and too heavy to be relevant.
