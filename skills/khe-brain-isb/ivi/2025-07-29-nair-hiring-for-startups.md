# Hiring for startups, Praveen Nair

Praveen Nair, a hiring practitioner with more than two decades in the field, ran an online session of about an hour on how a startup should hire. He works at a firm that helps venture-backed companies find leadership talent. He covered why hiring matters, stories of hiring that went wrong and right, a four-step hiring process, and questions from founders. Much of the audio is unclear, so this note keeps only the points that come through.

## What was said

### Why hiring matters

- Nair says people want to work where great people already work, so a strong talent brand brings good candidates to a company on its own.
- He says a startup does not get that pull automatically and has to build it.
- He frames the need as the right candidate, at the right time, in the right role. He says a leader far beyond the experience the role needs is no use.
- He says scale matters too. If the business needs ten sales people on a given day and they are not there, the business may not survive.
- He says employees who feel they are not working with superior talent score 33% lower on employee net promoter score, a figure he ties to his firm's own work with clients.
- He says having the right calibre of people is one of the most important differentiators for a startup when it starts and when it scales.
- He says most founders make the mistake of not paying enough attention to hiring.

### The four-step process

- Nair's process has four parts: define the need, assess for fit, measure and optimise the hiring process, and onboard.
- He says doing the first three well is what builds the talent brand.

### Defining the need

- He says AI tools can write a good job description, and that the more important work is defining the role's success factors, competencies and level.
- He starts with the role as it is today and where it needs to get to, in what time frame.
- From that he lists the success factors, then the competencies that get a person there, then the level of proficiency needed in each.
- He says the more granular this is, the easier the assessment becomes.
- He describes levels as the difference between someone who has practised a thing, someone advanced and an expert, and says the hiring manager must know which the role needs.
- He says founders who skip this step end up going back to the drawing board.

### Assessing candidates

- Nair says some competencies can be assessed by the founder and some cannot, so the founder must put together a panel of people they trust.
- Each competency is assigned to an interviewer who is able to judge it.
- He says one interviewer alone does not give enough data points on a person.
- He says panel members should form their views separately, so the readings are unbiased, and then sit together and compare notes.
- He says an interview is only one way to get data. A case study, a panel presentation, a conversation over lunch or a walk through the candidate's past work are others.
- With a case study, he says to look at how the person thinks and not at whether they reached the answer.
- He says a fair and objective debrief on each candidate is essential. Collecting the information and not debating it wastes the process.

### Behaviour red flags

- Nair says competence and behaviour together decide whether a hire works, and that behaviour should not be played down.
- His examples of red flags are a candidate who arrives late for the interview, has not read about the company, or treats restaurant staff badly during an interview over a meal.
- He says a clear behavioural red flag should be a no, even when the candidate is the most qualified on paper.
- He says he has seen companies go with the most competent person and have it not pay off.

### Closing with candidates

- He says every candidate should get feedback, hired or not, because that person can be a customer later.
- He says the feedback can be direct about what did not work.

### Measuring the hiring process

- Nair says to count how many people you are meeting. Meeting too many without a result means the competencies or the process are not right.
- He says to take feedback from candidates about the process.
- He says to take feedback on interviewers too: whether they schedule and arrive on time and whether they introduce themselves before they start asking questions.
- He says to remove poor interviewers from the panel.

### Onboarding

- He says onboarding starts when the offer is accepted, and the company should stay engaged with the person until they join.
- He says new leaders need orientation in the company's culture and processes.
- He says case studies given to new joiners should reflect the company's current business problems.
- He says recruiting partners do more than find people. They help sell the company and its vision to candidates.

### Roles that are not clearly defined

- Asked about startup roles with no clear job description, Nair says this is true of most roles at all startups.
- He says the founder should still write down where things stand today and where they need to go, and can be honest that the picture beyond six months is unknown.
- He says founders expect superheroes and will not find them.
- He says to separate the competencies that are not negotiable from the rest, because a founder who asks for everything will never find anybody.
- He says waiting for the perfect person has a cost to the business, and the founder should know the cost of leaving the seat vacant.
- He equates success factors with the jobs to be done in the role.
- He suggests the founder pick the few things they can delegate today and hire for those.

### Freelancers and contracts

- Asked about hiring freelancers for projects, he says the same thinking applies whether the hire is full time, for a task or on contract.
- He says to put in black and white the tasks, the time period and the payment. An email, a contract or a signed paper all work.
- He says that when terms become a point of dispute, the cause is usually that one side was not clear about what was expected.
- He says pay sits between what the company will pay and what the candidate wants, and public benchmarks help settle it fairly.

### Goals and performance reviews

- Nair says this is not his area. He describes his colleagues' approach in outline.
- The premise he gives is that a company goal is broken down by function into what each person must do to be successful.
- He says performance management should run through the year and not as a single annual exercise, on whatever period the company picks, such as monthly or quarterly.

## Examples and cases used

- An unnamed e-commerce startup: it hired about 2,500 people in four or five months for a rollout in several cities, found the model was not working and laid people off. The news spread, and he says the hiring team spent six to eight months winning candidates back.
- An unnamed technology company: it brought in senior engineers from large US technology companies who did not understand the Indian market or its talent. He gives it as a hiring choice that went wrong.
- Diageo: he says it bought an Indian business and took two to three years to align it with its global brand, and that over 20 years its exits and successions have all been planned. He says it is not a company he worked with.

## Numbers given

- 33% lower employee net promoter score among employees who feel they are not working with superior talent, a figure Nair attributes to his firm's work.

## Not covered

- Compensation design, equity and ESOPs for early hires.
- Where to source candidates.
- Hiring a co-founder or the first few employees specifically. The examples are mostly leadership hires at funded companies.
- Detail on goal-setting and performance management, which he says is his colleagues' subject.
