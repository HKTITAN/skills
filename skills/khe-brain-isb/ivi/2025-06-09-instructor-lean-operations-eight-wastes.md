# Lean operations and the eight wastes, jet game debrief

The operations management instructor, who is not named in the recording, debriefs the paper jet game the class has just played. He collects observations from the class and turns each into a lean concept, ending with the eight wastes and the three Japanese terms. The recording starts after the game. The date is taken from the same morning's earlier recording.

## What was said

### Bottlenecks and what they bring

- The instructor says productivity can suffer for two reasons. Either the worker is not doing the task correctly, through a training, skill, attitude or behaviour issue, or the process design itself makes one step take more effort.
- In the game he puts workstation 2's slowness down to design, not to the people there.
- He explains a bottleneck with a water bottle. The narrow neck slows the flow of water, and a task that slows the flow of material through the organisation is a bottleneck.
- He says a manager should be able to explain a term like bottleneck in simple words to a front-line employee.
- A bottleneck is a big enemy, in his words, because it brings other enemies with it.
- The first is wait time. Forklift people stood at workstation 2 waiting, so that resource was underused.
- The second is work in progress. Workstation 1 finished fast and work piled up before workstation 2.
- He says inventory should always be seen as a cost to the company. If demand shifts, the work in progress is wasted, and people are needed to look after it.
- The third is idle time. Workstation 3 sat doing nothing while it waited for workstation 2.

### Quality, layout and overburden

- Workstation 2 could see that workstation 1 had made an error but had to pass the work on. Quality was checked only at the end, which he calls product quality.
- He says checking quality only at the end is bad for business, because 3 dollars had already been spent on each jet that was then rejected.
- He attributes the logistics and information flow problems that the class noticed to the layout.
- Some people were overburdened while others sat and watched. He calls this overburdening of resources caused by bad design.
- He says overburdening people leads to attrition.
- He says anyone who claims attrition is good for a company is showing bad leadership.
- His reason is tacit knowledge. Knowledge sits in employees' minds, and an organisation cannot put every piece of it on paper. Processes, documents and SOPs are explicit knowledge only.
- He says lean process improvement means respect for people. If something must change, change the process, the system or the policy, not the people.
- People contribute, he says, from internal motivation and not by force, and the organisations that understand this become great places to work.
- He calls it myopic for a leader to overburden people for short-term revenue.

### Question the batch size

- The instructor says he was waiting for someone to ask why material had to move in batches of four.
- He says an operational excellence leader should not blindly follow what is written in a process or policy. That makes a follower, not a leader.
- A leader who shuts down questions loses the knowledge in people's minds and is left with their own biased decisions, he says.
- He reveals that demand in the game was simulated. Each time jets came to the market the customer drew a chip with a number and bought only that many, such as 3, then 4, then 2.
- He expected the account manager to pass this to the forklift, and the firm to stop and ask why it was making four at a time when the customer bought a different number each time.
- His advice is to ask the customer at each sale how much they will buy next time and produce only that.
- He calls what happened an efficiency trap. Told to make four, everyone hurried to make and push more batches without stepping back to question it.
- His rule is that demand should drive production, and that the other way round is incorrect.
- Whatever is produced and not bought is a cost to the company.
- He says a batch rule may once have had a logic, for example a forecast of past demand from ten years ago, and it is a legacy policy now. You should know the justification.
- The behaviour he most wants a process excellence leader to develop is questioning the status quo and asking whether there is a better way.

### The eight wastes

- The instructor groups the class's observations into what he calls eight deadly wastes. He says they occur in processes every day and stay invisible unless you look for them.
- Transportation waste is unnecessary movement of goods from one place to another.
- Inventory waste is work in progress, which is a cost to the company.
- Over-processing waste is work the customer does not require. Writing ISB on the wings was his example, because the two quality rules did not mention it and the customer had to buy the jet with or without it.
- He adds that the message is not that branding is a waste. The task as designed used resources on something that was not a buying criterion in this game.
- His rule is to do the things that are required to meet a customer requirement.
- Defects are anything that does not meet a customer requirement, and they are also a cost to the company.
- The eighth waste is underutilised skills of people. He calls it the hidden waste because it is the hardest to spot.
- Caps were handed out at random in the game, so people and jobs were matched at random. He says that after hiring, it is the manager's job to notice when a person's skill fits another job better.
- In his words, skill is not a waste, underutilised skill is.
- He gives the abbreviation Tim Woods for remembering the wastes.
- He names gemba as the way to find waste. Go to the floor, observe for yourself and map which waste appears in which task.

### Muda, mura, muri

- The eight wastes together are the first enemy, muda, which means waste.
- The second enemy is mura, which he translates as variation. In the game market demand fluctuated and jets were made differently each time.
- The third enemy is muri, which he translates as overburdening.
- He says a process free from waste is called a lean process, in the same sense as a lean, healthy body.
- He defines lean operations as improving value to the customer by reducing waste in the process.
- He says the changes are small ones that front-line employees can understand and make, and many small improvements add up to a large impact.

## Examples and cases used

- The paper jet game: every lean concept in the session is drawn from what the class did in it.
- A water bottle: the narrow neck as the picture of a bottleneck.

## Numbers given

- 3 dollars: the cost already spent on each jet rejected at the final quality check, in the game's terms.
- 4: the batch size the class followed without asking why, while simulated customer demand varied between sales.
