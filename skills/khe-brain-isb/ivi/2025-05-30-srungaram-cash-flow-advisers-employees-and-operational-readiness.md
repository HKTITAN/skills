# Cash flow, advisers, employees and operational readiness, Sonali Srungaram

Sonali Srungaram, founder of a distributed cancer care company, spoke to the cohort as part of her guest lecture series. This recording covers the part of the session on operational readiness: cash flow, the move from founder to chief executive, co-founders and leadership, the professional advisers a company needs, employees, founder loneliness and infrastructure. She taught from her own experience and took questions throughout. The recording starts mid-session and ends at a break before she moved to numbers.

## What was said

### Cash flow and financial awareness

- Sonali Srungaram says cash flow matters more than profit and loss for a startup because it is a survival question: salaries, rent and the founder's family all have to be paid.
- She says a founder who sees themselves as a creative mind must also be a practical one, and should sit with their chartered accountant once a month or once a quarter until they understand the numbers.
- She says there are industry-specific metrics a founder should look at and must not forget.
- She says nobody gives her leniency for choosing a distributed model. She is measured against the benchmark of a traditional hospital.
- Her own target is that of every 100 rupees a customer pays, 80 should reach the bank and gross margin should be 60.
- She says her team reported strong customer-paid numbers while the amount reaching the bank was low, and she had every department change how it worked to close that gap.

### From founder to chief executive

- She says once 50 to 300 people's livelihoods depend on the company's direction, creativity alone is not enough and the founder has to become a chief executive.
- She describes the change as a modification over time and a maturing, not a sudden switch.
- She says the founder is the one who is passionate, and the question is what the chief executive should do about it.

### Co-founders and leadership

- She says not to rush into finding a co-founder. A founder spends 5 to 10 hours a day close to that person and needs to know they truly work well together.
- She says a co-founder leaving is not necessarily a bad sign. It hurts, and it also shows the founder's resilience.
- She says investors want continuity and want to know the person driving the company is genuinely at the helm.
- She says leadership changes are normal, but a leadership team that changes every year causes confusion and makes people run.
- She says the buck stops with the chief executive, who has to make the call even when others disagree.
- She says leadership style is the founder's choice, but clear direction and clear thought are required, and every founder's leadership will be tested.

### Solo and women founders

- She says her co-founder left within a year and she ran the company alone for six years.
- She says investors do question solo founders, and a founder would do the same in an investor's seat, so the answer is to show that the company's processes are strong.
- She says women founders get questions that men do not, such as how they will cope when a child is ill, and they should expect it and not act shocked.
- Her advice is to self-select: do not take money from people who lack confidence in the founder's ability.
- She says investors are concerned with their money, and a founder who returns it well will find the same investors holding her up as proof of their support for diversity.
- She says when anyone, investor or team member, genuinely questions a founder's leadership, the founder has to stand up and prove it.

### The four advisers

- She says a company needs four professionals: a chartered accountant, a tax consultant, a company secretary and a lawyer.
- She says the chartered accountant handles accounting and talks the founder through cash flow and the balance sheet.
- She says a tax consultant is separate from the accountant, and once the company has GST it needs a GST consultant too.
- She says the company secretary does the investor filings when a company raises funds, which the accountant cannot do, and money raised from outside India also needs Reserve Bank of India permission.
- She says a lawyer is someone to ask whether a new plan breaks any rules.
- She says these professionals often form an informal group that refers clients to one another.
- She says the group will not come together at once. A founder should start asking around early and ask other founders whom they use.
- She says some law firms position themselves as startup friendly, and which ones are available depends on the state.

### Choosing and keeping advisers

- She says to go in person and see where the adviser works, and choose someone who does not make the founder feel insignificant, spends time and gives real advice.
- She prefers advisers whose offices are busy, and would be wary of one whose office is always empty.
- She says advisers who charge less and give more time tend to be old school. They do not do video calls, and the founder has to travel and wait.
- She says she would not trust a lawyer who starts the clock the moment a client sits down. In her experience in India, clients pay for work done, not for a half-hour of advice.
- She says she would not use a tax consultant who does not keep the current bound tax volumes in the office and look up the section when asked.
- She says a founder who only emails the monthly figures and never goes through the books in person will find nobody else looks at them either.
- She says it takes two to three months to settle into a rhythm with an adviser, and after that the founder should still visit about every six months and keep up traditional courtesies.
- She says whenever a founder plans something different, they should take a day or two to visit all the advisers and hear what they say.
- She says an expensive lawyer is fine for a founder who can afford one and trusts them. Her own rule is not to spend money the company does not have.

### Company form and tax

- She says the company form, whether a sole proprietorship, a limited liability entity, a private limited company or a non-profit, should be chosen by how the founder intends to make revenue.
- She says in a sole proprietorship liability is personal and the founder's own assets can be taken.
- She says a founder can start as a sole proprietorship and convert later, but incorporating takes more than a month, so it does not work when investor money is needed quickly.
- She says investors prefer a private limited company, and may ask for incorporation their way. The founder should check that this does not compromise their own interests.
- She says the tax system makes a loss-making founder pay tax first and prove the loss later, with the refund coming about 18 months afterwards.

### Employees

- She says not to expect employees to take a pay cut because the founder is taking one. The founder's hard time is not their problem.
- She says to pick a salary date and keep it with the same discipline applied to investor money in the bank.
- She says her company could not pay on the first because of expense claims from travelling staff, so claims must be in by the 30th and are processed within 5 days, while salaries go out on time.
- She says people expect founders to be unprofessional, so a founder has to be extra disciplined, and bad news about funds is no excuse.
- She says a founder who has to close should give employees at least the minimum one month's notice, even though little work will get done in that month.
- She says the startup sector is small, and a founder who treated staff well can call them back for the next venture.
- She says the way to judge a good founder is by how long their employees have stayed.
- She says staying one or two years is a big deal in this sector, and 25% of her staff have been with her for more than 10 years.
- She says the founders made sure employees were paid even when they themselves took nothing home, and those employees work extra when things get hard.

### Why founders are lonely

- She compares being a founder to gambling. The founder takes a high-risk bet and, while waiting for it to pay off, keeps receiving signals that it will or will not work.
- She says the loneliness comes from constantly deciding who can be told what, which she finds very taxing.
- She says investors hear that things are going well, employees who hear too much bad news may leave, and family who opposed the decision are not people a founder wants to bring problems to.
- She says a founder ends up saying less and thinking more, to the point of dreaming about cash flow.
- She agrees with a remark from the room that the cohort is a sanitised, supportive setting. She says outside it people explain why the idea cannot work, and a founder has to learn to tune some of that out.
- She says "these are possibilities, be prepared for them", not a fate every founder meets.

### Whether to scale

- She says not every founder needs to scale into something large. Running a business that keeps the founder financially viable is a legitimate choice.
- She says a new model of what good looks like appears every few years and not every founder fits it.
- She says she compromised on some things to take investor money, and only on things she was willing to give up.
- She says a founder who wants growth needs employees, because a solo owner tires and earns nothing on days off.
- She suggests starting with one or two employees to find out what kind of boss the founder is.
- She says registering a company makes someone a founder on paper, and acting like a founder is a different matter.
- She says the need for approval comes from the founder, not from society.

### Infrastructure

- She says not to spend heavily on infrastructure for a minimum viable product, because the founder does not yet know how much is needed.
- Her alternatives are to rent, to use an older refurbished machine, or to outsource a production run.
- She says landlords may ask for a lock-in of 5 or 10 years, which means paying rent for that period whether the business runs or not.
- She says not to accept long lock-ins unless the company can afford them, including a one-year commitment to a seat or cabin in a networking hub.
- She says a less attractive place with flexibility is the better choice.
- She says the landlord of a site she lost in a district town preferred a tenant who paid cash up front over one who offered bank transfers after an agreement and legal vetting.
- She says after losing a site this way she began paying a one-month token deposit first and doing the paperwork afterwards.

## Examples and cases used

- Infosys: she recounts being told by co-founder S. D. Shibulal, an investor in her company along with Kris Gopalakrishnan, that the Infosys founders took turns as chief executive and each deferred to whoever held the role.
- Her own company: a week before closing on a site for a cancer centre in a district town, the landlord gave it to a cloth merchant who arrived with six months of rent in cash.
- Her own career: she left a role as associate partner at Accenture in the UK, one step from partner, to start the company, and is 13 years into it.
- Her advisers: she uses an accountant based in an older part of the city instead of a premium area, and refused a bill of 1.5 lakh rupees from a lawyer in a premium office.

## Numbers given

- 100, 80 and 60: her target that of 100 rupees a customer pays, 80 reaches the bank and gross margin is 60.
- 2,500 rupees a month: what her chartered accountant charges, plus a fee per filing and about 10% added on payments the accountant makes on her behalf.
- 5 to 10 hours: the time she says co-founders spend in close proximity each day.
- 25%: the share of her staff who have been with her for more than 10 years.
- 18 months: how long she says a tax refund takes after a loss-making founder pays first.
- 50,000 rupees and a second director: what she says it takes to incorporate and be a founder on paper.
