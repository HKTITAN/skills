# Failure and founders

This file answers three questions: why startups die, what the founders who make it do differently, and where a founder's attention should go. Paul Graham, Jessica Livingston, Harj Taggar, Michael Seibel and Sam Altman speak in it, beside one post signed Y Combinator. Read every line with its person, its stage and its date, because the lists were made years apart for founders at different stages, and money figures carry the year of their source.

## What kills startups?

- Graham's 2006 essay lists 18 mistakes and says the list is not complete: it covers only causes a founder can control and leaves out ineptitude and bad luck. (The 18 Mistakes That Kill Startups, https://paulgraham.com/startupmistakes.html)
- Graham's 2006 essay says the most common failed startup is not the spectacular flameout but the side project a couple of people ran beside their day jobs, which never got anywhere and was gradually abandoned. (The 18 Mistakes That Kill Startups, https://paulgraham.com/startupmistakes.html)
- Graham's 2006 essay ends on effort: most startups fail because they do not make something people want, most of those because the founders do not try hard enough, and he calls not trying hard enough the biggest mistake a founder can make. (The 18 Mistakes That Kill Startups, https://paulgraham.com/startupmistakes.html)
- Graham's April 2006 essay says he has seen a lot of startups die from releasing too slowly and none from releasing too quickly. (The Hardest Lessons for Startups to Learn, https://paulgraham.com/startuplessons.html)
- Graham's October 2006 essay allows that launching too early is possible and can ruin a reputation, since early adopters who try something bad may never come back, while guessing that slow launches have killed a hundred times more startups. (The 18 Mistakes That Kill Startups, https://paulgraham.com/startupmistakes.html)
- Graham's April 2006 essay says far more startups wreck themselves than are crushed by competitors, names internal disputes, inertia and ignoring users as the three main ways, and picks ignoring users as the worst. (The Hardest Lessons for Startups to Learn, https://paulgraham.com/startuplessons.html)
- Graham's 2006 essay treats visible disasters as normal: a founder quitting, a patent that covers the product, crashing servers or a deal falling through will not kill a startup unless the founders let them. (The Hardest Lessons for Startups to Learn, https://paulgraham.com/startuplessons.html)
- The Y Combinator post says startups always die by their own doing and not a competitor's, calls time spent worrying about competitors nearly always a very bad idea, and says competition is highly unlikely to decide the outcome in the first year or two. (YC's essential startup advice, https://www.ycombinator.com/library/4D-yc-s-essential-startup-advice)
- Graham's 2006 essay says a founder leaving does not necessarily kill a startup: plenty of successful ones had it happen, and it is usually the least committed founder who goes. (The 18 Mistakes That Kill Startups, https://paulgraham.com/startupmistakes.html)
- The Y Combinator post says founders falling out is why most companies fail fast, and that relationships between cofounders matter more than founders think. (YC's essential startup advice, https://www.ycombinator.com/library/4D-yc-s-essential-startup-advice)
- Graham's 2006 essay reports that, as of its writing, few startups spent too much and none of the ones his firm had funded had, and it names hiring a lot of people as the classic way to burn through cash. (The 18 Mistakes That Kill Startups, https://paulgraham.com/startupmistakes.html)
- Livingston, in a talk the library dates to 2016, says startups run out of money by spending too much, that salaries are the main expense so overspending means overhiring, and that Y Combinator startups make this mistake constantly despite constant warnings. (How not to fail, https://www.ycombinator.com/library/5l-how-not-to-fail)
- Livingston, in a talk the library dates to 2016, ranks by phase: making something no one wants is the big mistake of a startup's first phase and overhiring is the big mistake of its second. (How not to fail, https://www.ycombinator.com/library/5l-how-not-to-fail)
- Livingston, in a talk the library dates to 2016, calls denial "the silent killer of startups": founders making the wrong thing, or wasting time on inessential things, are often in denial about it, and she says a founder held to growth targets cannot stay in denial. (How not to fail, https://www.ycombinator.com/library/5l-how-not-to-fail)
- The Y Combinator post's pocket list states that most companies do not die because they run out of money. (YC's essential startup advice, https://www.ycombinator.com/library/4D-yc-s-essential-startup-advice)
- Taggar, in a talk the library dates to 2021, works backwards: startups fail because founders give up, founders give up because money or energy or both run out, and those run out because the startup has no users or too few to convince investors or the founders themselves that it deserves more. (Why do startups fail?, https://www.ycombinator.com/library/9u-why-do-startups-fail)
- Taggar, in a talk the library dates to 2021, traces having no users to not building something people want, and says the most common way he has seen that happen is not talking to users and building what the founders think users want. (Why do startups fail?, https://www.ycombinator.com/library/9u-why-do-startups-fail)
- Altman, in a talk the library dates to 2014, says what kills startups at some level is the founders giving up: quitting when they should not, by mismanaging their own psychology, is what he calls the final cause of death for most startups, though he grants that sometimes a founder should quit. (Later stage advice for startups, https://www.ycombinator.com/library/4q-later-stage-advice-for-startups)
- Seibel, in a talk the library dates to 2019, says a lot of startups that fail do so because the founders lose motivation to keep working on the company, and that much of this comes from not really liking the problem they picked at the start. (Biggest mistakes first-time founders make, https://www.ycombinator.com/library/66-biggest-mistakes-first-time-founders-make)
- Seibel, in a talk the library dates to 2020, says the vast majority of companies that raise money at demo day still die, and about 70% never find any form of product market fit. (The 5 things that kill startups post seed rounds, https://www.ycombinator.com/library/8a-the-5-things-that-kill-startups-post-seed-rounds)
- Seibel, in a talk the library dates to 2020, lists five killers of companies that have raised a seed round, in this order: fake product market fit, turning the investor into the boss, co-founder conflict, copying the startups around you while expecting a massive success, and slow product development. (The 5 things that kill startups post seed rounds, https://www.ycombinator.com/library/8a-the-5-things-that-kill-startups-post-seed-rounds)

## Is there one mistake underneath the rest?

- Graham's 2006 essay says that in a sense only one mistake kills startups, not making something users want, and that his list is really 18 things that cause it, since nearly all failure funnels through that one. (The 18 Mistakes That Kill Startups, https://paulgraham.com/startupmistakes.html)
- Livingston, in a talk the library dates to 2016, says nothing else a founder does will matter without making something people want: the best spokesperson, fundraiser or programmer never succeeds with a product that meets no real need. (How not to fail, https://www.ycombinator.com/library/5l-how-not-to-fail)
- Livingston says the way to stay on the right path in the early stages of a startup is to build things and talk to users, and to do nothing else. (How not to fail, https://www.ycombinator.com/library/5l-how-not-to-fail)
- Graham's 2006 essay warns against having no specific user in mind: founders building for a group they do not belong to must be able to talk some specific members of it into using the product, and if they cannot, they are on the wrong track. (The 18 Mistakes That Kill Startups, https://paulgraham.com/startupmistakes.html)
- Graham's 2006 essay says not to get attached to the original plan, since most successful startups end up doing something different, but that switching ideas every week is equally fatal, and it offers a test: whether each new idea reuses most of what was built for the earlier ones. (The 18 Mistakes That Kill Startups, https://paulgraham.com/startupmistakes.html)
- Taggar says the version of this mistake more common among technical founders is rewriting the code to be prettier, more elegant and faster in place of talking to users, who care only whether the product solves a real problem for them. (Why do startups fail?, https://www.ycombinator.com/library/9u-why-do-startups-fail)
- Taggar says creative founders cite Steve Jobs as a reason not to talk to users, and answers that such a founder is likely not Jobs and that Jobs spent his whole life thinking deeply about how consumers interact with technology and products. (Why do startups fail?, https://www.ycombinator.com/library/9u-why-do-startups-fail)
- Taggar compares the founder to a doctor and users to patients: always listen to what they say their symptoms are, and never let them write the prescription, which is the founder's job. (Why do startups fail?, https://www.ycombinator.com/library/9u-why-do-startups-fail)
- Altman, in a talk the library dates to 2018, says a startup's success tracks how far it builds a product so good that people tell their friends unprompted, puts that at 80% of the work of being a really successful startup, and says founders want the secret to be something else because this is hard. (How to succeed with a startup, https://www.ycombinator.com/library/89-how-to-succeed-with-a-startup)
- Altman, in a post, says many founders hurt their companies by focusing on growth too soon: pushing growth before the product is loved gives a leaky bucket, where users come in the door and do not stay. (Before growing your startup, https://www.ycombinator.com/library/4p-before-growing-your-startup)
- The Y Combinator post says growth is the natural result of talking to users, building what they want and iterating quickly, and that growing before making something customers want makes little sense because poor retention always follows. (YC's essential startup advice, https://www.ycombinator.com/library/4D-yc-s-essential-startup-advice)

## Where should my attention go, and what is a distraction?

- Livingston, in a talk the library dates to 2016, says one of the most conspicuous patterns across the thousand startups funded is that the most successful founders are totally focused on their product and their users, to the point of being fanatical. (How not to fail, https://www.ycombinator.com/library/5l-how-not-to-fail)
- Livingston lists what she sees distract founders: coffee with investors, talking with potential acquirers, networking, recruiting boards of advisors, partnerships done in the hope of users, PR before the product is wanted, arguing on social media and going to conferences. (How not to fail, https://www.ycombinator.com/library/5l-how-not-to-fail)
- Livingston, speaking to female founders, puts worrying about being a woman in tech on the same list: she says it will be harder for a woman, but not so much harder that it decides success or failure, and advises not being intimidated or distracted by the noise. (How not to fail, https://www.ycombinator.com/library/5l-how-not-to-fail)
- Livingston says growth is the test of her first two rules: a startup that makes something people want and stays focused grows, a good rate as she spoke it is 10% a month, and a startup without it is either making the wrong thing or not focusing enough. (How not to fail, https://www.ycombinator.com/library/5l-how-not-to-fail)
- Seibel, in a talk the library dates to 2019, says first-time founders often prioritize "sizzle over steak", putting press, hiring, conferences and investors above getting product out and talking to users, which he describes as pretending to be a startup. (Biggest mistakes first-time founders make, https://www.ycombinator.com/library/66-biggest-mistakes-first-time-founders-make)
- The Y Combinator post says that as companies begin to grow the distractions multiply, naming conferences, dinners, meetings with venture capitalists or corporate development people and chasing press, and that writing code and talking to users should take almost all of an early-stage founder's time. (YC's essential startup advice, https://www.ycombinator.com/library/4D-yc-s-essential-startup-advice)
- The Y Combinator post reports Altman's name for avoiding the hard, ambitious problems and working on other things: fake work, which he says tends to be more fun than real work. (YC's essential startup advice, https://www.ycombinator.com/library/4D-yc-s-essential-startup-advice)
- The Y Combinator post's pocket list says to avoid conferences unless they are the best way to get customers, and to avoid corporate development queries from big companies because they only waste time. (YC's essential startup advice, https://www.ycombinator.com/library/4D-yc-s-essential-startup-advice)
- The Y Combinator post says a founder's instinct will always be to do more, while the best strategy is almost always to do less and do it really well. (YC's essential startup advice, https://www.ycombinator.com/library/4D-yc-s-essential-startup-advice)
- Altman, in a talk the library dates to 2014, says the most common way companies fail after Y Combinator is that founders who were incredibly focused during the program start doing a lot of other things afterwards, such as advising companies and going to conferences. (Later stage advice for startups, https://www.ycombinator.com/library/4q-later-stage-advice-for-startups)
- Altman, in a talk the library dates to 2014, reads loss of focus as a symptom of burnout: a founder burned out on running the business wants easier, more gratifying things, like conferences where people say how great the founder is. (Later stage advice for startups, https://www.ycombinator.com/library/4q-later-stage-advice-for-startups)
- Graham's 2006 essay tells founders to assume any deal in progress will not happen, whether an investment, an acquisition or a big customer, and to keep running the company as if the deal did not exist, so the company is not leaning on something that falls over. (The Hardest Lessons for Startups to Learn, https://paulgraham.com/startuplessons.html)
- Graham's 2006 essay says the way to succeed is to focus on getting lots of users and keep walking swiftly toward that goal while investors and acquirers hurry alongside offering money. (The Hardest Lessons for Startups to Learn, https://paulgraham.com/startuplessons.html)

## What did the successful founders have in common?

- Livingston, in a talk the library dates to 2017, lists nine things that separate massively successful startups from merely successful ones, and starts with luck: the biggest are lucky, timing is one of the most important kinds of luck and founders control it less than they might think, but none is merely lucky. (What's different about unicorns?, https://www.ycombinator.com/library/8L-what-s-different-about-unicorns)
- Livingston says founders of the super-successful startups are never in it mainly to get rich or to seem cool: they are fanatically interested in what the company does, so they work harder, think longer term and are much harder to capture with an acquisition offer. (What's different about unicorns?, https://www.ycombinator.com/library/8L-what-s-different-about-unicorns)
- Livingston says a huge startup needs a huge market but market sizes are impossible to predict, so the advice she reports giving at Y Combinator is not even to try for a big market early: work on something you want yourself and hope there are many more people like you. (What's different about unicorns?, https://www.ycombinator.com/library/8L-what-s-different-about-unicorns)
- Livingston says the biggest startups do something basic enough to explain to a five-year-old in a few words, and warns against using that as a test for what to work on, because ideas often start less general: Facebook began as a site for a couple thousand Harvard students. (What's different about unicorns?, https://www.ycombinator.com/library/8L-what-s-different-about-unicorns)
- Livingston says almost all the really big startup ideas seemed dubious at first and looked to most people like bad ideas, and admits she did not think much of Airbnb's idea when it was her job to judge it. (What's different about unicorns?, https://www.ycombinator.com/library/8L-what-s-different-about-unicorns)
- Livingston says working on an idea that looks bad takes an independent-minded person who does not care what others think, and that she cannot think of one successful founder she would describe as a conformist. (What's different about unicorns?, https://www.ycombinator.com/library/8L-what-s-different-about-unicorns)
- Livingston says ambition shows when an unpromising idea blossoms into a terrifyingly big one: most people react with fear, and a few are more excited than afraid. (What's different about unicorns?, https://www.ycombinator.com/library/8L-what-s-different-about-unicorns)
- Livingston says every founder of the really huge startups has exceptional drive, and that drive is hard to spot beforehand because it can be suppressed under authority, as in most schools or a job, where driven people may look less promising than merely obedient ones. (What's different about unicorns?, https://www.ycombinator.com/library/8L-what-s-different-about-unicorns)
- Livingston says drive alone is not enough: in all the really huge startups the company is the life's work of at least one founder, which is why they would never willingly be acquired. (What's different about unicorns?, https://www.ycombinator.com/library/8L-what-s-different-about-unicorns)
- Livingston says a founder who wants to keep running a startup that gets really big has to become a manager, that management can be learned on the job, and that the founder has to be able to learn it and, in her view, even has to like it. (What's different about unicorns?, https://www.ycombinator.com/library/8L-what-s-different-about-unicorns)
- Altman, in a talk the library dates to 2018, reports that Paul Buchheit looked at the traits of the best founders Y Combinator had funded and came up with four: frugality, focus, obsession and love. (How to succeed with a startup, https://www.ycombinator.com/library/89-how-to-succeed-with-a-startup)
- Seibel, in a talk the library dates to 2020, answers a question about what makes startups great with excellent technical talent first, meaning the ability to build software together with not being intimidated by building things never built before, and setting aggressive goals second. (The 5 things that kill startups post seed rounds, https://www.ycombinator.com/library/8a-the-5-things-that-kill-startups-post-seed-rounds)
- Altman, in a talk the library dates to 2014, says most founders think about a year ahead and expect to sell in three, so the few who make a real long-term commitment have a huge advantage, and he suggests building a strategy that assumes ten more years of work. (Later stage advice for startups, https://www.ycombinator.com/library/4q-later-stage-advice-for-startups)

## What do resourcefulness and determination look like?

- Graham's 2009 essay reduces being a good startup founder to two words, relentlessly resourceful, and names the opposite quality hapless, which he says implies passivity: being battered by circumstances. (Relentlessly Resourceful, https://paulgraham.com/relres.html)
- Graham's 2009 essay says being relentless is not enough: in any interesting domain the difficulties are novel, a founder cannot tell at first whether the obstacle is foam or granite, and so has to keep trying new things. (Relentlessly Resourceful, https://paulgraham.com/relres.html)
- Graham's 2009 essay says that after four years of trying to teach the quality, it can be taught surprisingly often, though not to everyone, and particularly to young people who have always been under some kind of authority. (Relentlessly Resourceful, https://paulgraham.com/relres.html)
- Graham's 2009 essay offers the phrase as a test, for whether you are the right sort of person to start a startup and whether to recruit someone as a cofounder, and says making something people want is the destination while being relentlessly resourceful is how you get there. (Relentlessly Resourceful, https://paulgraham.com/relres.html)
- Graham's 2006 essay says the most important quality in a startup founder is determination and not intelligence. (The Hardest Lessons for Startups to Learn, https://paulgraham.com/startuplessons.html)
- Graham's 2006 essay calls commitment a self-fulfilling prophecy: a team that plans to try a startup for three months and stay only if something great happens is very unlikely to see it, because acquirers and investors judge founders by their commitment. (The Hardest Lessons for Startups to Learn, https://paulgraham.com/startuplessons.html)
- Graham's 2006 essay separates determination from stubbornness, which it calls a disastrous quality in a startup: a founder should be determined but flexible, like a running back who improvises and never stands still. (The Hardest Lessons for Startups to Learn, https://paulgraham.com/startuplessons.html)
- Graham's 2006 essay says hackers cannot just hack: in the first batch funded, in the summer of 2005, one founder was away half the time talking to executives at cell phone companies, and that startup seemed the most successful of the group by an order of magnitude. (The 18 Mistakes That Kill Startups, https://paulgraham.com/startupmistakes.html)
- Seibel, in a talk the library dates to 2020, says the failure rate of startups is so high that being average among smart, motivated people is not enough, and that founders should treat the people around them as the floor and not the ceiling. (The 5 things that kill startups post seed rounds, https://www.ycombinator.com/library/8a-the-5-things-that-kill-startups-post-seed-rounds)
- Seibel says being extraordinary is not decided by birth: citing the book Atomic Habits, he says the people who are more effective do not find hard things easier, they have trained themselves to be better at doing hard things. (The 5 things that kill startups post seed rounds, https://www.ycombinator.com/library/8a-the-5-things-that-kill-startups-post-seed-rounds)
- Seibel says to challenge yourself with a business goal you do not know how you will achieve, and to aim 15% above a number you think you can hit. (The 5 things that kill startups post seed rounds, https://www.ycombinator.com/library/8a-the-5-things-that-kill-startups-post-seed-rounds)
- Seibel tells how the Airbnb founders, told by Paul Graham in January 2009 to try to become profitable, worked out the revenue they needed, which Seibel recalls as $4,000 a month, set a number for every week until Demo Day, posted the graph all over their apartment and got there before Demo Day. (The 5 things that kill startups post seed rounds, https://www.ycombinator.com/library/8a-the-5-things-that-kill-startups-post-seed-rounds)

## Which mistakes do first-time founders make?

- Seibel, in a talk the library dates to 2019, starts his list for a company's first year with choosing a problem the founders do not care about: not fatal, he says, since many founders learn to love the problem later, but the work asks for something like five years or more of their lives. (Biggest mistakes first-time founders make, https://www.ycombinator.com/library/66-biggest-mistakes-first-time-founders-make)
- Seibel's second mistake is helping users the founders do not care about: he says the Justin.tv team was not terribly in love with the people using the platform, and regained that love only when Emmett refocused the company on video game streaming, which he calls one of the core drivers of Twitch's success. (Biggest mistakes first-time founders make, https://www.ycombinator.com/library/66-biggest-mistakes-first-time-founders-make)
- Seibel says first-time founders choose co-founders they do not know well and then avoid transparent conversations about performance, goals and who does which job, so resentment builds and the eventual conversation turns into a fight. (Biggest mistakes first-time founders make, https://www.ycombinator.com/library/66-biggest-mistakes-first-time-founders-make)
- Seibel says founders put off launching because they fear the exposure, and points out that most people do not remember the day Snapchat, Instagram, WhatsApp, Uber or Lyft launched: a launch matters far less to users than to the founders, so it should be moved up. (Biggest mistakes first-time founders make, https://www.ycombinator.com/library/66-biggest-mistakes-first-time-founders-make)
- Seibel says most consumer and B2B startups he encounters could build and launch some form of MVP in under a month, with exceptions in extremely regulated markets such as banking or lending. (Biggest mistakes first-time founders make, https://www.ycombinator.com/library/66-biggest-mistakes-first-time-founders-make)
- Seibel lists not using analytics, meaning not measuring what users do when they come to the site, as a mistake he sees time and time again. (Biggest mistakes first-time founders make, https://www.ycombinator.com/library/66-biggest-mistakes-first-time-founders-make)
- Seibel says founders who have no idea where their first users will come from chose a problem where they know no one who has it: the first one to five users should be people the founders know or have already identified, and only the first hundred or thousand might need deep digging. (Biggest mistakes first-time founders make, https://www.ycombinator.com/library/66-biggest-mistakes-first-time-founders-make)
- Seibel adds a caveat to his own list: he bets some startup has made every one of these mistakes and still succeeded, but says those tend to be exceptions. (Biggest mistakes first-time founders make, https://www.ycombinator.com/library/66-biggest-mistakes-first-time-founders-make)
- Graham's 2006 essay says most groups applying to Y Combinator pick a small, obscure niche hoping to avoid competition, and that competition can be avoided only by avoiding good ideas. (The 18 Mistakes That Kill Startups, https://paulgraham.com/startupmistakes.html)

## What kills a company after the seed round?

- Seibel, in a talk the library dates to 2020, says it is extremely common for companies to claim product market fit when they have nothing close to it, and calls this one of the most common symptoms of impending death for post-seed companies. (The 5 things that kill startups post seed rounds, https://www.ycombinator.com/library/8a-the-5-things-that-kill-startups-post-seed-rounds)
- Seibel gives four causes of fake product market fit: treating money from impressive investors as proof, raising a Series A before the product is loved, magical thinking that ignores or never measures churn and payback period, and weak technical talent that makes it easier to believe the product is good than to improve it. (The 5 things that kill startups post seed rounds, https://www.ycombinator.com/library/8a-the-5-things-that-kill-startups-post-seed-rounds)
- Seibel says real product market fit shows in numbers more than in a concept: enough people are using and keeping the product that parts not built for scale start to break, and those users have the economics the company wants. (The 5 things that kill startups post seed rounds, https://www.ycombinator.com/library/8a-the-5-things-that-kill-startups-post-seed-rounds)
- Seibel's signs of fake product market fit are lots of hiring, with a team going from 4 to 12, more business people than engineers, no metrics dashboards, spending on nice things, flat graphs, reasons why missed estimates are fine, and changed KPIs. (The 5 things that kill startups post seed rounds, https://www.ycombinator.com/library/8a-the-5-things-that-kill-startups-post-seed-rounds)
- Seibel's remedies start with picking one honest KPI and sticking with it, almost always revenue and especially for SaaS, tracking retention carefully, and capping burn: before product market fit, fix a monthly amount of raised money to spend and fund anything above it from revenue. (The 5 things that kill startups post seed rounds, https://www.ycombinator.com/library/8a-the-5-things-that-kill-startups-post-seed-rounds)
- Seibel, in a talk the library dates to 2020, says that all things being equal raising more than a company needs leads to less success: a larger seed raises what a Series A investor expects, and he has yet to consistently meet founders who can keep from spending more when more is in the bank. (The 5 things that kill startups post seed rounds, https://www.ycombinator.com/library/8a-the-5-things-that-kill-startups-post-seed-rounds)
- Graham's 2006 essay says the trouble with raising too much is what comes with the money: investors want it put to work, the company hires, and it gets harder to change direction because the more people a company has, the more it stays pointed the same way. (The 18 Mistakes That Kill Startups, https://paulgraham.com/startupmistakes.html)
- Seibel, in a talk the library dates to 2020, says a significant number of companies could then raise five to ten million before having something people love, and that more often than not the founder then shifts from users and product to company building, which he says is typically the wrong move. (The 5 things that kill startups post seed rounds, https://www.ycombinator.com/library/8a-the-5-things-that-kill-startups-post-seed-rounds)
- Seibel calls turning an investor into a boss a very easy way to die, and traces it to fear and self-doubt, the false belief that an investor's past success is a path that can be repeated, and no longer talking to customers. (The 5 things that kill startups post seed rounds, https://www.ycombinator.com/library/8a-the-5-things-that-kill-startups-post-seed-rounds)
- Seibel's signs that an investor has become the boss: pressure to spend more than the founder wants, hiring faster than planned, a recruiter hired before product market fit, burn rising while the primary KPI does not, closing off communication with other investors, and believing the investor will backstop the company if the plan is followed. (The 5 things that kill startups post seed rounds, https://www.ycombinator.com/library/8a-the-5-things-that-kill-startups-post-seed-rounds)
- Seibel says the most important remedy is knowing that founders are the ones who give investors power over the business, since all an investor can do is use words. (The 5 things that kill startups post seed rounds, https://www.ycombinator.com/library/8a-the-5-things-that-kill-startups-post-seed-rounds)
- Graham's 2006 essay says founders should neither ignore investors nor let them run the company, and says angering them by ignoring them is, in his estimate, less dangerous than caving in to them. (The 18 Mistakes That Kill Startups, https://paulgraham.com/startupmistakes.html)
- Seibel says slow product development is a way he sees post-seed startups die constantly, because they take fewer shots on goal, and lists causes: no process for deciding what to build, no sprints or deadlines, no written specs, engineers left out of product decisions, no metrics and no more contact with customers. (The 5 things that kill startups post seed rounds, https://www.ycombinator.com/library/8a-the-5-things-that-kill-startups-post-seed-rounds)
- Seibel has a name for product founders who believe they know what the customer wants without ever talking to one, fake Steve Jobs, and counts them among the causes of slow product development. (The 5 things that kill startups post seed rounds, https://www.ycombinator.com/library/8a-the-5-things-that-kill-startups-post-seed-rounds)
- Livingston, in a talk the library dates to 2016, says later rounds are much harder than a seed round: seed investors look for promise and Series A investors for performance, so a startup halfway along the right path looks to them much like one on the wrong path. (How not to fail, https://www.ycombinator.com/library/5l-how-not-to-fail)
- Livingston, in a talk the library dates to 2016, says the critical question is whether a startup reaches breakeven before the money runs out if expenses stay the same and revenue keeps growing at its current rate, which she calls default alive, and that a surprising number of founders do not know which they are. (How not to fail, https://www.ycombinator.com/library/5l-how-not-to-fail)
- Seibel closes by telling founders who have raised seed rounds that most competitors will not do most of the things in his talk, and his last advice is "don't let anyone beat you on the basics". (The 5 things that kill startups post seed rounds, https://www.ycombinator.com/library/8a-the-5-things-that-kill-startups-post-seed-rounds)

## What changes when things start to work?

- Altman, in a talk the library dates to 2018, says all successful founders go through a switch from building a product to building a company, and that building a company is really about the team. (How to succeed with a startup, https://www.ycombinator.com/library/89-how-to-succeed-with-a-startup)
- Altman, in a talk the library dates to 2014, calls entertaining acquisition talks one of the biggest killers of companies that are starting to do well: the talks distract, a deal that does not happen demoralizes, and a founder who already feels done takes a low offer, so his rule is not to start a conversation unless a pretty low price would be acceptable. (Later stage advice for startups, https://www.ycombinator.com/library/4q-later-stage-advice-for-startups)
- Altman, in a post for founders whose company is good but not giant, writes "Companies get bought, not sold" and says an acquisition is very difficult to force, yet beginning to build relationships with potential acquirers is a good idea because it usually takes a long time. (Navigating success for your startup, https://www.ycombinator.com/library/4s-navigating-success-for-your-startup)
- Altman, in a post, compares a startup to assembling a bag of parts while falling: every once in a while they make a spaceship, much of the time nothing, in which case the founder should try to hit the ground as gently as possible, and a fair amount of the time an airplane. (Navigating success for your startup, https://www.ycombinator.com/library/4s-navigating-success-for-your-startup)
- Altman, in an undated post, defines a really good airplane as a company with profitability within reach and on track to be worth $100 million after several more years of hard work, and calls that a triumph by almost any metric. (Navigating success for your startup, https://www.ycombinator.com/library/4s-navigating-success-for-your-startup)
- Altman says this is where founders and investors often become misaligned: many investors are looking for spaceships and will usually accept almost any amount of risk to see whether an airplane can become one. (Navigating success for your startup, https://www.ycombinator.com/library/4s-navigating-success-for-your-startup)
- Altman advises being frank and realistic with existing investors, who he says can eventually be persuaded that a small multiple beats a loss, and warns that the longer a founder deludes them the worse the position gets. (Navigating success for your startup, https://www.ycombinator.com/library/4s-navigating-success-for-your-startup)
- Altman says raising too much, or at too high a valuation, can severely limit a founder's options and often rules out a good acquisition later, so a founder who thinks the company will not grow another 10x in value and would be happy to sell soon should not raise a lot at a high price. (Navigating success for your startup, https://www.ycombinator.com/library/4s-navigating-success-for-your-startup)
- Altman, in a talk the library dates to 2014, says most startups fail, that Silicon Valley almost goes too far in loving failure, and that a failing founder should tell investors and not run the company completely out of money. (Later stage advice for startups, https://www.ycombinator.com/library/4q-later-stage-advice-for-startups)
- Altman, in a talk the library dates to 2014, says of founder psychology "It gets worse, not better": as the company grows the highs are better and the lows keep getting worse. (Later stage advice for startups, https://www.ycombinator.com/library/4q-later-stage-advice-for-startups)
- Livingston, in a talk the library dates to 2016, calls her list of mistakes pretty complete, and says a founder who makes something people want, focuses on making users happy, keeps a good growth rate and does not overhire will be master of their own fate. (How not to fail, https://www.ycombinator.com/library/5l-how-not-to-fail)
