# Marketing myopia and coherence in go-to-market strategy

A marketing class taught by a course instructor who is not named in the recording and who shares the course with a second instructor. He discusses the Marketing Myopia article, gives the class four diagnostic questions to answer for their own ventures, then presents a go-to-market framework whose central idea is coherence, using airlines, Vanguard and Zerodha.

## What was said

### Marketing myopia

- The instructor says Marketing Myopia was written in the 1960s and changed how people thought about what business a company is in.
- He says oil and gas companies now think of themselves as energy companies.
- His railways example is that railways thought their business was trains, when what customers want is transportation.
- He says Microsoft thought of itself as making software for computers and laptops, and so missed mobile. Had it thought of itself as a software company across all platforms, it would have watched those platforms evolve.
- The key idea, in his words, is that "your business is more than your product".
- He says firms put their time and energy into what they currently do and so miss future trends.
- The goal he states is to satisfy customers, not to sell products, and he says every product in an industry will be replaced by something sooner or later.

### Four diagnostic questions for a venture

- The first question is need versus product. Could a different technology solve the same customer problem better?
- He uses technology in a broad sense. To him the wheel is a technology, and so is anything that uses tools to change how things operate.
- If the venture already uses a technology and adds something on top, he says, then that technology alone does not solve the problem better and the answer is no.
- The second question is a substitute scan. What would put us out of business if it took off?
- The third is the business definition. Finish the sentence "We are in the business of" without naming a product.
- The fourth is learning loops. How often do we test our assumptions with real users, as opposed to improving the current offering?
- He answers the four for a business school. The need is business knowledge and research that help people do better in business, and classroom education is only the current way of meeting it.
- For the substitute scan he names personalised tutoring and online offerings that give each person an individual setup.
- He defines the school's business as creating business leaders through business knowledge and research, and says that definition lets the delivery change over time.
- Testing assumptions with real users does not have to mean talking to customers, he says. It can be done with data, by checking whether the outcome you claim is really being produced and what is driving it.
- He advises writing down the assumptions clearly, because the KPIs you track are the test of whether those assumptions held.

### A go-to-market framework

- His framework is a loop. Decisions lead to outcomes, outcomes lead to analysis, and analysis feeds the next decisions.
- The decisions are what to offer, which is the positioning or value proposition, whom to offer it to, which is segmentation and targeting, and how to offer it, which is the four Ps of price, product, place and promotion.
- He says marketing strategy comes down to one question. Why should the customer buy your product?
- The outcomes can be market share or profit, or social impact for a non-profit. In the short term he looks at sales, market share and profit, and in the long term at retention, brand equity, social impact and environmental impact.
- The analysis is the five Cs: customer, company, competition, collaborators and the environment.
- Under company he puts internal capabilities and the choice between hiring people and outsourcing.
- He says competition is often not based on the product alone. Swiggy competes with Zomato and also with the customer's own kitchen, so ask what customers would do if your product were not there.
- Collaborators are the parties who help fulfil the value proposition. He says Zomato runs a large back end that helps restaurants manage their supply chain.
- Environment covers the cultural, technological and macroeconomic factors and the rules and regulations that could help or hinder.

### Coherence

- He says the key factor for a go-to-market strategy to succeed is coherence, meaning the what, who and how decisions all align.
- The checks he lists are whether the value proposition suits the segment picked, whether the firm has the capabilities to deliver it, whether the distribution channels are right, and where and with what content it promotes.
- A low cost manufacturer should never target the high end, he says, because that segment does not care about low cost.
- A high quality product should not be in every kirana store. It belongs in selected outlets that give a sense of exclusivity.
- He says anyone going for the high end has to offer experiences, because the moment a price tag can be put on what you give, you are no longer exclusive.
- A firm that starts offering 10 or 20 percent discounts has, in his words, lost the game and become a discount player.
- For acquiring early customers he suggests framing. Set a high price and give an introductory discount, while making it clear that the discount is temporary and does not define you.

### The airline value map

- He uses a value map that scores low cost and legacy carriers on the same attributes, with four moves: eliminate, reduce, increase and create.
- For a low cost carrier he rates a worldwide network, a wide choice of distribution, seat selection, business class, seat spacing, in-flight meals and frequent flyer miles as low.
- He rates new aircraft, for fuel efficiency, punctuality and refunds when the plane is late as high for a low cost carrier, because its customers are price conscious and treat the ticket as a transaction.
- He says IndiGo is trying to move from low cost towards a legacy position, and that this will be a very large transformation because the airline was not built that way.
- He says IndiGo's loyalty programme is cost based, while legacy carriers' loyalty programmes offer upgrades and experiences.
- The attributes on a value map come from customer research, he says, and for airlines many surveys make them clear.
- His Ryanair example is its proposal to cut bathrooms on its planes. He defends the logic. Removing them makes room for at least two more rows, most Ryanair flights are short haul, passengers can use a bathroom before boarding, and emptying bathroom waste is a large part of turnaround time.
- He says that is what coherence means. The idea sounds preposterous but it is in line with the core idea of low cost, which asks what more can be eliminated.

### Vanguard and Zerodha

- He says Vanguard's product is index funds but its value proposition is lowest cost, and that it chose to make money by reducing cost.
- He quotes Vanguard's message as: investors cannot control markets, but they can control the cost of investing.
- For Vanguard, he says, low cost is not a pricing strategy. It is how they do business.
- He describes Vanguard's target as disciplined, cost conscious, long-term investors who want to get rich slowly, plus institutional clients who want their money to be safe.
- On the four Ps he says Vanguard offers very few products with very few actively managed funds, has the lowest price in the industry, advertises very little, and uses no third-party distribution and no sales outlets.
- He contrasts this with firms that keep glamorous offices and relationship managers for wealthy clients, all of which are extra costs.
- He says Vanguard stayed true to its core when it expanded, adding ETFs, international ETFs and a low cost advisory service that mixes technology with human advisers.
- Asked where Vanguard could expand, he says India is not a fit, because Vanguard's two segments, retirement savers and large institutional investors, are not big enough there, and he names Japan as a better fit.
- He presents Zerodha as the Indian parallel. Its value proposition is low cost, its customers are retail traders with low ticket sizes and large volumes, it does no advertising and it has no retail branches, unlike bank-owned brokerages.
- He says Zerodha's interface is not easy and has a learning cost, because making interfaces easy and customisable raises cost.

## Examples and cases used

- Railways, oil and gas companies, Microsoft: firms that defined themselves by a product and not by the customer need.
- A business school answering the four diagnostic questions: the business is creating business leaders, and classroom teaching is one way of delivering it.
- Swiggy and Zomato: competition includes the customer's own kitchen, and collaborators can be served with a supply chain back end.
- IndiGo against legacy carriers: a value map shows how far a low cost carrier has to change to move upmarket.
- Ryanair: the proposal to remove bathrooms as coherence with a low cost position.
- Vanguard: every one of the four Ps aligned with lowest cost.
- Zerodha: the same low cost coherence in India.

## Numbers given

- Vanguard's cost: the instructor gives 25 basis points in 2003 and roughly 11 basis points in 2018, with its biggest fund at 3 basis points.
- Vanguard's assets under management: he gives about 435 billion dollars in 1998, 3.4 trillion in 2015 and 8.6 trillion in 2023.
- Vanguard's market share: he gives over 30 percent in 2023.
- Ryanair: he puts the fare saving from removing bathrooms at about 20 pounds or 20 euros and the space gained at about six seats.
