# Marketing bootcamp day 2, content, founder brand and low-cost marketing, Chandan Mendiratta

Chandan Mendiratta of Zepto ran the second day of a two-day marketing bootcamp. The first part was open questions from founders about marketing their own businesses. He then taught founder-led content, the LinkedIn playbook he uses at Zepto, low-cost executions, co-funded films, brand team culture and agencies. The day closed with three teams presenting marketing plans for made-up products and his comments on them.

## What was said

### B2B marketing is education

- Mendiratta says B2B selling is problem solving: educate the buyer on the answer to their problem, show what can be done about it, and then show that your product fits there.
- He says a B2B customer never wants a product, so you have to establish that the buyer will make more money than the product costs.
- He says there is always scepticism in B2B and SaaS buying, and the best way to know which problem to address is to talk to a lot of customers.
- He names eWebinar as a tool he likes: linked to a website, it tells a visitor that a webinar is starting in three minutes. He says the webinar should address a problem, and that content funnels down to the product, with a sales team as well.
- He says B2B is about building content such as blogs and videos, and that click campaigns do not work for it.
- He says many B2B buyers now ask ChatGPT how to solve a problem instead of searching on Google, so a company has to publish enough content for the language models to learn about it.
- He says a blog is still a legitimate way to get traffic: write a lot around the exact thing your buyer searches for, and both Google and the language models pick it up.
- He says content should target the person who buys, not the person who uses: for a child's product the problem being solved is the mother's.
- His advice for selling to a brand manager is to build something that makes the brand manager look good to their boss.
- He says a B2B reel should open with a hook, such as three mistakes to avoid, answer the hook, and then bring in the product.
- He says B2B advertisements do not work that well.

### Marketplaces, trust and offline media

- He says a marketplace has to build one side of the network first, and cites Cars24 and Spinny as marketing to sellers first because demand follows supply.
- He explains the lemon and peach idea for used vehicles: the buyer cannot tell a good vehicle from a bad one, so trust in the market is low, good vehicles sell for less than they should and bad ones for more.
- He says certification is the answer to that problem, and that a used-vehicle platform that does not talk about trust has not built trust.
- He says cheap outdoor media such as unipoles can work in small cities.

### Selling a D2C product

- Mendiratta says a D2C product has to be aspirational, because people need to feel cool after buying it.
- He says healthy food needs good packaging or people will not trust it.
- He says hiring a good designer is very important for a D2C brand, and that great design has a story behind it.
- He says the segment to aim for is the aspirational middle class: affordable yet aspirational, not cheap and not luxury.
- He says luxury is hard to sell without deep pockets because the cash runs out.
- He says a new product needs trials to be induced, through aspirational design and good pricing.
- He says quick commerce is the channel for impulse categories, because the snack is wanted while the guests are still in the house.
- He says for impulse purchases e-commerce is going to be non-existent.
- He says there is no shame in copying a playbook that works. His line is "agility is more important than originality".
- He says tier 2 buyers are more aspirational than tier 1 buyers, who are more aspirational than metro buyers, and that metros are moving to minimalism and no logos while showing off has only just started in tier 2.
- He says packaging sells a product once, and the product has to be true to its promise to get a repeat order.
- He says a brand built on Ayurveda should go fully Ayurvedic or fully modern and not sit in the middle.

### The founder as the channel

- Mendiratta says a founder's own profile is a free marketing channel, and not using it is laziness.
- He says "Face is bigger than your logo", and that it is much easier to build a founder's LinkedIn than a brand's LinkedIn.
- He quotes Scott Galloway as saying younger consumers trust individual influencers more than corporate brands.
- He says a founder can build a face through podcasts, PR, personal stories and failures.
- He says he posts on LinkedIn two to three times a week.
- He says the first posts will get few views and that will dishearten you, but the results only come to people who go through that grind.
- He says most people get bored of social media and stop, and the few who continue do very well.
- He says nobody is waiting for your post and nobody is judging it, so a founder should sell their own brand because no one else will.
- He says a founder's tone and content quality mature over time, and content from two years ago will always look cringe to its author.
- He says the marketing orientation must come from the top, and the founder should set the vision and then let the team run with it.
- He says founders who became famous before social media did not need it, but for someone starting now it is a free channel with no downside.
- He says a person can have one personality on Instagram and another on LinkedIn.

### How content compounds

- He says one post in a hundred brings about ten percent of your followers, and you cannot know in advance which one.
- He calls posting often increasing your surface area of luck.
- He says the second job is to keep learning what works and assign a reason to it, until that reason stops being true.
- His example of learning: a text post got about 1,000 impressions, a personal-sounding post with an image got about 3,000, and a post with his own photo got about 10,000.
- He says posting more often brings more followers, which makes it a flywheel.
- He says content should go on every platform, because there is no cannibalisation in impressions.
- He says creative teams should act like content creators: frequent, consistent and topical.
- He says being topical means having a point of view on what is happening, because people will know you whether they like the view or not.
- He says never talk about religion or politics, because a small player gets cancelled.
- He says patriotism sells in India and positive posts about the country get engagement.
- He says some founders provoke a controversy so they can apologise and gain attention. He calls it a risky strategy and says not to use it early in a career.
- He says people adopt and switch brands during festivals and life events such as marriage, a child, a new job or moving city, so a brand that is absent then misses out.
- He says posting how much you sold at Diwali creates validation and fear of missing out.

### The zero-cost LinkedIn playbook

- Mendiratta describes the cold start problem as a new brand with no distribution and little money.
- His answer to the cold start is to hire two or three creative people who can brainstorm among themselves and bring the founder twenty ideas to choose from.
- He says to test creative hires in the interview by asking for five ideas on the spot.
- He says everyone on a small team should post about the brand and like each other's posts, which costs nothing.
- At Zepto he sends each new film to a group of about 4,000 employees along with seven or eight caption options, because people find captions hard to write.
- He says about 40 of those employees post, he likes all 40 posts, and his followers then see the same creative many times in their feed that day.
- He says you have to explain the why to a team, otherwise they keep guessing and do not do the task.
- His hack for getting a team to do repetitive work is to nudge them to the answer so the idea appears to be theirs, and then praise it.

### Small execution, big show

- He says the idea is not to scale an execution across all touch points but to do one small execution and put it on social media so the whole country sees it.
- He says Zepto put a wedding hoarding on one flyover and one underpass and the whole country saw it.
- He calls the playbook low cost, small execution, big show.

### Co-funded films and the media you own

- He says Zepto makes films in which another brand appears and funds part of the cost, which saves Zepto production money and builds the other brand's mindshare.
- He names three kinds of film Zepto makes this way: a film for one brand, a festival film with several brands, and a category film.
- He says the Valentine's film was co-sponsored by many brands and its soundtrack was released on Spotify.
- He says the question for a small company is what media it owns or controls that a non-competing brand would pay to be in.
- He says pooling resources this way lowers the cost per campaign, so you can run more campaigns and be more frequent.
- He says Zepto launches a film on LinkedIn for free reach and also spends heavily on performance distribution on Google, Meta and YouTube.
- He says recent Zepto films always show the delivery rider as heroic and as a participant in the story, as a deliberate signal that he expects to compound over the years.
- He says brands cannot use memes taken from films or cricket footage, because the owner sends a copyright notice, and Zepto received one for a one-second clip.

### Brand team culture

- Mendiratta says hierarchy in a brand team kills surprise, and surprise is what wins attention.
- He says a crazy idea only comes out when people feel free to say it without being judged.
- He says there are no bad ideas in a brainstorm, only good ideas and ideas that lead to good ideas, so do not stop ideas at the first step and filter them later.
- He says brand voice can grow but cannot flip, and consistency is a reason to keep brand work in-house when you can afford it.
- He warns founders against a god complex and says a team should see the founder as a vulnerable human.

### Agencies and when to build in-house

- He names four kinds of agency: digital agencies that run performance campaigns, media agencies that buy channels such as TV, print and outdoor, creative agencies that write the script, and research agencies that help shape the brief.
- He describes the film workflow: you write the brief, the creative agency writes the script, a production house shoots it, and the media and digital agencies distribute the finished film.
- He says creative agencies work for awards, so their interest is not aligned with your business growth.
- His rule is to build in-house when the frequency of work is high and outsource when it is low. A company making two films a year is well served by a creative agency.
- He says to check a full-stack agency's research capability by asking who does the research and which brands they work with.
- He says a startup needs someone to run social channels who understands reels and LinkedIn posts, a designer if it posts daily, and a brand manager to run campaigns and manage agencies.
- He says the first brand manager can be junior, and a senior person can be hired above them as complexity grows.
- He says Zepto still uses focus groups because it has to talk to its own consumers.

### Starting with little money

- Asked how he would start Zepto's marketing today with one or two people, he says he would fix the colour, the tone of voice and a mix of brand archetypes, hire one person, and be active on LinkedIn and Instagram.
- He says digital ads let you advertise in one small locality to get your first customers.
- He says friends and family will always be kind, and a founder needs ruthless customers.
- He says the test of a frequently bought product is the repeat order, and you have to look at retention curves.
- He says the first funnel will be broken and inefficient, but it has to start so it can be optimised.
- He says the top of the funnel is either bought or owned, and a founder should build an owned one so they can offer the product in exchange for ruthless feedback.

### AI in brand work

- He says Zepto uses language models for designing creatives and for some ad films.
- He says AI is useful at the top of the funnel of thinking: it gives a starting thread for an idea but cannot write the script for you.

### Learning with frameworks

- He says the best way to cement learning is to put everything you see into a framework, and to coin your own name for it if none exists.
- In feedback on the class exercise he says content that is native to the platform, in the brand's own voice, always works.

## Examples and cases used

- Classplus: his example of a SaaS product for educators where the buyer first has to be taught that they can earn more.
- eWebinar: a tool that funnels website visitors into an educational webinar.
- Cars24 and Spinny: used-vehicle marketplaces that built supply first and talk about trust.
- Minimalist: his example of trust and aspiration at an affordable price.
- Sweet Karam Coffee: his example of design with a story behind it.
- Amul: its topical ads are made by an agency that the company trusts and lets have fun.
- Zepto wedding hoarding: one flyover and one underpass, seen nationwide through social media.
- Zepto co-funded films: a L'Oreal film, a Valentine's film with many brands, and a fitness category film.
- McCann, Leo Burnett and Ogilvy as creative agencies, GroupM as a media agency, Nielsen and Kantar as research agencies.

## Numbers given

- He says he had about 10,000 LinkedIn followers when he joined Zepto and about 120,000 a year and a half later.
- He says about 40 of roughly 4,000 employees post each Zepto film on LinkedIn.
- He says one YouTube short out of about 400 posts in two years brought him about 12,000 subscribers, around ten percent of his total.
- He says one Instagram reel with about 10 million views, out of about 500 posts, brought about eight percent of his followers.
- He says one of his LinkedIn posts crossed a million impressions.

## Not covered

- Brand archetypes and the creative brief were taught on day 1 and only referred to here.
