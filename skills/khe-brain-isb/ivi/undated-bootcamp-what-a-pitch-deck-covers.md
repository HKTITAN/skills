# What a pitch deck needs to cover

Speaker and date are withheld. This note keeps general teaching points only and leaves out the speaker's own frameworks, examples and materials. It covers the problem statement, value proposition, go-to-market, unit economics, competition, team and projections in a pitch deck, with two points on raising money.

## What was said

### Problem statement

- The instructor says a founder has to present the problem in a way that shows the company creates real meaning and value for the world.
- The instructor says an insight backed by data that only the founder has access to is what makes a problem statement unique.
- The instructor considers a professional opening the safer choice, and says a story opening can work but depends on the kind of person the founder is presenting to.
- The instructor says storytelling can be used when presenting a deck live, and is not needed in a deck sent as a pre-read.
- The instructor says that if a listener understands the business when it is explained aloud but not when reading the slide, the written version needs to be simpler.

### Value proposition and business model

- Written class notes from the session record that the value proposition has to say why people should come to the company and not its competition, and that the explanation needs depth.
- Written class notes from the session record that the business model part of the deck answers how the company makes money.

### Go-to-market

- The instructor says the go-to-market slide has to show how a customer first gets to the company, because a customer uses a website or app only after knowing the service exists.
- The instructor says how much location detail a go-to-market plan carries depends on the stage of the company, and on day zero there will probably be no split by location.
- The instructor says a clustered approach makes more sense than going everywhere at once, and that this holds for any offline model.
- The instructor says concentrating in one area makes brand resonance stronger.
- The instructor says to wait for an inflection point before opening a new location, even when people keep questioning whether the business will scale beyond the first city.

### Unit economics

- The instructor says unit economics starts from one unit, such as one customer, and shows the revenue, the cost of goods sold and the acquisition cost for that unit, down to a contribution figure.
- The instructor says acquisition costs in a business-to-business company include team costs, webinar costs and other sales promotion activity, not direct performance marketing.
- The instructor says a founder does not need to split contribution margin into three levels on the slide.
- The instructor says corporate costs, founder salary and other team costs sit below contribution and lead to EBITDA.
- The instructor says direct salaries belong in direct costs and indirect salaries sit lower: a field salesperson who onboards a customer is a direct cost, and an accountant in the corporate office is not.
- The instructor says the slide then has to show how revenue will increase and how costs will decrease, by going into each revenue driver and cost driver.
- The instructor says a founder should show which costs go down with scale and when operating leverage kicks in.
- The instructor says a pre-revenue founder has to use estimates and show what the profit and loss statement will look like at month 6 or month 12.
- The instructor says this slide shows clarity of thought, and that without it people will think the founder does not know what they are doing.
- The instructor says a consolidated company-level view suits scaled companies and belongs in management reports and annexures, not on a slide.
- The instructor says a marketplace should show the gross value of products sold through it, because that is an important measure alongside its own commission revenue.
- The instructor says retention is an operating metric, for marketplaces as well.
- The instructor says that for a pitch deck the format should stay simple: revenue, the top five or six cost items, and margins at different levels.
- The instructor says there is no hard and fast rule, and a founder should use whatever measures are relevant to the business.

### Competition

- The instructor tells founders not to use a table that lists their brand against competitors with ticks and crosses, calling it boring and too simple to carry any insight.
- The instructor says such a table puts 25 to 30 data points in front of the reader and is heavy to process.
- The instructor says a two-axis chart that shows the company alone in a white space is naive, because there is always someone else out there.
- The instructor finds it acceptable to group competitors into categories by their approach and show who operates where.
- The instructor says to find the parameters that matter most to the business and compare on those.
- The instructor says that while presenting the competition slide the founder has to say why the company sits where it does and what is different about it.

### Team

- The instructor says where the team slide goes depends on the stage of the company, and it can sometimes come right at the front.

### Projections

- The instructor says nobody asks for a financial model on day zero.
- The instructor says that by around month 6 to month 12 a founder should have a view of their own profit and loss and cash flow for the next 12 to 18 months, and that lacking one means something is wrong in the business.
- The instructor says projections beyond 18 months cannot be relied on.
- The instructor says a founder who is not confident can present two cases, each tied to the amount of money raised.
- The instructor says margin assumptions can be backed with data from competitors that are listed or publish annual reports, or from company data providers.
- The instructor says a founder convinces an investor on projections through conviction and through what they have seen in the business so far.

### Raising money

- The instructor tells founders not to take money from someone they do not like.
- The instructor says that if a founder does not manage to raise the amount planned, they build the business with five people and not ten.

## Numbers given

- 25 to 30: the instructor's count of data points a reader faces in a tick-and-cross competitor table.
- 12 to 18 months: the instructor's horizon for a founder's own view of profit and loss and cash flow.
- Month 6 or month 12: the instructor's point at which a pre-revenue founder should show an expected profit and loss statement.
