# Negotiation in practice, Amit Khanna

Amit Khanna, an alumnus of the school from the class of 2004 who runs the public markets equity fund Amaya Growth, joined the last session of the negotiation course by video as a guest. He opened with a short case from an auction he was bidding in at the time, then took questions for the rest of the hour, with the course professor adding comments. Questions about individual students' own deals are left out.

## What was said

### Preparation

- Khanna says information and preparation give you the edge, and that the more data you gather before a negotiation the better the outcome.
- His working ratio of preparation time to negotiation time is three to one. Two hours in the room means at least six hours of preparation.
- He says his team rehearses the negotiation inside the team beforehand: what will they ask, what will I say, what will they ask next.
- He says one item of preparation is finding out who the counterparty really is, because the final decision maker is sometimes not in the room.
- He says a negotiator should not assume. Ask, because there is no harm in asking.

### The auction case

- His case is a two-bidder auction, run by a government agency, for a one-of-a-kind restaurant space in Mumbai where past rents could not be used as a benchmark.
- His team downloaded the rival restaurant chain's filed financial statements and collected its menus.
- From these they estimated the rival's likely monthly revenue at the site and the share of revenue it usually pays as rent.
- He says those two numbers let them build a financial model of the rival and estimate the price at which it would stop bidding.
- He says the rival could have done the same analysis on his side and did not, since it would not have turned up had it known his price point was much higher.
- His point is that the information is often available and people simply do not use it.

### Signals during the auction

- He says his team always has an observer in the room whose only job is to write things down.
- In the auction the observer noted every question asked and the time gap between the rival's bids.
- He says a growing gap between bids suggests the bidder is nearing its target price. The rival's first bid came within ten seconds and its later bids were three to four minutes apart.
- His own team bid every ten seconds to signal that it would be aggressive and had a much higher limit.
- He says questions carry information about what matters to the other side, and that what they do not ask is also data.
- He says data is everywhere, even in the silences.
- In the online auction he once placed a second small bid on top of his own, to make the rival think a third bidder had joined, and says it made the rival stop.
- He says he sometimes approves of such moves, and that they are only tactics.

### Do not walk out on an assumption

- His team did not meet the technical qualification for the auction. They asked the landlord whether they could bid anyway, and the answer was yes.
- He says he never leaves a negotiation because he assumes he will not be welcome at the table.
- He had assumed the auction was purely financial, but the landlord also wanted a tenant who would do justice to a 300-year-old monument.
- His team therefore prepared a brand name and a story for the space, so that they would be remembered if the winning bidder dropped out.
- He says to keep the door open in case the winner walks away.

### Walk-away discipline

- He says he always knows his walk-away price before entering an auction, and agrees it with his stakeholders before going in.
- He allows no exception to that number in the moment, and compares it to going to a casino with a budget.

### A negotiation does not end when it ends

- He tells of losing a two-party bid for a building in Gurgaon because the seller preferred the other bidder.
- He then went to the winning bidder and proposed coming in as a co-investor, and his side ended up owning the majority of the building.
- His lesson is that the counterparty can change and the negotiation can continue after it seems to be over.
- He says venture investors respect perseverance. If they say no, keep sending them company updates.
- He tells founders not to burn bridges with customers who say no, and to keep telling them what the company is doing.

### Disputes between partners

- He says the worst negotiations he has seen were public disputes between long-standing business partners, because of the emotional baggage.
- He says a partnership dispute cannot be solved once the fight has started. It has to be solved in advance, while there is peace.
- He names the shareholders' agreement, a dispute resolution mechanism and a trusted third person as the tools for this.

### Culture

- He says American firms tend to be straightforward and state what they want.
- He says that in Southeast Asia face, meaning reputation, matters a great deal, and every party needs to leave the negotiation with respect.
- He says how far you adapt to the other side's culture depends on who needs whom. If you want something from them, you follow their way.
- He says negotiators play roles, including good cop and bad cop in a dispute, and that the role is not the person.
- He says his most satisfying outcomes were those where both sides enlarged the pie together and shared it.

### Information asymmetry

- He says the way to deal with information asymmetry is to ask questions and get to know each other before treating the meeting as a negotiation.
- He says this turns two adversaries dividing a fixed sum into two people working on a shared problem.
- He would even ask a rival bidder outright how much they are willing to pay, because any answer carries information.

### Leverage with investors

- He tells founders never to walk into a room assuming the investor has the leverage.
- He says good ideas are far fewer than the capital available in the market.
- He explains that a fund earns a management fee and a performance fee, and that both depend on deploying capital and making money on it.
- He says a fund that does not invest will struggle to raise its next fund, so investors need founders as much as founders need them.
- He says to treat investors with respect and without arrogance, because they become partners for a very long time.
- He says an investor you deceived or treated with hostility will make your time in the boardroom hard and will hold rights over your later funding rounds.

### Bluffing and honesty

- He says bluffing about a competing offer with a deadline is exposed when the deadline passes and no deal closes, while everyone is watching.
- He says he prefers to be as honest and direct as he can.
- In a role-play on valuation he shows that the first thing he asks about a stated number is whether it is negotiable.
- His advice from the role-play is not to be defensive when challenged on a number. Ask why the other side anchored where it did and bring data for your own figure.

### Threats

- He says the way to handle a threat is to ignore it: do not react to it and do not acknowledge it.
- His reason is that people say things in the heat of the moment that they later need an elegant way out of, and ignoring the threat gives them that way out.
- He also reads a threat as information that the other side is getting desperate.

### Reputation

- He says the financial outcome is never the most important thing to preserve in a negotiation. Reputation, trust and respect are.
- He says there is no harm in leaving a little on the table.
- His example is a large business group that priced its public share issue below the price it could have got, because it expected to come back to the market for later issues.
- He says the investor world is a small ecosystem where people call each other for references.
- He says you will forget the deal and its profit, but the other side will not forget how you made them feel.
- He says to highlight what the other side is getting and not boast about your own win, even if you leave looking like the loser.

### Rebuilding trust

- For a new negotiation with a party after a failed one, he suggests meeting them informally before the formal table.
- He says to tell them honestly what you could have done differently, and to take ownership rather than assign blame.
- He says he debriefs after every negotiation on what worked and what did not.
- He says changing the person who represents you can help when the problem is a personal clash.
- He says a failed negotiation is not in itself a breach of trust.

### Write it down

- He recalls a class exercise from his own student days where he accepted an unwritten gentleman's promise and regretted it.
- His lesson is that whatever is agreed should be documented.

### When the other side only talks price

- He says that when a buyer's offer is far off, the gap is about value and not price, so move the discussion from price to value.
- He says either your communication of value is not getting through, or you are talking to the wrong customer.
- He says to talk about how you are better than the next supplier and what saving you bring.
- He says to find another person on the buyer's side whose interest is not price, since a purchase manager's job is only to buy cheaply.
- His example is a human resources software company he invested in: the IT team cares about cost, the HR team about employee feedback, the finance head about cost and the chief executive about who the partner is.

### Cash and risky customers

- He warns founders not to book revenue just to support a valuation, and says cash flow is what keeps a startup alive.
- He says that with a risky customer you should do small deals you can afford to write off, and ask for guarantees, credit checks or a deposit even at a lower margin.
- He says that if a customer backed by investors does not pay, you can reach out to those investors, who care about their reputation.

### Sharing data with investors

- He says that before a term sheet you should share only information you would be willing to see made public.
- After a term sheet, he says, sign a non-disclosure agreement and share freely.
- He notes that a non-disclosure agreement cannot stop an investor from using what they have learned when they look at a competitor.
- He says he has paid a counterparty's due diligence costs only once, and that funds budget for diligence as part of finding deals.
- If an investor insists on the founder paying, he would offer to have the company bear the cost only when the deal closes.
- For updates to investors who have not invested, he suggests sharing growth rates in place of absolute numbers and nothing proprietary.

### Lawyers and market standard

- He tells founders to hire a good lawyer for the definitive documents, on a fixed fee and not hourly billing, so that incentives are aligned.
- When an investor calls a harsh term market standard, he would collect that investor's own past deal terms and ask why this deal should be stricter.
- He says to do this without embarrassing them in front of the room.

## Examples and cases used

- A two-bidder auction for a restaurant space in Mumbai: used to show how public filings, menus, bid timing and questions reveal a rival's limit.
- A building purchase in Gurgaon: used to show that a lost bid can be reopened by partnering with the winner.
- A large business group's public share issue priced below what it could have got: used to show that leaving money on the table can serve a long relationship with the market.
- A human resources software company selling to hotels and banks: used to show that each person on the buyer's side cares about something different.

## Numbers given

- Three to one: Khanna's ratio of preparation time to negotiation time.
- Ten seconds, then three to four minutes: the gaps between the rival's bids early and late in his auction.
- 250 and 230 rupees: the share price a business group could have asked and the price it chose, in his example.
