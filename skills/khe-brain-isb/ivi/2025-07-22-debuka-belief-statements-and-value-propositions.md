# Belief statements and value propositions, Parvesh Debuka

This is the second recorded part of Parvesh Debuka's marketing bootcamp, running up to the lunch break. He gave two fill-in frameworks, one for the customer's belief and one for the value proposition, and had founders write theirs and read them out for critique. This note keeps the frameworks and the general points he made while critiquing. The individual critiques are left out.

## What was said

### The belief statement

- Parvesh Debuka's belief statement has two steps. Step one states what the customer believes. Step two states why that makes them buy, avoid or feel uncomfortable with something in the category.
- He says the point of the belief is to capture what the customer believes and how that affects their buying behaviour.
- He says the belief has to be close to the category, because a person believes many things in life and only some affect what they buy here.
- He says a belief does not have to be positive. His example is the belief that carbonated beverages are terrible.
- He says the test for a belief statement is whether it is true and whether it affects buying behaviour.
- His example for Cult is that the customer believes exercise should be fun and not boring, so they will buy something that makes it fun.
- He says ambiguity begins at this stage if these things are not written down sharply, and it then flows into communication and into how the product is built.

### Reading the customer correctly

- He separates a problem of want from a problem of access. If customers want something but cannot reach it, the founder no longer needs to prove the want.
- He warns against turning a convenient association into a truth because it suits what the founder wants to build. He calls this playing into one's own biases.
- His example is the idea that people love to watch what they have read. He says people often dislike screen adaptations of books they have read, and that people enjoy visual storytelling far more than reading.
- He says not everything about a product needs to appear in the belief. Looking good is a hygiene factor and a given.
- He says Steve Jobs showed that everyone loves great design, and takes it if it is affordable.
- He says how ready a society is to adopt a technology changes from year to year, so he would not describe a later success as a latent need that others missed.

### The value proposition

- He defines the value proposition as the answer to why someone should care about the brand.
- He says a value proposition is not about features. It is about the benefit, and he means an emotional benefit.
- His structure is: for an audience, who have a need, we offer a category, that gives a benefit.
- His first example is: for first-time gym goers who want structure and expert guidance, we offer a personalised fitness app that adapts to their level.
- His second example is: for busy moms who want to feel less guilty about parenting, we offer healthy ready-to-eat snacks that kids actually love.
- He says features are the solutions to the problems identified for the customer, and the value proposition captures what is unlocked for the customer once those problems are solved.
- He says the value proposition goes out, in some form, as the marketing message.
- He says writing down the emotion the brand wants to evoke makes the brief to a creative team solid, and thinking this through is the job of the founder or the marketing lead.
- He says the more specific the statement is to the founder's own product, the better it works.

### Condense to one emotional benefit

- He says a long first draft is a fine place to start, and the work is to keep condensing it by removing what is less relevant to the customer.
- He says the statement should end on one major emotional value. Functional points such as convenience can be handled by other parts of the messaging.
- He says a founder should force themselves to say one thing, because a diluted message leaves them not knowing why people are not responding.
- He says a founder cannot plan a hierarchy of emotions to go after. They have to understand people's frustrations in the category and work from there.
- He says a benefit that is the founder's main selling idea but not the buyer's worry should be left as something the customer discovers, and the message should lead with what the buyer is worried about.
- He says the target audience should not be broader than the group the message resonates with.
- He tells founders to stick to who they believe the message is true for, since that is the only part they can control.
- He says being deliberate at this stage matters because a positioning that is not sharp leads to a longer gestation and a lot of money badly spent.

### Being deliberate with words and testing

- He says every word in the statement should be chosen deliberately, because a shift of one word changes what the line evokes.
- His example is time spent scrolling. He says the feeling afterwards is of having wasted time, which is not the same as feeling drained.
- He tells founders to go and listen to people, and to create a setting where they do not ask a direct question, because direct questions often get wrong or made-up answers.
- He says concept testing is a good idea when a founder is unsure. His example is calling seven people in one group and seven in another and showing them different messages.
- He says to go small and keep testing, and never go all out at the start.
- He says a founder cannot change what the customer wants but can change the product. On building trust, he suggests building the feeling of safety into the product so that testimonials follow.

### Brand asides

- He says Tzinga, the Hector Beverages drink that came before Paper Boat, did not have a demand problem. The organisation's focus moved to Paper Boat once it took off.
- He says Hector Beverages, the parent company, started with a protein powder, then Tzinga, then Paper Boat.
- He says what changed with Paper Boat's aam panna was the format and packaging. The recipe stayed authentic, and a drink that was only available at home became available anywhere.

## Examples and cases used

- Cult: the belief that exercise should be fun and not boring.
- Red Bull: it has stuck with "gives you wings", evoking the same emotion every time, as his example of consistency paying off.
- Nike: it consistently inspires people to push themselves to be athletes.
- Tesla and Prius: raised by a founder as a case of latent need. He treats wanting a product to look cool as an obvious need, not a latent one.
- Paper Boat aam panna: the same recipe in a new format and place.
- His own business: he worries about whether delivery platforms deduct the right amount from each order and wants a tool to check, as an example of an emotional stressor behind a functional product. He also lost a month and a half to a trademark clash over the name he first chose.

## Numbers given

- 20%: the share of his own restaurant's sales that he says comes through Swiggy and Zomato.
- Seven people per group: his example size for a quick concept test.

## Not covered

- Any quantitative way to size how many customers hold a belief.
- The positioning model of category, tension, promise and reason to believe came in the afternoon part.
