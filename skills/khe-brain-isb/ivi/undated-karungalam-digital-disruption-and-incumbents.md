# Digital disruption and how incumbents respond, Vishal Karungalam

Typed notes from a class titled Innovation and Design Thinking, taught by Vishal Karungalam. The notes cover what it means to digitise a product, how disruptors and incumbents behave, and Google as an incumbent.

## What was said

### Basics a founder must be clear on

- Vishal Karungalam says a founder needs a clear understanding of the value proposition, how the business will make money, how it will scale and how it will be built.
- He says research is truth and opinions do not matter.
- He says efficiency is measured in terms of cost, time to market or quality.
- He frames a business as creating value, transferring value and capturing value.

### What digitising means

- He says every company wants to be a digital company.
- He says you have digitised a product when you have found a way to separate the value-creating entity from the physical artefact.
- He says experience products are hard to replicate and hard to digitise.
- He says a digital channel can be complementary, a substitute or disruptive to the physical product.
- His examples are digital channels that drive awareness for museums or concerts as complementary, Kindle as a substitute, and vinyl records under disruptive.

### Disruptors and incumbents

- He says that when incumbents respond, the disruptors die.
- He says a disruptor's goal is to grow very quickly.
- He describes the incumbent's dilemma as choosing between the high value and the low value offering.
- He says mainstream customers and underserved customers want different things, and the incumbent gets confused between them.
- He says incumbents over-serve or ignore some customers, which gives disruptors an advantage there.
- He says incumbents ignore disruption because it is a process and not one fixed point.
- He says disruptors often have a business model that is different from the incumbents' model.
- His test for a disruptor is whether the innovation is more convenient or more affordable than the incumbent's existing offering.
- He says talent is key to building disruptive products, and that execution overrules everything else.

### Google as an incumbent

- He says people who search on Google have an intention to purchase, though not always at once, and that two thirds of people with an intention to purchase click on ads.
- He says Google predicted in 2007 that people would move from desktops to mobile, and that it happened in 2013.
- He says Google bought Android and backed it aggressively with a catch, which was Google ads and the Google Play store, and calls this an incumbent's response.
- He says Bing gained share through vertical search, rising about 1% a month to 14%, and Google responded by buying the companies Bing got its data from.
- He says Bing then had to pay Google for the data, and Google used the data to build its own vertical search.
- He says Google as an incumbent could not beat social media, because it did not think social media would be a place for ads.
- He says AI is now making multiple specialised kinds of search possible, such as Midjourney for images and GitHub Copilot for code.

## Examples and cases used

- Uber: disrupted car ownership because people buy fewer cars, and helped real estate because people are happy to live farther away.
- Dollar Shave Club: a monthly subscription for blades against Gillette's blades model, later acquired by an incumbent.
- Google and Android: an incumbent buying its way into mobile and tying it to its ads and app store.
- Google and Bing: buying the sources of a rival's data.
- Kindle: a digital substitute for a physical book, which still lacks the feel of a book.

## Numbers given

- His Google figure: two thirds of people with an intention to purchase click on ads.
- His Bing figure: share went to 14%, rising 1% every month.
- His Android figure: 70% of the market by 2013.
