# NPV and IRR for investment decisions, part 2

The second half of a finance class taught by the programme's finance instructor, who is not named in the recording. Using cash flow projections for two drinks from the first half's example company, she teaches net present value and internal rate of return, compares the two rules, shows the NPV profile and closes with a recap. Much of the session is questions from the cohort.

## What was said

### Net present value

- The instructor says NPV discounts every cash flow over the life of a project back to the present and adds them up. It is net because some of the cash flows are outflows.
- She says the discount rate is the opportunity cost for the investors, the minimum return they need.
- She says the discount rate is not the fixed deposit rate and not nominal GDP growth. It includes a risk premium that depends on the nature of the business and is always higher for startups.
- She explains discounting with one line of the example: 500 received after one year at 10% is worth 500 divided by 1.10 today.
- In Excel she uses the present value function on the cash flows from year 1 onward and then adds the year 0 cash flow separately.
- She says a positive NPV means the project gives back more than it takes, after the investors have already been given their required return.
- Another way she reads it: the project is like a mini startup whose value today is the present value of its future cash flows, and the NPV is that value less the money raised.
- NPV is a rupee amount and not a percentage, which she says is one reason people find IRR more intuitive.
- Her NPV rule: never pick a project with negative NPV, because it does not even earn the minimum return and destroys value. You can wait for better projects.
- When projects are mutually exclusive, pick the one with the highest NPV. Her example of mutually exclusive is choosing between a high quality and an ordinary version of the same video.
- When several projects can be done together, all those with positive NPV add value, but she tells founders to check synergies and cannibalisation between them first.
- Given uncertainty, she suggests rerunning the NPV with a pessimistic sales forecast to see whether it stays positive.
- NPV has to take in cash flows over the entire life of the product. The four year life in her example is a simplifying assumption.

### Why cash flows and not profits

- She says cash flows capture the timing of when money has to go into the business and how long it stays locked in, and a minimum return is owed over that time.
- Profit, she says, is useful for a view from period to period.
- She says VCs think the same way about exits: if an IPO or acquisition will take 15 years and not 10, they look for more growth to make up for the wait.
- She says that in a projection the sum of the negative cash flows is the total funding you need.

### Internal rate of return

- She defines IRR as the return per year that a project generates.
- It is calculated as the discount rate at which NPV becomes zero, which she calls a mathematical trick. Excel has an IRR function that takes the stream of cash flows.
- Her simplest illustration: put in 80 today and receive 100 a year later, and the IRR is 25%.
- The IRR rule is to accept a project when IRR is greater than the cost of capital, which she says is equivalent to a positive NPV.
- She contrasts IRR with return on investment or return on capital employed, which is based on profit, is calculated year by year and not over the life of the project, and ignores investments such as working capital.

### NPV against IRR

- She says IRR ignores scale. A tiny process improvement can show 100% IRR while a marketing campaign ten times larger shows 15%, and the small project cannot be scaled up ten times.
- IRR assumes every extra rupee invested earns the same return. She says that is reasonable for VCs, who invest across many startups, and for mutual funds, and that VCs use IRR extensively.
- For business decisions she says scale matters and cannot be changed at will, so NPV, which shows total value created, is the more useful number.
- She says NPV in turn ignores replication possibilities, such as one store that could become ten.
- Her summary: NPV is better for startups focused on achieving scale and is what large corporations use for internal decisions. IRR can be better for small, high growth companies that are short of capital.

### The NPV profile

- The NPV profile plots NPV on the vertical axis against a range of discount rates.
- She says it works as a sensitivity analysis: you may not know the exact return investors want but you may know the range, and the profile shows whether NPV stays positive across it.
- The rate at which a project's line crosses zero is its IRR.
- She uses the profile to show that when capital is cheap the project with larger but later cash flows wins, because you do not mind waiting.

### DCF and early stage startups

- She says discounted cash flow is still the fundamental way to understand valuation, but other methods are used for VC valuation because startups stay cash flow negative for a long time and their cash flow estimates are uncertain.
- She says an early stage startup can be cash flow negative overall while each new product, store or contract is still judged on its own incremental cash flows.
- Asked about entering a market because rivals have, she says a negative NPV should at least make the company pause, and the rest is business judgement.
- She says part of the 40 to 50% a year return expected of early stage startups reflects the risk that the startup does not survive.

## Examples and cases used

- Nirvana Juices and Beverages, a classroom example: Strawberry Smoothie has higher cash flows early that taper, and Lychee Lemonade starts lower and grows. Both have positive NPV at 10%, and the smoothie's is higher.
- A marketing decision or a choice of video quality: she says these can be evaluated with the same framework as a new product.
- Swiggy Bolt: raised by a student as a case of entering a market because competitors have, which prompted her point about pausing on a negative NPV.

## Numbers given

- Classroom example: upfront investment of 1,000 thousand rupees, year 1 cash flow of 500 thousand for the smoothie, a four year life and a 10% discount rate.
- Classroom example: NPV of about 79 thousand rupees for the smoothie and about 49 thousand for the lemonade.
- Classroom example: the smoothie's IRR is about 14.5%, so its excess over the 10% cost of capital is about 4.5 points. The lemonade's IRR is lower.
- Her IRR illustration: 80 invested, 100 received one year later, IRR 25%.
