# Amazon working backwards, Girish Krishnan

Girish Krishnan, an ISB alumnus who worked at Infosys and Yahoo and has spent about twelve years at Amazon, the last six on payments in India, spoke to the cohort about culture, clarity and mechanisms in product building. He walked the room through Amazon's working backwards document using a group exercise on a service for selling used items, then applied the same questions to ideas from founders in the room. The general teaching is kept here and the founders' own ventures are left out.

## What was said

### Culture comes first

- Girish Krishnan says a founder who wants a good product company should think deeply about the culture that makes repeated product success possible, because the company is the collective of people and partners, not the founder alone.
- He organises the talk around three things: culture, clarity about what you want to build, and mechanisms that make success repeatable.
- He describes Yahoo's culture as fun, open and good for hacking together innovative products, and says it was too relaxed to produce business outcomes at scale.
- At Yahoo India nobody had cabins and everyone sat in the open, which he counts as a good part of the culture.
- He says Amazon does not use PowerPoint. The first 30 minutes of an hour-long meeting are spent reading a written document.
- He calls reading a written document an inclusive and unbiased way to judge an idea, because it does not reward the person with better salesmanship in the room.
- He says Amazon hires people against its leadership principles and then evaluates them at work against the same principles.
- The leadership principles he names as core are customer obsession, invent and simplify, and think big.
- He says Amazon's habit of extracting efficiency from everything is customer centric, since a saving in the supply chain is passed to the customer.
- His advice is either to set up your own culture this way or to anchor your product work onto the culture your company already has.

### Finding the opportunity: need and discontent

- He maps opportunities on two axes: whether the customer has a stated need or a hidden discontent, and whether it is critical or not.
- An obvious need is easy to learn by asking the customer, and because it is obvious it is already well covered by others.
- He looks for hidden discontent that is close to critical, where an early mover can build something.
- He says that if you are in the plain need space and better funded companies are in the same space, you should rethink.
- He says discontent is found by being in the field with customers for a long time, not by sitting in the office.
- His apparel example: a shopper says she wants size, fit and looks, but the hidden discontent is that the new item may not match the clothes she already owns. Letting her order five items to try at home with no return fee speaks to that.
- He says cashbacks and discounts in India work to handhold customers into a new habit.

### The mental model of the business

- He says a founder must reduce the business to a simple mental model of how it operates and makes money, and impart it to every stakeholder including investors.
- His quick commerce example is a per store model: one dark store, a set number of items, a neighbourhood of a given density, and the volume at which that store breaks even, then replicated in other places.
- The mental model states the core hypothesis, so stakeholders know what you are testing and noise does not clutter the discussion.

### Customer obsession

- He describes customer obsession as simple to state and hard to practise: fully focusing on the customer, and for the long term.
- He plays a Jeff Bezos clip making the point that customer obsession includes inventing on the customer's behalf, because inventing is not the customer's job.
- He asks founders to picture the moment of delight, such as a child opening a parcel that arrived on time, and to list everything that had to go right for it.
- He says you should define your customers broadly and not too narrowly. For Amazon the customers include buyers, sellers, authors and publishers.
- He says you need mechanisms for listening to customers, and that watching internal and external metrics is also a way of listening.
- He uses the Swiss Army knife as a warning: it does everything, looks cool, and does nothing really well. A product should do one thing deeply.
- He says cool can be a fad, so index on usefulness.

### The working backwards document

- He says working backwards is a process for getting clarity, not a document, and you use it when you need clarity on what you are doing.
- He gives three parts: a press release written as if the product has launched, simple customer FAQs, and a visualisation of the customer experience.
- He adds internal FAQs, where questions such as whether the product is financially sustainable are answered.
- He gives five questions to put any idea through: who is the customer, what is the customer problem or opportunity, what is the most important customer benefit, how do you know what the customer needs or wants, and what does the customer experience look like.
- The first line of the press release should work as an elevator pitch.
- He says the cheapest test of whether a product works is to imagine and write a specific quotation from a customer. If you cannot write a testimonial that reads as real, you are struggling for product market fit.
- A testimonial must be in the customer's words. He says no customer will ever describe a product as discovery based instead of intent based.
- He says a cohort such as an age band or phone ownership is a filter, not the customer definition.
- He says every line in the press release is loaded. Promising pickup from the customer's doorstep instead of a pickup point changes the cost by 100 to 200 rupees in his example, and each line implies pin codes, manpower, infrastructure and cost.
- Once the press release is written it is treated as set, and the team goes and makes it happen.
- He says each problem has to be probed five or ten levels deeper before the document is ready, and calls this very hard work.
- He says every feature on Amazon goes through this process.

### Mechanisms, metrics and unit economics

- He defines a mechanism as anything closed loop that lets you do a thing repeatedly: you build it, drive adoption, and keep inspecting it.
- He says to look at rate metrics first and scale metrics later.
- If customers like the product and the net promoter score is good but the unit does not break even until scale, he reads that as a product that works with an open question about reaching scale.
- He says to build a replicable unit and inspect its input metrics closely, seeing how far each input can be pushed.
- For a store built around trends, he would track any missed trend or missing selection as a defect, with demand weighted selection coverage as an input metric.
- He warns against working backwards from a business metric. His example is cutting customer contacts by hiding the contact option, which fixes the number and not the reason customers call.
- He describes a team practice from his own product: an engineer, a business development person, a marketer and a product manager listened to customer interviews together, then listed problems and solutions the same day.
- He says a model whose outcomes are not repeatable and predictable is hard to turn into a mechanism, and suggests an A/B test against the existing method to get data.

### Large companies and startups

- He says Amazon's private brand selection is plain data driven: top reviews, maximum sales and highest margin qualify a product for the funnel.
- He says a large company is slow and a startup can iterate quickly, so a space where you can move fast is an advantage. He cites quick commerce.
- On why Amazon keeps its interface, he says cool is good but not at the cost of useful, and changing a design hurts customers whose habits are built on it.
- He says an incumbent has to switch only when there is a seminal trend, and names Google facing AI answers and Kodak as examples of that threat.
- He says any commerce business rests on selection, convenience and price.
- He says AI does not yet have the judgment and intuition this high judgment customer delight process needs.

## Examples and cases used

- Amazon used item selling in India: he built a customer to customer selling service around 2017 to 2018 with one click listing, an escrow, and doorstep pickup with packaging. He says it was shut down for two reasons, cost economics and the complexity of the tax regime after the move from VAT to GST, and that UPI was not yet prevalent.
- eBay and PayPal: his example of an escrow model where money is held until the buyer inspects the item.
- Cashify: he calls it a one sided model that buys, refurbishes and then sells, which solves the trust problem.
- Kindle and the origin of Amazon: he says the hidden discontent was limited selection in book stores, and Bezos's hypothesis was that without a store he could offer far more selection.
- Alexa: he says shared music at home was an unsaid need, with family members taking turns to ask for songs.
- Amazon smart stores: scanning a product in an offline shop to see the Amazon detail page with reviews and ratings, plus offers and a digital invoice. He says it has not scaled as far as hoped.
- Handyman services in the US: one person doing many jobs generates more demand than a single trade.
- Books: he recommends the book Working Backwards for the full method.

## Numbers given

- 30 minutes: the reading time at the start of an hour-long Amazon meeting, in his account.
- 100 to 200 rupees: his example of the cost difference between doorstep pickup and a pickup point.
- About 120 people worked on the used item selling product, in his telling.
