# Behavioural pricing and mental accounting

A marketing instructor took the cohort through behavioural ideas that shape how customers see a price. The recording starts partway through the slides on mental accounting, moves to reference prices and the perception of cost, works two classroom puzzles, and ends with a pricing exercise in which founders presented their products. The exercise itself is left out.

## What was said

### Mental accounting

- The instructor gives four rules of mental accounting: separate out gains, combine losses, integrate smaller losses with larger gains, and separate a small gain from a larger loss.
- A student raises a company that entered two new markets where one failed badly and one succeeded. The instructor's point is to think of the rules in terms of how results are grouped when presented.

### Prospect theory

- The instructor summarises three elements: loss aversion, diminishing returns, and reference dependence.
- On diminishing returns the instructor says repeated discounts have less and less value to the customer over time.

### Managing reference prices

- The instructor says a pricing policy has to manage credible reference prices: establish reference prices, encourage favourable comparisons, and avoid unfavourable comparisons.
- The Tesla example: the company began with the Roadster sports car at a premium price, which set the segment it would be compared against, and over time offered other models.
- The instructor says the customer assumes an original price and does not know the real price.

### Managing the perception of cost

- The instructor gives three ways to manage how cost is perceived: bundle products to obscure individual costs, focus attention on customer value, and focus attention on the fully loaded cost, including development cost and other sunk costs.
- The instructor says entrepreneurs tend to leave part of their real cost out, and that a price waterfall should include it so people understand what justifies the price.
- On focusing on value: if a product cuts the customer's cost by 10 percent, state the saving in money. In the instructor's illustration that is 500 dollars.
- The LED example: an LED costs more than an incandescent bulb, so the seller should talk about the energy and power bill saved and not the purchase price.
- The restaurant example: raw material cost is about the same for a five star restaurant and an ordinary one, yet the price difference is huge, so the price has to be justified by value.

### Lump sum versus per use payment

- The ski example: one group buys four one day passes at Rs 4,000 each and another buys one four day pass for Rs 16,000. After three good days, rain ruins the slopes.
- The instructor says the group with one day passes is more likely to ski on the ruined day, because each pass is seen as its own transaction and skipping it feels like a distinct loss.
- The class notes add that the four day pass group has the better experience because it integrates the loss into the whole purchase.
- The instructor agrees with a student that this is the same choice as subscription against per use or microtransaction pricing.
- The instructor says customers overstate how often they use something when asked, and report lower use the moment price is mentioned.

### Percentage bias

- The fuel puzzle: Anil moves from a car that runs 12 km per litre to one that runs 14, and Meghana moves from 30 km per litre to 40. Both drive the same distance.
- Over 100 km Anil's use falls from 8.33 litres to 7.14, a saving of 1.19 litres. Meghana's falls from 3.33 litres to 2.5, a saving of 0.83 litres. Anil saves more petrol.
- The instructor says most people look at the 30 to 40 figure and call it the greater efficiency gain, and that this bias matters for policy: if the least efficient vehicles such as trucks do not change, the rest matters little.

### Pricing exercise

- In the exercise a founder describes the product and privately writes the list price, the reference price they expect the audience to hold, and the actual price. The audience then writes what it is willing to pay.

## Examples and cases used

- Tesla Roadster: starting at the premium end sets the reference price for later models.
- LED against incandescent bulbs: sell the saving, not the price.
- Five star restaurants: price is justified by value, not input cost.
- Ski passes: how the form of payment changes behaviour after a loss.
