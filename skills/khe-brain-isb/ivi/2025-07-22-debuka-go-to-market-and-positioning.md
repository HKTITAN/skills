# Go-to-market and positioning bootcamp, Parvesh Debuka

Parvesh Debuka is a brand consultant who began in sales and marketing at Tanishq, was brand manager for the launch of Mia, and later worked on Paper Boat and at Cult. He ran a full-day marketing bootcamp on go-to-market strategy. He taught a set of templates (customer, tension, belief, value proposition, promise and reason to believe), had founders write and read out their own versions, and closed with the stories of how Mia and Paper Boat were taken to market.

## What was said

### What go-to-market is

- Debuka describes go-to-market as the bridge between the product you create and how the world discovers it, trusts it, buys it and keeps coming back to it.
- He says go-to-market covers the whole funnel from acquisition to repeat purchase, and is not only about getting the first customers or the first set of products.
- He says founders often confuse go-to-market with marketing, and that marketing is only a part of it.
- He says a great product that went nowhere and an average product that is everywhere both come down to go-to-market, as a gap in one case and a win in the other.
- His slide lists the building blocks of go-to-market as target audience, value proposition, acquisition channels (paid, owned, earned), conversion strategy, pricing, messaging and positioning, and a launch plan for the first 60 to 90 days.
- He says a founder should lose sleep over three questions: who the customers are, how they will discover the product, and why they will buy it.

### Describing the customer

- Debuka says that if you are selling to everyone you are selling to no one.
- He says a sharp customer description goes beyond demographics to mindset, tension and beliefs.
- His template for the customer is: a type of person, who is feeling an emotion, because of a tension, and who holds a core belief.
- He says the belief has to sit next to the category and fuel buying behaviour in it.
- He says a belief can be negative, such as a belief that carbonated drinks are terrible, as long as it is true and it affects buying.
- His template for a belief is "my customer believes that" followed by "which is why they" buy, avoid or feel uncomfortable with something.
- When a business sells through a company to that company's employees, he says the customer to understand is the individual who has the problem.
- For a product meant for children, he says the brand should keep talking rationally to the parent who decides, because talking directly to the child means shifting to a different target group.

### Finding the tension

- Debuka sets out a tension ladder with three rungs: a generic want, a category tension, and a sharp emotional tension.
- His example of the ladder is healthy eating. "People want to eat healthy" is too vague. "Healthy food is usually boring or expensive" is better. I feel like a bad parent when I give processed food, but my kid refuses healthy snacks is the real tension.
- He defines a real tension as a place where what the customer wants and the options available in the market are in direct conflict.
- He says the real value is unlocked at the emotional rung, because people are emotional and having or lacking something changes how they feel about their day.
- He says a tension statement has to capture how the situation makes the customer feel, not the founder's macro view of a market gap.
- He says nobody articulates the tension at once, and that a founder will go through many drafts.
- He tells founders to write the statement down, because when we only speak we can fool ourselves easily.
- He says that leaving room for ambiguity at this stage makes everything built on top of it more ambiguous.
- He says a founder working on something niche should check that the tension is widespread among a large group of people.
- He says a founder should not conclude that a category has no tension. The better stance is that a tension exists and has to be looked for by talking to people.
- He says that if customers have no frustrations around a problem, it may not be a problem worth solving.
- He says a product that first needs customers to be educated adds a gestation period of a year or two before the business takes off, and the founder should go in knowing that.
- He says a hypothesis can start from a personal experience or strong feeling, and should then be tested by talking to about 20 other people.
- He says brand managers in the industry rarely talk to customers directly, and that he values face-to-face conversations because expressions and tone of voice carry rich information.
- He says people say one thing and behave in the opposite way, and that the gap between how customers think and how they behave is where a new brand can come in.

### Writing the value proposition

- Debuka's structure for a value proposition is: for a target audience, who have a need, we offer a product, that delivers a benefit.
- He says the benefit at the end of the statement must be an emotional benefit and not a feature.
- He says features are the solutions to the customer's problems, while the value proposition states what solving them unlocks for the customer, and it goes out as the marketing message.
- He says a first draft is usually long, and the work is to condense it by removing what is less relevant or less impactful to the customer.
- He says the statement should land on one major emotional benefit, and that convenience and other benefits can be handled in other parts of the messaging.
- He says there is no hierarchy of emotions to pick from. The emotion has to be true for the people in that category, and an imagined one will most likely fail.
- He says things like good design or a quality check are hygiene. They must be there but do not need to be called out in the value proposition.
- He says the question to ask before any creative work is what you want the customer to feel, not what you want them to do.

### One promise and a reason to believe

- Debuka has founders state four things for positioning: the category, the tension, the promise, and the reason to believe.
- He says iconic brands have usually stood for one promise, because one promise lets the whole organisation rally behind it.
- He says a brand that tries to say two or three things dilutes itself, and that the return on spend is highest when you pick one thing and chase it.
- He says a brand that serves everybody will see some sales at the start and will begin to struggle later.
- He says picking one promise is the hard part, because the temptation is always to add more.
- He says a brand that sits between two positions usually loses ground. "You either go this way or that."
- He says a promise should not depend on something outside the company's control. His example is a guarantee of job placement, which depends on employers.
- He says a reason to believe should be concrete proof a stranger can check, such as a track record or named customers to speak to, and that a broad certification leaves doubts.
- He says a founder's own professional background in the field is a strong reason to believe and should be at the centre of the brand's story.
- He says that one well-known name backing a new brand is enough to give it a good start on trust.
- He says the goal is not 100 sales but 100 sales to the right customers, who then become evangelists.

### From the brief to the creative

- Debuka says the customer, tension, value proposition and intended emotion together form the brief that a brand person hands to a creative agency.
- He says turning the brief into creative work is the easier part, and that an agency or AI can do it. "Making it creative is a monkey's job."
- He says the quality of the input given to the agency or to AI decides whether the message will be accurate and relevant to the target customer.
- He says a sharp, powerful positioning line is recognised by feel when it arrives.

### Differentiating in a crowded, price-led category

- Debuka says few Indian consumers will choose health over taste, and a large group will choose price over health.
- He says that when the product itself offers no differentiation the customer will pay for, naming, packaging and branding are the levers left.
- In that case he advises deliberately outlandish branding to get noticed on the shelf and drive trials, and then letting the product do the work of repeat purchase.
- He says beverages are a crowded impulse category with very low loyalty, where a product that is not seen is not bought.
- He says about 100 beverage brands appear in India each February and disappear by August, and he questions why anyone would want to enter the category.

### Staying focused as the company grows

- Debuka advises against starting with two brands or two different target groups, because the language, tone and persona for each are very different.
- He says a founder should take one problem, in a market large enough, to a point of real success before moving to adjacent opportunities, and calls early diversification a sure way to burn out.
- He says that once a brand has won a core consumer it can keep launching more products for that same consumer, which is different from launching for a new consumer.
- He says a founder who picks a market of Rs 300 to 400 crore instead of one of Rs 5,000 crore will run into the urge to diversify, and should ask why that market was chosen.
- He says lasting institutions take years to build, and that founders should ask themselves hard questions about which problem they want to solve and how large they want to become.

## Examples and cases used

- Mamaearth: he describes its starting customer as millennial mothers anxious about chemicals in baby products, who believe natural ingredients are safer.
- Cult: he gives the tension as wanting to get fit while finding the gym monotonous. Cult answered with varied classes such as boxing, dance, swimming and sports.
- Cult, product mechanics: classes were booked on the app with a fixed number of floor squares per class. A member who booked and did not show up lost a day of membership. A waitlist worked like a train ticket, with members confirming an hour before class. New users got three free trial classes. He says the message was that Cult cares about your fitness more than you do, and that the product did the heavy lifting.
- Mia by Tanishq, the tension: around 2010 to 2011 women were entering workplaces in large numbers. Jewellery had been designed for home, weddings and family occasions. Women aged 25 to 35 wanted to be known for their work and still express themselves, and had no jewellery made for the workplace.
- Mia, the decision: the team chose a separate brand over a Tanishq range because Tanishq was typically for buyers of 35 and above and its stores felt intimidating to younger women.
- Mia, the line: the agency took seven rounds, with two or three directions each, before presenting "loves to go to work".
- Mia, the launch: counters inside Tanishq stores, hoardings, a radio song, a TV campaign six to eight months later, and a bus in Bangalore fitted with a light make-up area, a photo booth with instant photos, breakfast and a live musician on popular commuter routes. He calls the bus not scalable but a source of buzz.
- Mia, the limits: low price points left very little gold to design with. In hindsight he says many buyers were more traditional women wanting lighter, cheaper designs, while working women often accessorise with non-precious pieces because of cost, fear of loss and safety. He says he would still launch it the same way because the positioning was sharp and true.
- Paper Boat, the origin: the company's earlier drink was moving too slowly in general trade to sustain a sales team, so it looked for faster-moving drinks. Aam panna was the first idea, followed by other traditional drinks.
- Paper Boat, the tension: research with people of about 27 to 35 found fondness for these drinks and no packaged option besides sodas. The consumer was defined as a 30-year-old millennial living away from home.
- Paper Boat, the positioning: he says the line about drinks and memories took a long time to write. Campaigns showed home, family and relationships to signal authentic recipes.
- Paper Boat, the packaging: an opaque pack gave up the desire created by seeing the drink, and the team accepted that in order to stand out on the shelf.
- Paper Boat, the mistake: the company spent heavily on TV, which created demand that distribution could not reach, and he says a lot of money was wasted.
- Paper Boat, distribution and price: he calls getting onto IndiGo flights a great go-to-market move because it reached the right consumer. He says the limiting factor was price, not the number of consumers, and that this led to Swing, a Rs 10 brand.
- IndiGo: his example of a brand built on one promise, being on time. He says it published longer flight times than rivals to include ground operations.
- Titan: he describes its model as taking an unorganised market and organising it with standard service and a promise of quality, first in watches, then jewellery, then eyewear, and now sarees.
- The Whole Truth and Epigamia: his examples of brands that picked a premium core consumer and kept launching products for that same consumer.
- OYO: his example of a brand that brings one thing to mind, which is cost.
- His own pizzeria: he noticed he had stopped eating pizza, tried a Neapolitan pizza, asked people around him about their pizza habits, found many had also stopped, and launched on that insight.

## Numbers given

- He recalls Paper Boat launching at Rs 25 to 30, in a market where he puts Rs 10 drinks at about 70% of single-serve beverages. He notes his data is about ten years old.
- He puts the Rs 30 beverage market at about Rs 1,000 crore at that time.
- He says Paper Boat's aamras had 45% pulp against about 10% in the mainstream mango drinks.
- He says that around 2013 companies spent under 5 to 6% of their annual marketing budget on digital, and at most under 10%.
- He gives Tanishq's net profit margin as roughly 5 to 8%.
- He says an energy drink at Rs 20 to 25 had price as its differentiation in 2010 to 2011, when Red Bull cost Rs 90.
- His IndiGo example: other airlines listed Mumbai to Bangalore as 1 hour 20 minutes and IndiGo listed 1 hour 50 minutes.
- He says Myntra has only now posted profits after 15 years, and that Paper Boat is still not profitable.
