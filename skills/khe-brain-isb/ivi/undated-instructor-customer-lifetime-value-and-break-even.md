# Customer lifetime value, break-even on a promotion and valuing a startup by its customers

The same marketing professor as the previous day's class on diffusion ran a worked exercise on customer lifetime value. The class calculated whether a promotion costing 10,000 dollars was worth accepting, then he explained how the same method supports a startup's valuation. The last part of the recording is a class discussion of one founder's business, which is left out here apart from the general points. This was his final session with the cohort.

## What was said

### Where marketing returns come from

- The instructor says the return on any marketing investment arrives in one of two forms, or both: a rise in the lifetime value of existing customers, or the lifetime value of newly acquired customers.
- He says a promotion meant to acquire customers is judged by how many new customers it must bring for their lifetime value to cover its cost.
- He says this calculation is an input to a decision, and tells the class "Do not be a slave to these numbers".
- His example of using the number in a negotiation is that if break-even comes to about 100 customers, the company can ask the promoter to guarantee a slightly higher minimum.

### What a lifetime value estimate is

- He says customer lifetime value is always a forecast, and every forecasting model has assumptions and a time period built into it.
- He says different assumptions give different answers, so the founder makes reasonable assumptions and states them.
- He says the class deliberately underestimates, to stay conservative.
- He says exact accuracy is not what matters. Decisions rest on the relative lifetime value of the options, and an error in one assumption affects every option the same way, so the ranking still holds.
- He says the estimate is not about perfection but about the best a founder can do in a reasonable way.

### The printer exercise

- The exercise is a retailer offered a promotion for 10,000 dollars that brings in customers who buy a printer bundle. The class works out the margin on the first purchase and the discounted margin on repeat cartridge purchases over the printer's life.
- The first purchase mixes a printer sold at a negative margin with a cable and a cartridge sold at positive margins.
- Each later year's value is the cartridge price, grown for price increases, times the cartridges bought per year, times the margin, times the retention rate, divided by one plus the discount rate raised to the year.
- Dividing the promotion cost by the lifetime value of one customer gives the number of new customers needed to break even.
- He says conceptually this is a check on the cost of customer acquisition inside the promoter's proposal. If the promotion acquires many customers for the money, the answer is yes.
- He suggests running the calculation under several sets of assumptions to get a range.

### From lifetime value to company valuation

- He says a founder can estimate the lifetime value of an average customer, multiply by the number of customers and get the value of today's cohort.
- He says a founder without their own data can use category or industry data to get a reasonable range.
- He says the founder then adds future cohorts from the planned acquisition rate, with each older cohort worth a little less every year, and the running total is customer equity.
- He says this ties the valuation directly to customer behaviour and how it will change, which lets the founder justify the number asked for instead of bargaining over arbitrary percentages.
- He says it also lets the founder show that investing in a given activity raises the value by a given amount.
- He says the estimate is easiest to do at the cohort level.
- He says early-stage startups are very hard to value, and high-level data makes it harder, but this method can still be justified.
- He says a company growing customers 10% a year while churn runs faster is a bubble, and the lifetime value view exposes it.
- He says people used to look at cross-sectional data and count customers acquired. The time series shows whether a customer becomes net positive over time, which he calls the safety valve to always look for.
- He says a positive lifetime value under reasonable assumptions means it is acceptable not to earn money from the customer today.

### Convincing yourself before investors

- He says if the numbers genuinely make sense to the founder, they will make sense to a smart investor.
- He says the worst outcome is to get money for a business the founder is not convinced by and then lose it, so a founder who is not convinced should not ask investors for money.
- He tells the class they do this estimate because it protects their own company, not because a professor asked.

### Platforms and lifetime value

- He says a platform company looks at lifetime value on both sides of the platform.
- He repeats that a two-sided business must create value on both sides. A model that gives the benefit to one side while the other side bears the cost and effort will not get that side to join.

## Examples and cases used

- A printer bundle sold by a retailer: a loss-making printer with profitable cartridges, used to show that the value of an acquired customer sits in repeat purchases.
- Johnson and Johnson: he mentions that consumer goods companies keep costly warehouses in cities, as a reason a logistics idea could have some room.

## Numbers given

- 10,000 dollars: the cost of the promotion in the exercise.
- 12.99 dollars and 85%: the cartridge price and margin used in the exercise.
- 40.22: the first-year value of one customer worked out in class.
- 81.95: the lifetime value per customer the class reaches in the recording, which gives 123 new customers to break even.
- The written class notes attached to the recording list the other inputs as a 3-year printer life, 4 cartridges a year, retention of 100%, 80% and 50%, a 2% yearly price rise and a 12% discount rate, and work the same exercise to 95.48 per customer and 105 customers. The two results differ, and the recording does not show which inputs caused the gap.
