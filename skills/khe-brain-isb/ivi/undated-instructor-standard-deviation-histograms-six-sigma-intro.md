# Spread, standard deviation and histograms before Six Sigma

The opening part of an operations management class taught by the course instructor, who is not named in the recording. He explains central tendency, spread and standard deviation by intuition, then uses a restaurant's delivery times to show how an average misleads and how a histogram shows the truth. The recording stops at a break, before Six Sigma itself is taught.

## What was said

### Mean and spread together

- The instructor's first example is three people who arrive 10, 11 and 12 minutes late to a class. The central tendency is 11 minutes and the spread is from 10 to 12.
- He compares this with a second class whose mean lateness is also close to 11 minutes but whose values are spread much wider.
- On the mean alone the two classes look the same, he says, so performance has to be read from central tendency and spread together.
- He defines spread as the lowest and highest values the data set has actually taken, not the values it could possibly take.
- He says spread can be measured in three ways: range, variance and standard deviation.
- His marks example is a student who scores 40, 70 and 60 in three courses and another who scores 10, 100 and 75. Both average roughly 60 percent, but the second failed one subject.
- If he went only by the mean, he says, he would give both a first class degree and that decision would be wrong.

### Standard deviation by intuition

- He explains deviation in plain English as how far something is from a reference point, and says he fixes the mean of the data set as that reference point.
- The natural measure would be the average of all the deviations, he says, but deviations on one side cancel those on the other. Someone 5 minutes early and someone 5 minutes late add to zero.
- That would give the impression that there is no deviation in the class when there is.
- The fix is to square each deviation, because a negative times a negative and a positive times a positive are both positive, so the values no longer cancel.
- He calls this squaring step standardisation, and says it is what gives standard deviation its name.
- The squared values are averaged and then the square root is taken to bring the figure back to normal values.
- He says the question standard deviation answers is not how many minutes people are late. It is how far the data can deviate from its mean.
- For a sample, as opposed to a population, he says the denominator is n minus 1 and not n, because that is the degrees of freedom.
- Variance is the square of the standard deviation. He says it does not give the true deviation but is a good measure for comparing two data sets.
- Range is the maximum value in the data set minus the minimum value.

### What sigma is not

- He asks the class what Six Sigma means and gets answers such as the highest level of quality and continuous improvement.
- He tells the class firmly that sigma should not be equated with standard deviation, and says trainers who teach that on the first day are wrong.
- He says the summation symbol, also called sigma, has nothing to do with the concept either.

### How an average hides defects

- His example is a restaurant that promises delivery to the table in 10 minutes. A customer orders the same dish for 30 days and the time from order to table is recorded each day.
- The owner asks the manager whether customers are being served within 10 minutes, and the average comes to 9.2 minutes, a true average with no fudging of data.
- Compared with the promise it looks as if the restaurant beats customer expectations, yet after 30 days the customer is unhappy.
- He says most organisations report dashboards this way, so the dashboard always looks green while customers leave and nobody knows why.
- In his words an average is a theoretical calculation, and a customer never experiences an average.
- He says an average reported in isolation is meaningless and is not the right way to look at a KPI.

### Building the histogram

- He takes the lowest value in the data, about 8 minutes, and the highest, about 20 minutes, and divides that range into bins such as 8 to 9 minutes.
- He counts how many of the 30 days fall in each bin. In his data five days fall in the 8 to 9 minute bin. The count is the frequency.
- Plotting the bins against their frequencies as a bar chart gives a frequency distribution, which has the special name histogram.
- He says a histogram is not rocket science. It is the same data plotted from a frequency table.
- On the histogram he marks the promise of 10 minutes. Everything beyond it is the defect area.
- He defines a defect as not meeting the customer's expectation.
- In his example the share of deliveries that kept the promise is around 40 percent and the share that missed it is more than 60 percent, although the average was 9.2 minutes.
- He says behind every bell curve there is a histogram.
- He says a bell curve is read for three things against the customer's expectation: the defect area, the average performance and the superior performance.

## Examples and cases used

- Students arriving late to class: two groups with the same mean and different spread.
- Marks across three courses: the same average hides a failed subject.
- A restaurant's 10 minute delivery promise: an average inside the promise while most deliveries miss it.

## Numbers given

- 9.2 minutes: the average delivery time over 30 days in the instructor's restaurant example, against a promise of 10 minutes.
- More than 60 percent: the share of those deliveries that missed the promise once the data was put in a histogram.
- 10, 11 and 12 minutes: the three lateness values that give a mean of 11 in his first example.
