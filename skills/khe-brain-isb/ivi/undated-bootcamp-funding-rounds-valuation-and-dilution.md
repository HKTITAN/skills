# Funding rounds, valuation and dilution

Speaker and date are withheld. This note keeps general teaching points only and leaves out the speaker's own frameworks, examples and materials. It covers how an early round is priced, how much equity a round usually costs, how long a round lasts, multiples and comparables, what an acquirer pays for, and the Indian instruments used for unvalued and priced rounds.

## What was said

### Pricing a round when there is no revenue

- The instructor says that with no revenue and no profit there is no valuation to calculate, only a negotiation over how much equity is given for the money raised.
- The instructor says valuation at this stage is the investment divided by the stake given, so the same cheque values the company at twice as much when the founder gives 10% as when the founder gives 20%.
- The instructor says that number reflects the dynamics of the negotiation and not how good the idea is.
- The instructor explains post-money valuation as the investment divided by the stake, and pre-money valuation as post-money minus the investment. The example given is 2 crore rupees for 10%, which is 20 crore post-money and 18 crore pre-money.
- The instructor says revenue multiples quoted for early deals are calculated afterwards by databases, and the deal itself was priced on something else.
- The instructor describes two ways a round gets priced. One starts from how much the founder will dilute for the money needed to run for about two years, and the other starts from revenue.
- The instructor says founders should not push for the highest possible early valuation, because the next round is priced on revenue and can come in lower if revenue has not grown into the earlier number.

### How much equity a round costs

- The instructor gives about 20% as the market standard that early-stage investors expect for a round.
- The instructor says the equity given is higher in early rounds, when traction is low and risk is high, and lower in later rounds.
- The instructor says early-round terms are a negotiation, and a founder with other investors ready can give less than the standard.
- The instructor says that if a cheque covers only half of what the company needs for two years, it should buy about half the equity a full cheque would, or the founder has paid double.
- The instructor says a founder in a sector with little deal activity will have to offer more than 20% to bring investors in.
- The instructor says founders of software as a service companies usually hold a larger share at IPO, because those companies raise less capital.
- The instructor says closing the deal sometimes matters more than the terms, because the company needs the capital.

### How long a round lasts and how long raising takes

- The instructor says each round should fund about two years, and no investor gives three years of money when they cannot see what the founder will do over three years.
- The instructor says raising a round takes about nine months of one person's full time: about six months to a term sheet and three more for due diligence, paperwork and the money arriving.
- The instructor says the accepted norm is to tell investors the money is for 18 months, while planning to make it last 24 months in case the next raise takes longer.
- The instructor says that if the money runs out during a slow raise, the team leaves and the founder starts again from zero.
- The instructor gives about ten years as the usual time from starting to an IPO, with rounds about two years apart.

### Multiples and comparables

- The instructor says there is no single right multiple, and a deal is a debate between several ways of looking at value.
- The instructor says the most common mistake is asking for a multiple because a competitor got it, since the investor will answer with a different lens and the founder has no reply.
- The instructor says multiples are applied to achieved numbers and not to next year's projection. Projections are used to sell the deal.
- The instructor says a comparable deal has to be recent. Founders should filter first for recent transactions, then for the closest competitor, stage and investor type.
- The instructor says later-stage private equity investors rely more on discounted cash flow and profit multiples, and their revenue multiples are lower.
- The instructor says profit margin is one lens and return on capital is another, and a low-margin business can still earn a high return on capital if it turns its capital over quickly.
- The instructor advises mapping every competitor's funding history before meeting investors: when each raised, how much, from whom, the gap between rounds and whether the company survived.

### What an acquirer pays for

- The instructor says that in an acquisition the seller's story about the future stops mattering, because the buyer is buying an asset and the future depends on the buyer's own execution.
- The instructor says an acquirer looks at how quickly the capital comes back, and most want to recover the principal within about three years.
- The instructor says the same business with the same numbers gets its lowest value in an acquisition, its highest when raising growth capital, and a value in between at an IPO.
- The instructor says an equity investor gets no dividend, so the return comes from selling shares at a later round, an acquisition or an IPO.
- The instructor says founders should not present acquisition as the exit plan to an investor, because acquisition multiples are low.

### Unvalued rounds and convertible notes

- The instructor says an unvalued round is used when founder and investor cannot agree on a valuation but both want the deal.
- The instructor says a convertible note is debt that is repaid in equity, that India has allowed it since 2016, and that the minimum from one investor is 25 lakh rupees.
- The instructor gives a 20% discount to the next round's valuation as the market standard on an unvalued round.
- The instructor's example is 1 crore rupees on a note with a 20% discount. If the next round values the company at 10 crore, the note converts at 8 crore, which gives the note holder 12.5% and not 10%.
- The instructor says a discount is another way of paying interest. The alternative is interest that accrues and converts, which suits a founder who expects to close a priced round within months.
- The instructor says investors rarely accept a discount alone and add a valuation cap, so that a very high next round cannot shrink their stake.
- The instructor says a founder who accepts a cap should ask for a floor, so that the note cannot convert at a value so low that the investor takes most of the company.
- The instructor says a note needs conversion triggers: a definition of how much money raised counts as a valuation event, and a time limit after which it converts.
- The instructor warns against stacking one unvalued round on another, because earlier note holders will object.
- The instructor says a convertible note is a 7 to 10 page document with little compliance, but the company's articles of association must already allow it. If they do not, the company has to pass a shareholder resolution and file the change first.
- The instructor says a convertible note and a SAFE are the same thing for a founder's purposes.

### Compulsorily convertible debentures

- The instructor says investors putting in less than 25 lakh rupees each are pooled through compulsorily convertible debentures, on the same commercial terms as a note.
- The instructor says a debenture round takes two months or more, and the money cannot be used until the whole round closes.
- The instructor suggests signing notes with the investors who meet the 25 lakh minimum first and pooling the rest in a debenture round.
- The instructor says founders should understand the terms and leave the choice of instrument to the company secretary.

### Preference shares

- The instructor says investors take compulsorily convertible preference shares so that they get their principal back first if the company sells low, and convert to equity for the upside if it sells high.
- The instructor's example is an investor who put in 2 crore rupees at a 10 crore valuation. If the company sells for 5 crore, the investor takes 2 crore first and the equity holders share the rest.
- The instructor explains participating preference as taking the principal back and then also taking a percentage share of what remains.
- The instructor gives a 1x non-participating liquidation preference as the market standard, and tells founders not to sign at 1.5x or 2x.

### Founder loans and idle cash

- The instructor says founders should put money into the company as a director's loan and spend from the company, and not pay company bills from their own pocket.
- The instructor says early investors often bar the use of their money to repay a director's loan, while a later and larger investor will usually let it be repaid.
- The instructor advises keeping that loan as a loan and not converting it to equity.
- The instructor says a tax officer can assume interest on a director's loan, and suggests putting a modest interest rate on it.
- The instructor says funding that is not yet needed should sit in fixed deposits with staggered maturities, with about three months of cash kept in the bank.

## Numbers given

- About 20%: the instructor's market standard for dilution in an early round.
- 18 months stated and 24 months planned: the instructor's guide for how long a round should last.
- About 9 months: the instructor's figure for raising a round, six to a term sheet and three to close.
- About 3 years: the payback period the instructor says most acquirers look for.
- 25 lakh rupees: the minimum per investor in a convertible note in India, as the instructor gives it.
- 20%: the instructor's market standard discount on an unvalued round.
- 1x non-participating: the instructor's market standard for liquidation preference.
- About 10,000 to 15,000 rupees for convertible note paperwork and about 2 lakh rupees in professional fees for a debenture round: the instructor's cost figures.
