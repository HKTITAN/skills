# Negotiation packages, MESOs and scoring systems

A session of the six-part negotiation course, taught by the course professor, who is not named in this recording. She debriefs a multi-issue licensing exercise the class had done, uses a recruiter and candidate exercise from her other courses as the running example, and takes questions on power and fairness. ## What was said

### Not every issue is a fight

- The instructor says people carry a fixed-pie bias into business negotiations and assume every negotiation is zero-sum.
- She says the two sides' preferences are sometimes fully aligned. Her example is a professor whose contract raises his salary each time he publishes a paper, which is what the school also wants.
- She says that when one issue such as salary is fixed, you can add other issues. Her examples for a faculty job are research budget, travel budget and conference budget.
- She says an added issue has to be a real one that matters, because contracts are in writing and a phantom issue cannot be carried into them.
- She says most business negotiations with several issues are integrative, so the issues are not all distributive.

### Asking and listening

- She says to ask questions, listen, ask follow-up questions, and ask the same question in different ways to learn what the other side really wants.
- She says to always talk about the underlying interest behind a demand, since it is hard to satisfy both sides without knowing it.
- She says research shows people trust us more when we reveal information about ourselves, so revealing your interests is acceptable.
- She links trust to recall: if the other side can remember you, they are more likely to think well of you and trust you.

### Post-settlement settlement

- She describes a post-settlement settlement: once a deal is signed, ask whether there is anything else the other side cares about and keep talking.
- She says people are more willing to discuss untouched issues once an agreement is already in place.
- The rule she gives is that the new contract is signed only if it is better than the first one.
- She says the aim is a Pareto optimal outcome, where no money is left on the table.

### Why issue by issue fails

- In her recruiter and candidate exercise there are eight issues and a points table for each side.
- She says people start with salary because it carries the most points, and since salary is distributive they end up in a fight.
- She says that on some issues both sides score highest on the same option, yet pairs still settle in the middle on them.
- She says pairs tend to settle in the middle on every issue, which leaves value unused.
- She says nobody wins on every issue. You give up what matters less to get what matters more.
- She says pairs that made such trade-offs in the class exercise ended up with both sides better off.

### Scoring system

- She says a multi-issue negotiation cannot be done without a scoring system.
- She defines a scoring system as a way to put numbers on subjective issues as well as money issues, so that offers can be compared and traded off.
- She says the scoring sheet should hold every issue that matters to you, and the other side's issues too where you can work them out.
- Her analogy is the points students bid for elective courses and exchange places: the points are arbitrary, but without them nobody could decide who gets what.
- Her cautionary example is a student who spent half his points on one course in his first round of bidding without knowing what he wanted.

### Extreme offers and agreement bias

- She says an opening offer far outside the zone of possible agreement only offends the other side, who also has a sense of where that zone lies.
- She says an extreme first offer invites an extreme counter-offer.
- Her rule for a ridiculous offer is never to negotiate from that number. Ask where it comes from and ask for data.
- She says that if you have a better alternative, you can walk away from a ridiculous offer.
- She says the goal of a negotiation is the best deal, not a deal. "Sometimes no deal is a good deal."
- She names the urge to close at any price agreement bias, and says she has run the recruiter exercise for six years and many people accept below their reservation price just to make a deal.

### Power is relative

- She says power in a negotiation is never absolute. It depends on what you have that the other side wants, how much they want it, and how many alternatives they have.
- She says founders and job candidates assume the investor or employer holds the power, and that this is not necessarily true.
- She says an interviewer has power over you only if you have no alternative, so you should always have alternatives.
- She tells the class not to sell themselves short because they think they lack power, since the perception of power is subjective.
- She says trust, fairness and power should be used together rather than relying on one.

### Fairness

- She says she cannot define what is fair for another person. She can only make them perceive the offer as fair.
- Her ways of doing that are showing your homework, showing you took their interests into account, and being transparent about how you arrived at the offer.

### MESOs

- She tells the class to create packages and never negotiate issue by issue, which she also calls sequential bargaining.
- MESOs are several packages offered at once. She says to build them so that you are indifferent between them.
- She says people like choices because they want some agency in a decision, and that this is one reason packages work.
- She says research shows that when both sides use MESOs and negotiate on packages, both get better outcomes.
- She says offering several packages shows the other side you have done your homework and are there to make a deal.
- She says packages give more chances for trade-offs than a single offer does.
- She notes that a similar but inferior option makes the option beside it look better, and that this can be used when building packages.
- She follows MESOs with many why and why not questions about the package the other side prefers.
- She says the only disadvantage of MESOs is that they reveal information about your own priorities.
- She says that if both sides keep misrepresenting what they want, both end up worse off, because creative solutions need some disclosure.
- She calls MESOs a good lie detection device, because the package the other side picks shows their real priorities.

### Threats

- She says a threat has to be a wise one. The first test is willingness to execute: you must be ready to carry it out.
- The second test she gives is interest: the threat must hurt something the other side cares about, or it does not matter.
- Her joke example of a threat nobody would believe is telling students she will jump out of the window if they come late.
- When threatened in a dispute, she says to go back to interests and be future focused.

### Teams and risk

- She says research shows teams get better outcomes than solo negotiators, so you should match a team with a team.
- She says three to five members works, three is generally a good number, and beyond five a team is hard to manage.
- She says that when something lies in the future and carries risk, the two sides can write a contingent contract to share that risk.

## Examples and cases used

- A recruiter and candidate exercise with eight issues: used to show that starting with salary leads to a fight and that people wrongly split every issue down the middle.
- Bidding points for elective courses at a business school: used to show why a scoring system is needed even when the numbers are arbitrary.
- A professor paid more for each published paper: used to show that preferences can be aligned.

## Numbers given

- Team size: she gives three to five members as the workable range for a negotiating team, with three as generally good.
- Six years: how long she says she has run the recruiter exercise.
