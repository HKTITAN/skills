# Capital budgeting with NPV and IRR

A finance class in the ivi core course, given by the course's finance instructor, who is not named in the recording. She works one example on slides and in Excel, a juice company deciding whether to launch a new drink, and takes questions throughout. The recording starts after the class has begun and runs through a break to the end.

## What was said

### What capital budgeting is for

- The instructor says capital budgeting is the analysis a business does before an investment decision, such as launching a product, opening a store, producing a video or entering a market.
- She says the same framework can be used to choose between two options and to prioritise among several.
- She says such decisions usually need money up front and return cash later, with no guarantee, so both timing and uncertainty have to be handled, and the time value of money framework handles both.
- She lists three inputs: the upfront outflow needed to start the project, the cash flows expected in each period, and the discount rate.

### Estimating the cash flows

- She gives two principles for cash flow estimates: count only things that are actually cash, and count only what is incremental to the decision.
- She defines incremental cash flow as the cash flows with the project minus the cash flows without it, where "without" is the status quo projection for the existing business.
- She says a new product can cannibalise an existing one or lift it, and only the net effect belongs to the project.
- She says the more a new product overlaps with an existing one, the greater the chance that users shift, and a premium version is less likely to cannibalise.
- She says estimating cannibalisation takes a mix of market research and judgment, for example surveying existing customers on whether they would switch and new customers on whether they are interested, or looking at what happened to existing products after an earlier launch.
- She says a project's cash flows are likely to be negative in its first years, and the later cash flows then have to be that much higher to justify putting the money in.

### From profit to free cash flow

- She starts from NOPAT, net operating profit after tax, which is EBIT minus EBIT multiplied by the tax rate, and calls it unlevered net income because it ignores how the business is financed.
- She says leverage is another word for borrowing, and the analysis looks only at the operating decision, not at how it is funded.
- She adds back depreciation and amortisation because no cash leaves the bank for them.
- She says depreciation is subtracted first only because the tax authorities allow it as an expense, so it reduces the tax bill, and once the tax is right it is added back.
- She then subtracts any increase in net working capital and subtracts capital expenditure to reach free cash flow.
- She describes free cash flow as the cash a project or business generates that it does not itself need and that can be used elsewhere.
- She says negative free cash flow means money has to be put into the business, and a business "turning cash flow positive" means it no longer needs additional money.
- She says the adjustments exist only to fix the gaps between how profit is calculated and how cash actually moves.

### Net working capital

- She defines net working capital as inventory plus accounts receivable minus accounts payable.
- She says money spent building unsold inventory is not in sales or expenses yet, so when inventory goes up, cash flow is lower than profit.
- She says the same holds for accounts receivable, because the sale is in profit but the cash has not arrived.
- She says accounts payable works the opposite way, because vendors are giving the business time to pay.
- Her rule to remember: when net working capital goes up, the business needs that much more money and it is a cash outflow, and when it goes down it is a cash inflow.
- She says net working capital typically rises as a business grows, though not always, because of some economies of scale.
- She says a business with no inventory and no receivables but some payables has negative working capital, which is not a bad thing, because customers pay at once and vendors give time.
- She says that in a business with negative working capital, growth makes it more negative, so vendors are financing part of the growth.
- She says an upfront investment such as revamping the supply chain can pay back by reducing the working capital the business needs.
- She says comparisons of these figures should be made only within the same type of business, or for the same business over time.
- Answering a founder in the room, she says unsold event tickets are not inventory, because no variable cost is spent per ticket and they expire.
- Answering another founder, she says content or IP that can be licensed for years is a long-term intangible asset, not inventory, because inventory stops being yours once it is sold.

### The NPV rule

- She says cash flows arriving at different times cannot simply be added, so each is discounted to today and the sum, including the upfront outflow, is the net present value.
- She says the discount rate is the opportunity cost of the investors, meaning the return they expect on average if they put the money elsewhere.
- She says this rate is not the fixed deposit rate and not nominal GDP growth, because it includes a risk premium that depends on the business and is always higher for startups.
- She says a higher discount rate makes cash flows that are further out even less valuable today.
- She says a positive NPV means the project gives back more than it takes after covering all spending and the minimum return investors need.
- Her rule: never take a project with negative NPV, take every positive NPV project if there are no capital constraints, and when only one can be chosen take the highest NPV.
- She defines mutually exclusive projects as those where only one can be picked from a set.
- She says NPV is an amount in rupees, not a percentage and not a score out of 100.
- She says another way to read the result is as the value today of the project's future cash flows, against which the upfront money is being raised.
- She suggests redoing the estimate under a pessimistic case, and treats a project that stays positive as a green signal.
- She says NPV has to cover the whole life of the project, and for a product that settles into a stable pattern you project five or seven years and then assume a constant growth rate beyond that.
- She says Excel's NPV function is applied to the cash flows from year one onward and the year zero cash flow is added separately.

### Why cash flow and not profit

- She says cash outflows are the capital owners and investors put in, and cash inflows are what the business gives back, so cash flow captures when money is locked in.
- She says profit is useful for a period-by-period view, but cash flow shows how much money has to be invested and at what points.
- She says the time between cash going out and coming back is the time over which investors have to be given their minimum return.
- She says that in a forecast, the sum of the negative cash flows is the total funding the business needs over that period.

### The IRR rule

- She defines IRR as the return per year that the project generates, expressed as a percentage.
- She says it is calculated as the discount rate at which NPV becomes zero, which she calls a mathematical trick and not the interpretation.
- Her rule: accept when IRR is above the cost of capital, which is the same as a positive NPV, and reject when it is below.
- She says many people find IRR more intuitive than NPV because it is a percentage per year.
- She says IRR differs from ROE or ROCE because those are worked out year by year from profit and capital employed, ignore the timing of cash flows and ignore needs such as working capital, while IRR is one number for the whole life of the project.
- She says the terms discount rate and cost of capital mean the same thing in this class.

### NPV profile

- She describes the NPV profile as a chart of a project's NPV at different discount rates, with the point where the line crosses zero being the IRR.
- She says it works as a sensitivity analysis when a founder knows only a range for the return investors want.
- She says that when capital is cheap the project with larger but later cash flows looks better, and when capital is costly later cash flows are punished.

### Limits of each rule

- She says IRR ignores the scale of a project, so it can mislead when a very small project is compared with a very large one.
- She says IRR assumes every extra rupee invested earns the same return, which is reasonable for a VC or a mutual fund that can spread money across many investments.
- She says NPV ignores the possibility of replication, such as opening ten more stores if the first one works.
- She says NPV suits startups focused on scale and is also used by large corporations for internal decisions.
- She says IRR is useful for bootstrapped or capital-constrained companies that need to prioritise projects, and is the measure private equity, venture capital and other investment funds focus on.
- She says mutual funds report the same idea under the name XIRR.

### Startups and investors

- She says DCF is still the fundamental way to understand valuation, but startups are cash flow negative for a long time and their estimates are very uncertain, so other valuation methods are used as proxies.
- She says VCs think about when an exit by IPO or acquisition will come, and the longer it takes the more growth they look for.
- She says an early stage company can be cash flow negative overall because it keeps investing in growth, while each project or store is still judged on its own incremental cash flows.
- She says entering an already competitive market to chase market share can be an emotional decision, and the projections should at least make a founder stop and ask why.
- She says exponential growth matters to VCs because only that produces the level of IRR they look for.

## Examples and cases used

- Nirvana Juices and Beverages, the class example: an existing drinks company deciding whether to launch a strawberry smoothie, used to build free cash flow line by line and then to compare two possible launches.
- Swiggy launching a new service and Amazon entering quick commerce late: decisions she says would have gone through this kind of analysis first.
- Restaurants: a business with little inventory, almost no receivables and some payables, so working capital close to zero or negative.
- A process improvement against a marketing campaign: her illustration that a small project with a high IRR can create less total value than a large project with a lower IRR.
- A single store that could be repeated ten times: her illustration of what NPV leaves out.

## Numbers given

- In the Nirvana Juices example the upfront investment is 10 lakh rupees, depreciated in a straight line over 5 years, so 2 lakh a year.
- In the same example, year one sales are 20,000 bottles at 100 rupees a bottle, cost of goods sold is 40% of revenue, and other fixed expenses are 3 lakh.
- The example assumes 2.6 lakh of extra working capital in year one, and year one free cash flow comes to 5 lakh.
- In her working capital illustration, payables rising from 10 lakh to 12 lakh is a cash inflow of 2 lakh.
- She uses a 10% discount rate for the example and calls it generously low, the kind of rate that fits money from friends and family.
- At 10%, the smoothie project has an NPV of about 79,000 rupees on a 10 lakh investment, and the second project about 50,000 rupees.
- She notes 79,000 on 10 lakh is about 8% in total, over and above the 10% a year already built into the calculation.
- Her simplest IRR example: put in 80 today, receive 100 a year later, IRR 25%.
- She gives the second project's IRR as about 11.8%, and says both projects clear the 10% cost of capital.
- Her scale example is a process improvement with an IRR of 100% against a marketing campaign costing ten times as much with an IRR of 50%.
- She gives 30 to 50% as the IRR VCs typically look for, and says a business that cannot show that is not suited to that source of funding.
- She uses 3% a year as an example of a constant growth rate after the projection period.
