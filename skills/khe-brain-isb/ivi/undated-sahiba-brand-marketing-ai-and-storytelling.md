# Brand marketing, AI and storytelling, Sahiba

A marketer and actor who ran brand and content work at Zomato spoke to the cohort in a question and answer format. She is addressed as Sahiba and names Chandan Mendiratta as her former boss. The session moved from her Zomato work to AI in advertising, brand authenticity, and questions from founders. The recording is undated.

## What was said

### Building a mass brand at Zomato

- Sahiba says her aim at Zomato was that people think Zomato, eat Zomato and talk about Zomato, so that the brand name becomes the generic word the way Xerox did.
- She says Zomato is a mass brand and not a luxury brand, which let her make it very accessible.
- Her method was to put the brand inside whatever people watch: YouTube, films, music videos and stand-up shows.
- She says the Zomato YouTube channel was launched with zero budget, so she fronted the content herself.

### Saturation

- She says social listening on Zomato's push notifications first showed people calling them cute, and later showed comments that people were sick of them and that the brand was trying too hard.
- She says every brand and every person has a saturation point, and the fix is as simple as taking a break.
- Saturation does not mean the brand switches off. A brand has several target groups, and once it has achieved what it wanted with the first group it moves to the second.

### Language and regions

- She says a mass brand must accept that people speak different languages and have different purchasing power, and that a generic campaign can be adapted into many languages.
- Her team's voice was Hindi first until they saw they were speaking to only a part of the market, and then they moved into Tamil and other regional languages.
- She calls it "a little pompous" for a brand to think in only one language when its market is everywhere.

### AI in advertising

- She says a campaign used to start from a problem statement, such as entering a market or telling an audience about a sale. She finds it a problem that teams now start from a new AI tool and then release a campaign.
- Her Zomato example: with only a few hours of a celebrity's time, the team generated 250 versions of one ad, each naming a restaurant in the viewer's own pin code.
- She says this makes advertising cost effective, because one creative can become a thousand ads.
- Her Nike example, which she says she read about: the brand uses data about a person to send an email saying a new pickleball court has opened near their house, it will rain tomorrow, and waterproof shoes are available. She adds that this is also scary because personal data is for sale.
- She predicts that generic mass campaigns, and the money put into them, will become obsolete in a few years.
- The bad side, in her words, is that AI content is detached from human emotion and comes in such quantity that it does not resonate. She asked the room to recall an ad seen last week and nobody could.
- She says brand retention is going to be a huge problem, and that brands should not rely fully on AI.
- She says brands that keep telling stories and take time over one to one personalisation have a very loyal following.
- Her rule is a human core with AI used for execution. Relying on AI 100% will never work.
- On budget, she would use AI for all performance ads, where an ad is only about 6 seconds and she can make more than 300 versions by region, language and pin code. The money saved goes to the bigger seasonal campaigns.
- Asked whether AI will make storytelling easier, she says AI makes it more efficient, and the question is whether it will be effective.

### Humanising a brand

- She says humanising works for mass brands and will never work for a luxury brand.
- A brand must never come across as a superior entity to its consumer, and she says brands often make this mistake in their communication.
- A brand should be accessible everywhere its consumer is, and that takes a very high level of research about the consumer.
- Her example of that research: Zomato needed to raise orders between 11pm and 4am, so it ran ads on an adult website where that late-night audience was.
- To reach the 15 to 24 age group she went to YouTube, found a streamer who was getting large views for chess, and made content with him, which she says blew up.
- She says Indian consumers are smart and marketers should stop treating them as stupid.

### Do not copy a blueprint

- Her first advice to people building their own brands is not to replicate anyone. She says even inspiration becomes seated in your head and you try to reproduce it.
- She says to create your own niche, and that the same applies to the business itself and not only to the brand.
- She says memes, trending reels and out of the box content only work once the brand is established.
- She ties this to the product life cycle: in the growth stage do not chase viral content, and only at the maturity stage take those twists.
- For a startup she would build the company's brand first and the founder's personal brand after it. She compares the two to a see-saw.

### Authenticity

- She says she turns down well paid brand work when the brand does not resonate with her.
- She uses the term cognitive dishonesty for a public figure promoting something that contradicts what they have said they stand for.
- She says an endorser does not have to use the product, but if their whole personality is against something and they still promote it, it will not make sense to the audience.

### Virality and staying aware

- She asks marketers to delete the word virality, because nobody can quantify it or predict it, and she says even AI cannot predict it.
- She says no framework for clutter-breaking content exists, because trends in India change by the minute.
- Her one request is to be aware of everything happening in the country: geopolitics, culture, history, cricket matches, politics, even the weather.
- She says a brand that knows what is happening, even in a small city, can create a broad campaign with a single tweet.

### Purpose of an ad

- She says the question to ask of any ad is its purpose: customer acquisition, sales, or making the brand name known.
- She says a new brand should first establish what the brand is, and virality and trends should come later.

### Research without a budget

- She says the company will not give you money for research, and she paid for some tools on her own card.
- She recommends Google Trends because it is free.
- She used Statista but warns to check the year, because it also holds old data.
- Her third method is to talk to a sample of at least 50 people from the target group and ask them questions yourself.

### Personal brand and platforms

- She says juggling several careers gave her an identity crisis, because she could not answer the question of what she does. She decided to make that her brand and "own the narrative".
- She says each platform has a different job. People come to Google to figure things out, they go to Instagram to switch off, and on LinkedIn they present a different self.
- She says it is important to keep that division and not post the same kind of content everywhere.

### Hiring a first marketer

- The first thing she would check is attitude, before degree or experience.
- She says marketing and content need someone available 24x7, and a person who says "I can't do it right now" will not work.
- The second check is familiarity with the platform, whether Instagram, YouTube, LinkedIn or performance ads.
- She says she knew little about YouTube when she launched the channel and had to study it, sit with people from Google and take online courses, which took time it should not have.
- Degrees and marks come later for her, and she would hire someone without a marketing background.

### Nostalgia marketing

- Answering a founder in the room, she says the first question in nostalgia marketing is which cohort, because nostalgia means a different decade to millennials, Gen Z and boomers.
- She says sports and entertainment stay constant across generations.
- She warns that a founder's own age pulls the brand's idea of nostalgia toward the founder's own decade.

## Examples and cases used

- Zomato: 250 pin-code personalised versions of one celebrity ad, late-night ads placed where the audience was, and brand placement in films, music videos and stand-up.
- Cadbury's Shah Rukh Khan ad: named as the known case of the same personalised ad technique.
- Nike: hyper-personalised email using location and weather data.
- Comet: she says it did not replicate Nike or Adidas and is doing very well.
- McDonald's: the yellow arch is recognised with no branding or copy.
- Amul: it knows everything happening in the country, releases one artwork, and she says it has never performed badly.
- CRED's Indiranagar ka Gunda ad: she says everyone remembered it and it established the brand name, but it brought no tangible consumers, and it would have performed far better later in the brand's life.
- Dream11: she says it timed its marketing smartly.
- American Eagle and Sydney Sweeney: she says the campaign was criticised in the US but the brand stayed true to its ethos, which she appreciates.
- Alia Bhatt: her example of cognitive dishonesty, calling sugar poison on a talk show and appearing in a Frooti ad a month later.
- Christopher Nolan films: remembered because the action and effects are real, which she applies to content and ads.

## Numbers given

- 250: versions of one Zomato ad generated from a single celebrity shoot.
- 236: digital ads an average person is exposed to per day, which she attributes to a Harvard study.
- 6 seconds: length of a performance ad in her budgeting example.
- More than 300: versions of one performance ad she says she can make with AI.
- 11pm to 4am: the window in which Zomato wanted to raise orders.
- 15 to 24: the age group she targeted through YouTube.
- At least 50: people to speak to when doing your own customer research.
