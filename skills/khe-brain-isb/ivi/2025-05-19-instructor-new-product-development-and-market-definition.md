# New product development and defining a market, Professor Siddharth

The first of five sessions of the Marketing and Market Readiness course, taught by a marketing professor at ISB who gives only his first name in the recording and who earlier worked at Johnson and Johnson. He lectures from slides, tells cases from his own career and takes many questions. The session covers the new product development funnel, how context decides how fast to go to market, the customer-centric marketing process, growth hacking, types of needs and how to define a market.

## What was said

### Why marketing and innovation are different

- Professor Siddharth says the knowledge a company must hold itself to exist is its brand name, its understanding of customers and its relationship with them, and the new products and services that make customers choose it.
- He says every other function can be outsourced and the company survives, but a company that outsources marketing and innovation does not exist as a proper company.
- He says marketing and innovation are intertwined in new product development and raise much the same issues.
- He says in finance, HR or operations you succeed by doing better than last time on your KPIs, but in marketing and innovation you succeed only by beating everybody else in the marketplace.
- He says a formula cannot win in this area because everyone went to the same business schools and applies the same formula.
- He says a founder has to ask why a framework was made, whether it fits today's context, and then apply it, modify it or discard it.
- He says that understanding comes only from first principles, and that lasting success comes from the deepest grasp of concepts and knowing how to adapt them to your own context.
- He says without conceptual understanding a founder falls back on copying what someone else did.
- He treats product, service, offering and solution as the same term for the whole course.

### The new product development funnel

- He describes four stages of new product development: the fuzzy front end, concept development and testing, pre-test market simulation, and launch followed by product management.
- He says the fuzzy front end is the idea generation or opportunity identification stage, and it is fuzzy wherever the idea comes from, R&D, a founder's head or deliberate strategy.
- He says ideas are a dime a dozen, and one idea can be turned into a final solution in thousands of ways, so the possibilities have to be narrowed like a funnel.
- He says no company can take every idea to the end because it is too expensive, so the aim is to eliminate low potential concepts quickly and carry the promising ones forward.
- He says a concept test puts concepts in front of real or potential consumers, drops those below the bar, and gives diagnostic information on how to revise the rest.
- He says talking to family, friends and colleagues about an idea is informal concept testing, and a survey is the formal kind.
- He calls concept testing the heart of the new product development process.
- He says pre-test market systems, sold by research firms, grew up because companies found test marketing had many disadvantages and wanted real market feedback without exposing the product.
- He says anything done to get information, analyse it and use it is marketing research, and some mix of formal and informal research informs every stage.
- He says the stages are split differently in different books, and other schemes such as proof of concept, prototype and MVP are special cases of this funnel.

### Context decides how fast to go

- He says marketing research for a new product should be tailored, because standard tools used without thought are unlikely to fit something new.
- He says the context of a new product is defined by three factors: the purpose of the project, market and company newness, and opportunity cost and development risk.
- He gives market share expansion, profitability and technology demonstration as possible purposes.
- He says every concept test should check each concept against that purpose, and since the common purpose is market share, the test should ask respondents how likely they are to purchase.
- He defines company newness as how new the product is to the company, meaning how well its operations, marketing strengths, customer knowledge and assets fit it.
- He says when both market and company newness are low, the concept test asks what the respondent uses now and how likely they are to buy, so the company can see which concepts cannibalise the most and drop those.
- He says when the market knows the product but the company is new to it, product and company fit is the issue, and the test should ask how credible people find this company offering it.
- He says the challenge when the product is new to the market is market development, meaning educating customers who have never seen it.
- He says where serious market development is needed and funds are limited, a company should avoid being first in the market.
- He says there is no first mover advantage, only first successful mover advantage.
- He says when both the market and the company are new to the product, the founder must first decide whether to enter at all, given limited resources and knowledge.
- He defines opportunity cost here as the cost of missing a fast-moving window of opportunity, not the economic opportunity cost.
- He defines development risk as the company's own assessment of how costly it is to go to market with a product that is not good enough.
- He says when development risk and opportunity cost are both high, the company must enter quickly and also get it right, which means thorough testing done very fast and therefore a lot of resources.
- He says when development risk is high and opportunity cost is low, the plan is to test again and again and not take risks.
- He says when development risk is low and opportunity cost is high, the company should go to market fast with basic functions and let users guide what to add.
- He says an MVP only makes sense where development risk is low, and for a product like an electric car the minimum viable product is the full product.
- He says investors keep asking for an MVP because they grew up with products whose development risk was almost always low.
- He says these are guide rails from a marketing view only, and the decision stays with the founder, whose motives and resources differ.

### How much research to do

- He says marketing research exists to inform decisions, so if the decision will not change, do no research.
- He says research is always done within a cost and time limit, and the aim is the best you can do, never perfection.
- He says a founder who knows the field well can manage with far less research, because their own insight fills the gap.
- He says it is better to get a little high quality information than a lot of garbage.
- He says marketing research never gives the answer, and the founder must use it and then apply judgment.
- He says analytics from your own website cover only the people who came, so they cannot tell you why others do not come.

### Choosing which battle to fight

- He says a founder does not have a right to success, and can do everything correctly and still most likely fail.
- He says the targeting decision is the choice of which battle to fight, and it is the critical decision in the business.
- He says the most common problem he sees across hundreds of Indian startups is a poor targeting decision, with people quick to enter a business and slow to regret it.
- He says a founder should take as long as needed to prepare and choose which business to enter.
- He says a founder who has spent years in an area with big players and has lost the window should decide when to cut losses.
- He says the most important asset in a startup is the founder, who should think of themselves as a full-time unpaid employee and ask where that person has the highest chance of winning.
- He says investors in a platform business look past the product to the long fight against large incumbents, because network effects leave room for only one or two winners.
- He says large companies now have a far greater advantage than they did, and it is tougher to become very successful than it was 30 years ago.
- He says a market leader built on trust and reputation will not take the risk of launching radical innovation, which leaves an opening for startups that it later acquires.
- He says disruption in Christensen's sense is a simple version of a product gaining a foothold in niches that big companies' complex products do not serve.

### The customer-centric marketing process

- He says customer-centric marketing starts with a need, which is simply a problem someone has that the company wants to solve, whether or not the person knows it or can say it.
- He says people differ in what they require, which he calls heterogeneity, and a large part of marketing is about handling it.
- He says mass marketing leaves many people less satisfied and one-to-one marketing is very costly, and segmentation is the sweet spot between them.
- He says heterogeneity is the timeless concept and segmentation is only the usual instrument for dealing with it.
- He says personalisation is segmenting at a finer and finer scale, while mass customisation lets customers tailor the solution themselves.
- He says the closer a company gets to each customer's need in a cost efficient way, the more loyal the customer.
- He says after targeting, marketing runs roughly independently in each targeted segment.
- He says within a segment the company studies what other solutions people use for the same problem and how each is positioned in their minds.
- He says the planned position is the answer to how the company wants people in the segment to think about its solution so they choose it over all others.
- He defines strategic marketing as market definition, segmentation, targeting and positioning, or in plain words what problem you solve, for whom, and how they should think about your solution.
- He says the fuzzy front end of product development is the same thing as strategic marketing.
- He says the elements of the marketing mix are used to design a strategy that creates and delivers superior value in a way that leads to the planned position.
- He says consumers do not care about a company's strategy, they have a problem and buy the solution they perceive as giving the most value.
- He says the 4Ps are only one way to group the elements of the marketing mix, which are "whatever you can do", and a founder can group them any way that helps.
- He says the 4P framework is flexible enough to fit a digital business if read properly, but a founder can build something better for their own context.
- He says a product has a core, a tangible and an augmented level, and a founder should always start with the core.
- He says R&D and marketing should work closely together from the idea stage until launch.

### Perception

- He says positioning is a mind game, and what people call reality is a social agreement.
- He says consumer decisions happen at two levels, instinctive and thoughtful.
- He says conditioning to think in agreed ways is a hindrance to innovation.

### Growth hacking

- He says growth hacking is rapid experimentation for growth, and conceptually it is an age-old practice.
- He says marketing research is of three types: exploratory, descriptive and causal.
- He says exploratory research uses interviews, reports and focus groups with judgment-based sampling, and descriptive research uses proper statistical sampling and surveys to describe a population.
- He says survey-based concept testing is descriptive research.
- He says causal research asks whether X causes Y and is done in practice mainly through experiments.
- He says exploratory and descriptive research rest on the hope that consumers will act as they answered, while a properly run experiment shows how they do act, so causal research is far more trustworthy.
- He says causal research used to be expensive and was used sparingly, mainly to test advertising, and digital technology has made it cheap and easy.
- He says causal research done before launch is just marketing research, and the same thing done after launching a version is called growth hacking.
- He says growth hacking can be used only where development risk is low, which is also where the term MVP has meaning.
- He says a company should always experiment with its communication.
- He says for the other elements of the marketing mix, experiment only with those where the risk is not a problem.

### Needs

- He says in any product the core need must be satisfied, and the other dimensions of the need are what make the product competitively attractive.
- He says needs have many dimensions, and the product may not even be the most important part of the solution.
- He says the most important question is who uses the product and why.
- He defines an existing need as one where the customer is willing and able to answer when asked directly.
- He defines a latent need as one where the customer, asked directly, is unwilling or unable to answer.
- He says with a latent need direct questions give the wrong answer, so the company has to infer it, for example by observing.
- He says the founder has to judge which kind of need they face before choosing a research method.
- He says the way to sharpen that judgment is to go and spend time with consumers and understand their emotions and fears, which they will not state.
- He says no survey result can be taken at face value on its own, because wording changes the answers, so questions are kept fixed and tracked over time against a benchmark.
- He says people will give an answer even when they do not know, so the mistake belongs to whoever asked.

### Defining the market

- He defines a market as those who have a need and are willing and able to enter into an exchange to satisfy it.
- He says under this definition different products can be in the same market, the same product can be in different markets, and a market can exist with no supplier.
- He says the alternatives are product-based or platform-based definitions, and a founder who starts from them cannot be innovative.
- He says the questions what market are you in, what business are you in, and what problem are you solving are the same question.
- He says the founder decides the market definition, nobody else, and the solution, the competitors seen and the measures of success all follow from it.
- He says blue ocean thinking rests on the need-based definition of a market.
- He says if there is no need, no business will run.
- He advises B2B founders not to let a customer steer the product unless tailoring is the value proposition, because customers know a startup is desperate and will tie it to custom work at minimal margins.

## Examples and cases used

- Apple outsourcing its finance function: his illustration that any function but marketing and innovation can be outsourced.
- Procter and Gamble and a green shampoo line: an opportunity identified first, with ideas generated afterwards.
- ISB opening a bakery: his illustration of high company newness in a market that knows the product.
- Insects as the cheapest protein: a product nobody enters because market newness is too high.
- His own analytics venture at Rice University: he and colleagues built a customer lifetime value model, set up a company, did not sell, and shut it, while less technically able analytics firms that started at the same time were later sold.
- Perplexity: he says it saw a window when ChatGPT did not search the internet, and grew before large players, who carry reputational risk, responded.
- Johnson and Johnson: a market leader that keeps innovations in house, will not put its name on something untested, and buys startups once the market develops.
- An electric car, a heart valve for children and an online portal for doctors: his three examples for the opportunity cost and development risk grid.
- Power Star bipolar scissors, which he launched in India for Ethicon: a technically superior product recommended for 30 surgeries per scissor that Indian surgeons reused far beyond that, and that the company withdrew a year later. He gives reuse as his own guess at the reason and says the company had carried a US strategy into India without analysing the context.
- A beggar at New Delhi railway station: his illustration that choosing where to stand, whom to approach and how to look is segmentation, targeting and design by experiment.
- Microsoft: an engineer who coded an idea in two days in place of waiting months for review, which he gives as the origin of the modern idea of growth hacking.
- Fast food chains: testing special items and moving those that pass a threshold onto the main menu.
- Children's toothbrushes: the first small, cute brushes failed, and the category took off when the handles were made thick so that small hands could grip them.
- A milkshake seller, which he names as Dairy Queen: discounts and flavour changes did not lift sales until research found two groups, morning commuters and parents with children, and the product was changed for each.
- A poll on a United States president: two equivalent groups got the same survey with one change in how he was named, and the ratings differed.
- Mineral water for travellers and for offices: the same product in different markets.
- Short distance transport: walking, cycling, cars, cab apps, buses and trains as different products in one market.
- Tourism to the moon and a cure for arthritis: markets that exist without a supplier.
- TurboTax: he says it ignored the roughly 40 tax software products already on sale and set out to beat the most common method, paper, pencil and calculator.
- Airbnb: he reads it as solving the traveller's problem of hotels being expensive and intrusive, not as a product idea.
- Starbucks: he says it defines its business as a place to work away from the office, not as coffee.
- Politics in India: the need is good governance, the exchange is the vote, so the market is people aged 18 and over.

## Numbers given

- He says that within 10 to 15 years the analytics companies that started alongside his venture were each sold for 50 to 100 million dollars.
- He says he and two partners spent about a year and a quarter of weekly discussions choosing which business to enter before co-founding a venture about ten years ago.
- He cites four research programmes on new product success: all four found fit with the company's own strengths mattered, and three found match to customer needs highly correlated with success.
- In the bipolar scissors case, the recommended use was 30 surgeries per scissor, and he recalls a surgeon reporting 120 surgeries on one.
- He says the roughly 40 tax software products on sale before TurboTax together held under 10% of the market.
- In the survey wording example he gives the gap between the two groups' ratings as 15 to 20%.
