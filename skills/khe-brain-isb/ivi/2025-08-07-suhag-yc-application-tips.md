# Y Combinator application tips, Akhil Suhag

Akhil Suhag, a founder who went through Y Combinator, spoke to the cohort online about how to write a YC application. He gave a short list of what the application must show and then answered founders' questions about their own applications. This note keeps the general advice and leaves out the individual cases.

## What was said

### Writing the application

- Akhil Suhag says many people he knows from YC got in on their second or third application.
- He says he knows founders who had already raised seed funding and still went to YC for what it brings overall.
- He says the first rule is to make the application as simple to understand as possible, written as if to a non-technical person.
- He says big words and big terms make the reader think the founder is hiding something behind them.
- He says only a deep tech startup needs technical language.
- He says readers can tell when an answer uses more words than it needs, so answers should be kept simple and straight.
- He says not to move away from the simple structure of problem, solution and why you.
- He says honesty is the rule of thumb for questions such as why you are applying, and that it has worked for him.
- He says blog posts and past YC applications are available online to study.

### Four things the application must show

- He says the application must show four things: a big market, why you are the right person, that you move fast and validate at every step, and that you know your path to 100 million.
- He says these four should be made apparent throughout the application, even in answers that do not ask for them directly.
- He says the market can be big today or have the potential to become big, because many startups begin in a wedge that widens over time.
- He says why you has two parts: whether there is founder market fit, and whether the founders understand their customers well enough to know something others do not.
- He says YC prefers a bottom-up market calculation: the customer, what they will pay, how many such customers exist in India and how many more globally.
- He says a research report figure with a claimed 10 percent share is the top-down approach YC does not prefer. Top-down numbers can be added, but the bottom-up calculation is necessary.

### Validation

- He says a core YC belief is "make something people want", which is different from something people need or should want.
- He says YC does not accept "I spoke to 10 people and they want this" as validation. Customer behaviour is what counts.
- His example of a validation trail is one week talking to users, then a landing page with two dollars a day of Facebook ads to count sign-ups, then a small MVP with usage and retention figures.
- He says YC wants founders to get customers as soon as possible in any way, and that its advice is to talk to users, not to random people.
- He says YC is far more open to pivots than many other investors, because it wants the market to tell founders what to build.

### Speed

- He says YC looks at progress week by week and expects at least 10 percent growth a week from companies in the batch.
- He says progress in the application should be stated in days, such as what was done in 2 days and in 7 days.
- He says writing that two months went on research amounts to nothing, especially now that AI tools speed things up.
- He says YC should not be the only plan. A founder should intend to build the company whether or not YC accepts it.
- He says outcomes involve luck and competition, and a founder can be rejected for one bad moment in the interview.

### Demo video

- He says the demo video is not mandatory but is good to have, because it is proof that the product described in the application works.
- He says to keep the video short. His own was 20 seconds long.
- He says YC is not looking for a finished product in the video. It should match the story told in the application.
- He says all YC partners are former founders, so they understand how products develop.

### Technical co-founder and co-founder history

- He says YC generally wants one co-founder to be technical.
- He says a team without a technical co-founder should be open about it, give the logic for choosing its co-founders, and not present it as a handicap.
- He says co-founder history matters to YC because founders fighting among themselves is one of the big reasons startups fail.
- He says YC is mostly looking for evidence that the co-founders have been through tough times together and stayed together.
- He says co-founders who have worked together only briefly should describe what brought them together and what structures cover the risk, such as what happens if one of them leaves.

### Founder market fit

- He says founder market fit can come from past experience, from having lived the problem, or from early and constant customer conversations that reveal something competitors do not know.
- He says the application asks what the founders know that others do not, and that this is the core of founder market fit.
- He says some depth comes only from having worked in an industry, as with software for one medical specialty or for steel manufacturing.
- He says founders cannot change their profile for the application. What they can do is move fast and show traction.

### Pivots and focus

- He says a team cannot target two customer groups at once in the application. Earlier work should be framed as part of the learning and validation journey.
- He says telling YC that an existing line of business is going well works against a claim that the team wants to build something else.
- He says many founders move from B2B to B2C because it seems more glamorous, even though founders spend years trying to find paying customers.
- He says moving away from the original thesis for short-term cash makes the reader doubt whether the founder will move away again.
- He says pivots must be shown as clean breaks, not as going back and forth while keeping the old thing running.
- He says changing the target customer between applications does not count against a founder. What matters more is how much progress was made since the last application.
- He says experiments are fine when they are shown as a logical sequence of testing, validating and taking the next step towards product-market fit.
- He says running several things in parallel for different markets is a different matter, and signals the team has not yet worked out who its customer is.
- He says traction is the number one thing YC looks at once a company has any. Team counts most for a second-time founder who scaled a first company well.
- He says he was accepted on an unvalidated idea because of how far he had scaled his previous startup.

### Other application questions

- He says matching a YC request for startups cuts both ways, because many applicants simply pick up those ideas.
- He says not to tailor the application to a request for startups. If the company fits, the readers will work it out.
- He says the question about other ideas gives YC a view of how the founders think, and leaves room for YC to back the founders if it likes them and not the idea.
- He says the growth rate question is a place to add context to a metric, such as strong retention behind a low user count.
- He says he gave India as his location because that is where his market is.
- He says the parent entity has to be registered outside India, and after acceptance YC connects founders with lawyers who help with that.

### What the programme is like

- He says YC is a three-month programme, and founders should enter when they are sure what they are building so they can scale it and raise on that traction at demo day.
- He says a team still working out its customer or segment during the three months will waste them and will not be able to raise.
- He says there are few lectures. Founders meet their group once a week, review their metrics and growth rate, set a target for the next week and defend it the week after.
- He says most learning comes from founders booking calls with their partner or others at YC, so a founder can get a lot or nothing from the programme.
- He says the number of Indian companies in recent batches has dropped sharply, and he links that to almost 90 percent of the batch being AI companies.

## Examples and cases used

- The YC T-shirt line about making something people want: his way of explaining what validation means to YC.
- A landing page with two dollars a day of Facebook ads: a cheap validation step that counts as customer behaviour.
- Duolingo: raised in a founder's question as an example of a language app built for engagement.
- Software for one medical specialty and for steel manufacturing: problems that can only be found by working in the industry.

## Numbers given

- At least 10 percent growth a week, which he says YC expects from companies in the batch.
- 20 seconds, the length of his own demo video.
- 100 million, the path he says the application must show.
- Two dollars a day on Facebook ads, in his example of early validation.
- Almost 90 percent of the batch being AI companies, as he estimates it.
- Three months, the length of the programme.

## Not covered

- Deal terms, the amount YC invests or the equity it takes.
- How to raise at demo day.
- His own company's story in any detail.
