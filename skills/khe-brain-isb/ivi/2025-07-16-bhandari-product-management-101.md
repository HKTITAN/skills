# Product management 101, Ashank Bhandari

Ashank Bhandari, a product leader at foundit (formerly Monster.com) who earlier worked on search at Flipkart and on a content commerce product at Amazon, gave the cohort an afternoon introduction to product management for founders. He opened with his own career, then covered what a product manager does, the product life cycle, how to read customer requests, and prioritisation frameworks worked through an Amazon wish list example. He played two video clips and set a group exercise on breaking tasks into stories and epics, which ran out of time.

## What was said

### What product management is

- He says the job of a founder or product manager is to get users to the kind of personal attachment people show when asked to name their favourite product.
- He asked the room for favourite products and pointed out that every answer came from personal use, which he takes as the sign of a real connection between user and product.
- He says listening is a core part of being both a product manager and a founder.
- He describes a founder as the director of a play, who thinks constantly from the audience's point of view.
- He draws a second parallel from theatre: an actor who gets into the character's shoes does a better job, and so does a founder who gets into the customer's shoes.
- He says the one takeaway he wants from the session is that building a product has a rhythm and a cyclical nature, and is not a point and shoot activity.
- He contrasts product companies with service companies. A product company has no fixed requirement and stays in line with customer needs directly, while a service company builds to a client's specific requirement.
- He defines product managers as problem solvers who scale the solution using technology.
- He calls product managers "specialist generalists" who fill the gaps between users, developers, designers, business teams and support functions.
- The one critical role he gives the product manager among those specialists is being the voice of the customer.
- He compares the product manager to the all-rounder in a cricket team, whom a captain values because the all-rounder fills gaps.
- He says a founder plays the all-rounder at the start, across legal, tech, design and delivery, and that product management as a function is born when the company grows and the founder can no longer do all of it.
- He rejects the saying that product managers are mini CEOs. His reason is that a CEO has both accountability and autonomy, while a product manager has high accountability and very little autonomy.
- He says the difference between good and great founders is how rooted they are in the voice of the customer, as a consistent habit and not a one-time activity.
- He says product management became popular in software because software gives access to data at scale, and that the discipline is now spreading to other industries.
- He distinguishes brand marketing, which runs as a separate team with its own objectives, from product marketing, which works hand in hand with the people building the product.

### Why before what and how

- He uses a three-part model: an insight, then creative solutioning, then execution.
- In that model the insight answers why you are doing something, the creative solution answers what, and execution answers how.
- He says the why is where a founder needs the most clarity and is the part most often missing.
- Of desirability, viability and feasibility, he says desirability and viability sit closer to the why, while feasibility addresses the what and the how.
- He says staying connected to customers is the discipline that keeps the why clear.
- He applies the same idea to a job application. The resume is the product, and it has to answer why the recruiter should call you.
- He names time spent and scroll depth as two core metrics of engagement, and says they are different measures.

### The product life cycle

- He gives six phases for a product: conceive, plan, build, launch, refine and an optional retire phase.
- In the conceive phase ideas are born and the founder does market research and user research and asks how large the market is.
- In the conceive phase the founder's research is primary research, interviews and focus group discussions, plus secondary research, industry benchmarking and competitive benchmarking. The output is a vision that puts structure on abstract ideas.
- In the plan phase you pick a specific set of problems, because you cannot do everything, then define features and create the roadmap.
- In the build phase come wireframes, design, technical specifications and functional and non-functional requirements.
- The launch phase covers launch planning, the four Ps of marketing, feedback loops and the metrics to monitor.
- In the refine phase you check whether the product is hitting its intended use, through data and through conversations with customers.
- He says parts of a retired product can be salvaged and offered elsewhere, and that retiring something also needs planning.

### Reading what customers ask for

- When customers are asked what they want they list features. He says the founder's task is to understand the journey they are on and why they are asking.
- He says the solution a customer proposes is limited by what the customer knows, while the founder can offer a wider range of solutions.
- He played the part of an Instagram user demanding more reactions on posts, and the room struggled to get to his reason.
- The reason he then gave was that he also gets news on Instagram, including deaths and calamities, where a heart is not an appropriate reaction.
- His lesson from the role play is that you reach the why by understanding the user's journey, not by thinking at the level of the feature.
- The next step he gives is to find more customers with the same use case and then design a solution that keeps the ease of the original action.
- He says to get feedback from people who represent your target customers and who are not family or friends, because family and friends give biased feedback.
- He says changing user behaviour is very hard, takes time and usually happens in increments.
- A product at scale cannot talk to every user, so the team looks for patterns in the data.
- He says founders need to build the skill of asking the right kind of questions of their data, questions that lead to insight and then action.
- He says customer conversations and data have to be correlated. A conversation gives an insight, and the data shows whether the problem is large enough to solve.
- He says, "If you are building for everyone, you are building for no one." Start with who you are specifically building for and expand from there.
- He says the product must be ready before demand is driven to it, or advertising spend loses those early customers.
- He says Figma prototypes are enough for user testing unless the product is very niche, and that he has done 200 to 300 hours of user interviews on Figma prototypes alone.

### Layers of planning

- He orders the layers as business strategy, then product vision or mission, then product strategy, then roadmap, release plan and sprint plan, down to daily tasks.
- He says product mission and product strategy are often blended into one and should be kept distinct.
- The product mission is a subset of the company mission and is more point in time, while the company mission is set for a longer period.
- He says the product mission addresses the why and the product strategy addresses the what.
- If a founder cannot see these layers distinctly, he says the cause is taking on too many things, lacking clarity, or trying to solve several problems together.
- He loosely maps the layers to an annual plan, a quarterly plan and a two week sprint.
- Too much planning with no development means the product does not win. Too much development without planning means building things people do not care about.
- He says there is no formula for splitting a founder's time between planning and building, and that it comes with experience.

### Breaking work down: themes, epics, stories, tasks

- He says the ability to break work down is the fundamental first step of prioritisation.
- His structure is a theme, which contains epics, which break into stories, which break into tasks, forming a tree.
- In his example the theme is an Amazon wish list, and two epics are adding to the wish list and coming back to view it.
- He writes stories in the customer's voice, in the form: as a customer I want to save a product to my wish list so that I can view it later.
- Tasks are the most executable level. His examples are the heart icon and the database entry behind it.
- He shows how tasks multiply: whether the icon can be untapped, what colour it turns, whether there is an animation, whether there is a sound.
- He says planning has to work backwards from the engineering date, so design and business decisions land in the weeks before, with time left for feedback.
- He says work should be broken down so that build, test and feedback fit inside one cycle.
- He says the person planning should absorb the overwhelming detail so specialists receive only the clear task they need to do.
- He says a team of four does not need Jira and that a spreadsheet and talking to each other is enough.
- He says informal coordination works only at early stages and becomes a drag on pace as the company scales, so the change should be handled sooner.

### MVP and MLP

- His MVP example is early Flipkart, where orders were placed online and the founders went to physical bookstores to pick up the book and deliver it.
- He contrasts the MVP with a minimum lovable product, where the builder refuses to go live until the experience is delightful, even with limited functionality.
- He says a founder chooses the lovable route when confident enough in the underlying insight.
- He says a product can stay an MVP and still reach scale, as long as it delivers the value it set out to deliver.

### Prioritisation frameworks

- He says prioritisation exists only because time and resources are limited.
- MoSCoW sorts features into four buckets: must have, should have, could have and will not have.
- A must have is a feature you cannot go live without. For the wish list, his must have is the heart button.
- In his wish list example, sharing the list is a should have, animation is a could have, and promoting the wish list outside Amazon is a will not have for this phase.
- MoSCoW can be applied at the epic level as well as at the feature level.
- He calls MoSCoW an intuition based approach, and says how well you sort depends on how well you know your customers, which is why user personas exist.
- He says a chatbot can sort a feature list but does not understand your customer.
- RICE is reach multiplied by impact multiplied by confidence, divided by effort.
- Reach is how many users the feature touches. A change on the home page reaches more users than a wish list feature that only some users find.
- Impact is measured against the north star metric.
- Confidence is how sure you are the impact will happen. It is lower when the feature affects sales only through two or three steps of behaviour.
- Effort comes from asking designers and engineers. In his example the designer finds a heart icon easy, while the engineer sees an architectural change taking at least three weeks.
- He says the wish list came from an insight that customers were using the cart as a place to drop products and forget them, which got in the way of purchases.
- The value versus effort matrix is a two by two. Low value and low effort items are fillers, done only when time permits.
- Low value and high effort items are time sinks, and they go to the will not have list.
- High value and low effort items are quick wins. High value and high effort items are big projects.
- For Amazon, he places the wish list as a quick win and Amazon Pay as a big project.
- Weighted scoring adds a numeric weight to each item, which he describes as simpler than RICE and a little more complex than MoSCoW.
- He says value versus effort is the method he has used most, including at Amazon, and that it has been enough for executive level decisions.
- He says an early stage startup does not need RICE, and that MoSCoW and value versus effort are sufficient.
- In his own guide for choosing a framework, a high cost of error calls for the precision of RICE and other mathematical methods.
- For quick decisions he recommends value versus effort or MoSCoW.
- When many stakeholders from different disciplines are involved, usually at the growth stage, he recommends weighted scoring with MoSCoW, because not everyone follows a purely mathematical method.
- For products with fast build cycles and high iteration he recommends RICE or weighted scoring, because the data exists.
- He says a framework is a guideline and not a formula. Using one does not guarantee success, it reduces the chance of failure.
- A framework has to be adapted to the founder's environment and customer understanding.
- He says there is no excuse for a founder not to know the customer, the addressable market and the behavioural insights.

### The founder's own time

- He recommends Shreyas Doshi and played a clip of Doshi's LNO framework.
- In the clip, tasks are of three types: leverage, neutral and overhead.
- In the clip, leverage tasks return 10 or 100 times the effort, neutral tasks return about what is put in, and overhead tasks return less than is put in.
- In the clip, the mistake of ambitious people is treating all three types the same way.
- The advice in the clip is to do leverage tasks when most energetic and to do them thoroughly, with the time taken from neutral and overhead tasks.
- On a team that does not meet expectations, he suggests either hiring people who fit the requirement or spending the time on expectation setting with worked examples of how he would have done the task.
- He says the hardest part of being a founder or product manager is stakeholder management, which means speaking to business people and technical people each in their own language.
- He says a founder will know it is time to hire a product manager when the founder is burning out and that effort is producing returns such as revenue.
- At an early stage he says the founder is better off doing product management personally and not hiring for it.

### Using data outside software

- He played part of an electric scooter launch video to show product thinking in a non-software industry.
- The insight in the video was that most buyers paid for the higher performance variant but few used the high performance riding modes, because of range on longer trips.
- The response in the video was a larger battery and changes to weight and tyre grip.
- He says the company could see this because its vehicles are connected and send usage data, which a conventional scooter maker does not have at scale.
- He says every industry has scope to use data this way, including physical retail through AI-enabled cameras, and asks founders how their own product could get access to usage data.
- He says a launch does not have to reach all customers at once and can go live incrementally.

## Examples and cases used

- Flipkart search: many users spell words the way they say them, such as a phonetic spelling of Bluetooth, while the catalogue has clean spellings. The team built data science models using Indian phonetics instead of expecting users to spell correctly.
- Flipkart fashion: a women's trouser style whose name most shoppers cannot spell, used as a second case of search volume without purchases.
- Early Flipkart: the first product sold was a book that a founder collected from a bookstore and delivered, used as the example of an MVP.
- Amazon wish list: the running example for epics, stories, tasks, MoSCoW, RICE and value versus effort.
- Amazon Pay: his example of a high value, high effort big project.
- Instagram reactions: the role play on finding the why behind a feature request.
- Kellogg in India: the company expected Indians to switch to its cereals within ten years, that did not happen overnight, and it had to introduce instant upma. He uses it to show that behaviour change is slow.
- IKEA, Decathlon and CRED: he says they share one governing principle, which he names as the Gruen effect, and gives IKEA placing bedsheets in the kitchen section as the illustration.
- BlackBerry: when the phone business died, its messenger was salvaged and offered as a security product, his example for the retire phase.
- Google and Infosys: his illustration of the gap between product and service companies.
- Zomato's founder doing deliveries and the Uber CEO driving and taking rides: founders staying in touch with the end customer.
- Sundar Pichai and Satya Nadella: named among CEOs who came from a product management background.
- Ravindra Jadeja: the all-rounder who fills gaps for a captain, as the model for a product manager.
- A superbike e-commerce site he worked on: a filter to add your bike so only compatible products are shown, an insight that came from accompanying bikers to stores.
- Ather 450X launch video: usage data from connected scooters driving the next version of the product.
- His home cricket team's 18 year wait for a title: right inputs do not guarantee the outcome on the timeline you predict.

## Numbers given

- 35 times: the gap in revenue per employee he cites between Google and Infosys, with Infosys having roughly a lakh more employees. He says the figure is about a year old.
- 200 to 300 hours: the user interviewing he says he has done on Figma prototypes alone.
- 85 per cent and 18 per cent: figures stated in the Ather launch video for buyers choosing the higher variant and riders using the high performance modes as their main mode.
- 2.9 kWh to 3.7 kWh: the battery capacity increase stated in the same video.
- 10 or 100 times, and about 1.1 times: the returns Shreyas Doshi gives for leverage tasks and neutral tasks in the clip played.
- At least three weeks: the engineering estimate in his wish list example, used to show how effort enters RICE.
