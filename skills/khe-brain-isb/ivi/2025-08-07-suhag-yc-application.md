# Applying to Y Combinator, Akhil Suhag

Akhil Suhag, a founder who went through Y Combinator, held an online session for the cohort on how to write a YC application. The recording starts partway through his opening remarks. He set out what the application has to show, then answered founders' questions about the demo video, co-founders, pivots, location and the programme itself. Advice he gave on individual founders' own applications is left out of this note.

## What was said

### What the application has to make clear

- Akhil Suhag says the application should make four things plain throughout: a big market, something people want, the right founders, and fast movement with validation at every step along a path to large revenue.
- He says "why are you the right founders" covers two things: whether you come from the industry, and whether you understand the customers well enough to know something others do not.
- He says the core YC belief is "make something people want", which is different from something people need or should want.
- He says the application should explain how you worked out that people want it, and that "I spoke to 10 people and they want this" is not an answer.
- He says the things YC looks for should show up wherever possible in the application, in examples as well as in direct answers.

### Validation

- He says YC does not treat talking to people as validation. It wants you to get customers as soon as possible in any form, and it treats customer behaviour as the validation.
- He says that when YC says talk to people it means talk to users, not random people.
- His example of a good two-month story is: a week of user conversations, a landing page with a few dollars a day of Facebook ads to count sign-ups, then a small MVP with usage and retention figures.
- He says you should validate at each step before taking the next one.
- He says YC is very pro-pivot, unlike many other investors, because its view is to let the market tell you what to build.
- He warns against treating an idea as a matter of ego and insisting it will work because you will make it work.

### Speed

- He says YC looks at progress on a weekly basis, and that founders in the batch who are not growing at least 10% a week are called out in front of the whole batch.
- He says the application should describe progress in days and weeks, such as what was done in 2 days and in 7 days.
- He says two months spent researching reads as time when nothing was being done.
- He says you must intend to build the company whether or not YC accepts you. Treating YC as make or break also hurts you in the interview.
- He says luck and competition play a part in selection, so an application alone should not carry your plans.

### Market size

- He says you generally need to show a path to about 200 million dollars in revenue, because that is what a billion dollar company means to YC.
- He says YC prefers a bottom-up case, built from the customer you are building for and what they are ready to pay, over a top-down share of a market figure from a research report.

### The demo video

- He says the demo video is not mandatory but is good to have, because it adds proof to what you wrote about the product.
- He says to keep it short and show what exists today. His own video was 20 seconds long.
- He says YC is not looking for a finished product in the video, and that it should match the story in the written application.

### Founders and co-founders

- He says every YC partner is a former founder, so they understand product and early-stage realities.
- He says YC generally wants one co-founder to be technical, though there are exceptions.
- He says a team without a technical co-founder should be open about it and explain the logic of the choice, without presenting it as a handicap.
- He says founder-market fit can come from past experience, from having lived the problem, or from early traction and constant customer contact that taught you something competitors do not know.
- He points out that "what do you know that your competition doesn't" is itself a question in the application.
- He says YC asks about co-founder history because co-founder breakups are a major reason startups fail, and that it mainly wants to see whether you have been through tough times together and stayed together.
- For co-founders who have not worked together long, he says to show the structures in place: a properly settled equity discussion and a plan for what happens if someone leaves.
- He says you cannot do much about your founder profile, since your experience and education are fixed. What helps is how fast you have moved and what traction you can show.

### Pivots, focus and multiple business models

- He says a change of direction has to be told as part of a learning and validation journey: we did this, we learned this, then we built that.
- He says you cannot present yourself as doing two things at once and targeting both.
- He says telling YC that the old line is going well works against you, because they will ask why you are not doubling down on it.
- He says moving away from your thesis makes YC wonder whether you will move away again when you see the next shiny thing, so the break has to be clean.
- He notes that many founders move from B2B to B2C because it seems more glamorous, while other founders spend years failing to find any paying customer.
- He says narrowing the same product to a different customer segment is not a concern for YC. For a repeat applicant, what matters more is how much progress was made since the last application.
- He says running several experiments is fine when it is a logical step-by-step search for the right segment. Running many things at once across different markets is a different matter.
- Asked what YC weighs most among team, product and traction, he says it is traction. Team counts when you are a second-time founder who scaled before.

### Other application questions

- On aligning with YC's Requests for Startups, he says it cuts both ways, because you then compete with many people who simply took those ideas.
- He says to write plainly what you are actually building. If it fits YC's thesis, they are smart enough to see it, and you should not try to outsmart them.
- He says the question about other ideas you considered gives YC a look at how you think. It also helps when they like the founders but not the idea.
- He says the growth-rate question is a place to add context to your numbers, such as strong retention behind a small user count.
- He says YC can tell when your word count goes beyond what the point needs, and that honest, simple, direct answers have worked for him.
- On location, he says he simply wrote India because that is where his market is.
- He says YC funds Indian founders but the parent entity has to be registered outside India, and that after acceptance YC connects you with lawyers who help do it.

### What the programme is like

- He says YC is a three-month programme and you should enter it sure of what you are building, so you can scale in those months and raise capital on that traction at the end.
- He says a founder who enters still working out the customer and the segment will waste the three months and will not be able to raise.
- He says there are no regular lectures. You meet your group once a week, set a target for the next week, and report whether you met it.
- He says most of the learning comes from setting up calls with your partner or others at YC yourself. You can get a lot out of the programme or nothing.
- He says that examples of successful applications are easy to find online.

## Examples and cases used

- His own YC application: he says he got in on an idea, with nothing built, on the strength of his previous startup's results, and calls that the exception.
- A vertical software product for a narrow specialty such as cardiologists: his example of depth that you only get by working in the field.

## Numbers given

- 10% a week: the growth Akhil Suhag says YC expects from companies during the batch.
- About 200 million dollars in revenue: what he says YC needs to believe you can reach.
- 20 seconds: the length of his own demo video.
- 25% one-week retention that settles at 20% after two months: figures in his made-up example of a validation story, not real data.
- About 90% of the batch being AI companies: his explanation, offered as a guess, for the drop in Indian companies in recent batches.
