# Marketing strategy, market definition, targeting and customer value

A marketing professor, addressed in the room as Siddharth, taught a long class in a series on marketing strategy. He recapped the previous class, then worked through market definition, growth hacking, market sizing, segmentation, targeting and customer value, mostly through stories from his own work. The class took many questions, and a long stretch was a debate with founders about what counts as growth hacking.

## What was said

### Start with the customer, not the product

- The instructor says the sequence is fixed: understand the consumer's needs, address the differences between consumers, segment, target, and only then look at the competition, meaning what other solutions people use and how those are positioned.
- He says that if founders keep to this stop and think sequence, more than 90% of their problems go away.
- He says people default to being product centric because society trains everyone to conform to the norm, and conforming means studying what others around you are doing.
- He says a startup that copies what 40 existing products do ends up making the 41st.
- He says strategic marketing is the question of what problem to solve for whom, and the 4Ps are the result of that strategy.
- He says a startup can pick its position by its own choice, or it can test several value propositions and go to market with the one most people prefer. He calls these judgment calls.

### Latent needs

- He says latent needs are found by asking why again and again and by a deep understanding of the customer's context.
- He says one sign of a latent need is people using several different products for the same use.

### How you define your market

- He shows the same sales figures twice and says they are good news or bad news depending on whether the whole market is growing or shrinking.
- He says nobody tells you which market you are in. The founder sits in the office and decides it, and it is the same question as what problem you solve for whom.
- He says how you define the market changes how you judge success and who your rivals are.
- He says product markets are dynamic, so a company cannot rest on what it has done.
- He says a founder who is not already working with AI cannot be saved.
- He says "it is never about technology". It is about the need and the problem, and technology is one element of the solution.

### Growth hacking

- He defines growth hacking as conscious, rapid experimentation with elements of the marketing mix after launch, in order to grow.
- He leaves communication out of his definition, because companies always experiment with communication.
- He says launching version 2.0 of a product, or adding a new variant when sales fall, is a new product launch and not growth hacking.
- He says a real experiment needs a control group that is equivalent to the experimental group. Without that it is trial and error.
- He gives three conditions for saying X causes Y: X is correlated with Y, X comes before Y, and other causes of Y are ruled out. Even then the conclusion is accepted only temporarily.
- He says there is no minimum viable product in television entertainment, so every context needs its own thinking.
- He says platforms with very strong network effects have little incentive to experiment, and names OLX and Craigslist. He says Amazon experiments a lot because it also competes with individual vendors.
- Asked how often to experiment, he says there is no rule: know your context and do not upset the customer.
- He says the concept is new in marketing research and that he is writing a paper on it.

### Data and market size

- He says secondary data was collected by someone else for another purpose, and primary data is what you collect for your own purpose.
- He says secondary data leads to typical errors when the product is new, so innovators need primary research on how many people need the product and who can use it.
- He says market potential is what is possible in a given time under stated assumptions about what you, competitors, the context and customers will do. A forecast is what is expected.
- He says to use several estimation methods and not trust one.
- He says to estimate market size in units first and not in rupees or dollars, because price is an element of the marketing mix that is decided later.
- He says marketing research is never perfect. It only has to be good enough to inform the decision.

### Segmentation

- He says to define the market by need and to segment on several factors including need.
- He says two segments should be combined only when the strategy to serve them is the same, not just because their stated needs are the same.
- His example is two age groups that both want status and confidence but mean different things by them, so the product, spokesperson and price would differ.
- He says a company wants as few segments as possible for economy, while customers differ and want many. Segmentation is the middle ground: few segments, but more than one.

### Targeting

- He says targeting is arguably the most important decision in a startup, and that many pivots happen because the first targeting decision was poor.
- He says "we like to give fancy terms to our bad decisions".
- Step one of his targeting method: decide your own criteria for what makes a segment attractive. He lists size, growth, less competition, ease or difficulty of entry and patent protection as possible criteria.
- Step two: rank the segments that are attractive to you.
- Step three: starting with the most attractive, work out what any company would need to win there, then check how your own strengths match. Enter the most attractive segment where the match is best.
- He says the factors needed to win belong to the segment, not to the company.
- He shows a weighted scoring of segment attractiveness against business strength, and says a weak score can be improved by narrowing the segment definition, for example plastics accessories for automobiles.
- He says a startup that is not ready for a segment should prepare before entering, and should take a long time over the choice.

### Customer value

- He defines value as the customer's perception of benefits received minus costs given up, relative to competing offers.
- He says the value has to be perceived as superior in a specific way that produces the position you want.
- He says buying has changed from a single transaction to a long journey with many touch points, and online it is more complex still.
- He says value creation rests on understanding the customer's needs, behaviour and emotional state at each touch point of the journey.
- He says a company should build a hook so that returning customers see more value, and gives loyalty programmes and a familiar user interface as examples.
- He says the world is hyper competitive: one click fewer than a competitor matters, because switching is easy.
- He says it does not matter what a company's internal systems are. If the end consumer does not see the value, the company is dead, so the company must reorganise inside to deliver it.
- He says perception drives value in business to business markets too, not only in consumer markets.
- He says every decision is based on perceived value.
- He says to look carefully at the money when choosing a target segment, but after entering, to focus on getting the solution right. Money is the result.
- He says the customer is rarely one person. Every stakeholder who buys, sells or uses the product has to see value, explained in their own language.
- He says when a sale is lost, diagnose step by step from the market definition down to each element, instead of changing everything at once.

## Examples and cases used

- Nintendo: research showed people disliked how they interacted with games, so Nintendo made family friendly games, kept prices lower, left out high definition and added a motion stick.
- Sony PlayStation and Microsoft Xbox: he says both were really aiming at a home entertainment hub that people would not have asked for, so they entered through young gamers, who tolerate early technology. Buyers then started using the console for music and other things.
- Sony and Microsoft console pricing: he says they sold at a loss because every customer won is locked in for the next 10 to 15 years.
- Console competitors: he says Nintendo's rivals were the other two console makers, while Sony and Microsoft also competed with television and computer companies. In his own home a PC replaced the Xbox.
- A VoIP device maker in New York, 2011: it defined itself by its product and its sales fell.
- MagicJack: he says it defined its market as the need for inexpensive long distance communication, so when smartphones spread it made an app.
- Long tail: a physical store with limited shelf space stocks only the most popular items, and internet distribution opened up niche needs.
- Sony television in India: concept tests cannot predict a series, so the channel experiments with characters and plots after launch. He calls this growth hacking.
- McDonald's: he says it hires top chefs to create items within limits such as a two minute preparation time, runs them as specials in test stores against control stores, and adds an item to the menu when sales pass a threshold.
- Diapers: with capacity for 20,000 diapers a year and a rough market potential of 230 million a year in India, a back of the envelope estimate is good enough.
- Coca-Cola in the United States and India: the need is the same but the strategy differs, so they stay separate segments.
- A former student with a trained sales force of 800 to 1,000 people: he picks new businesses where that sales force is what it takes to win.
- Lexus: its website helped first time luxury car buyers compare models, which cut their cost of searching for information.
- Yebhi.com: he could not order shoes in a way that let him try sizes, customer service told him to place two orders, and he bought elsewhere. He says the company went out of business.
- Eggs: regular eggs at 80 rupees a dozen sit beside eggs at 325 rupees, which he calls a mind game.
- A tile fixing chemical brand: its owner told him the product was the same as others, yet it sold at a 20 to 25% premium because it is a small part of a large tiling bill, failures are never traced to it, and the brand advertised early in a fragmented market.
- Johnson and Johnson at SGPGI Lucknow: a purchasing manager ran a reverse auction for sutures, J and J walked out and lost the account, and within months the hospital asked it back because surgeons insisted on its products.
- Johnson and Johnson market definition: local rivals defined the market as the need for sutures and competed on price. J and J defined it as improving surgical outcomes, so it invested in product quality and training doctors. He says its failure was in communicating that value to the buyer.
- Children's toothbrush: parents and child both decide, so parents are told about health and price on a news channel and children are shown superheroes on a cartoon channel.

## Numbers given

- More than 90% of a founder's marketing problems go away by following the consumer first sequence, in his estimate.
- 10 to 15 years: how long he says a console customer stays locked in.
- 230 million diapers a year: his illustrative market potential for India, against a plant capacity of 20,000.
- 800 to 1,000: the trained sales force of his former student's company.
- 80 rupees against 325 rupees a dozen: the two egg prices he quotes from a hypermarket.
- 20 to 25%: the price premium of the tile chemical brand over others.
- 60 to 70%: the market share he gives for Johnson and Johnson in sutures, meshes and endoscopy equipment at the time.
- One: the number of professional accounts Johnson and Johnson had in Uttar Pradesh, a state he puts at 160 million people then.
