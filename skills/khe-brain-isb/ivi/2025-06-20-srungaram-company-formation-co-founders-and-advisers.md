# Company formation, co-founders and advisers, Sonali Srungaram

Sonali Srungaram, founder of a cancer care company, continued her guest lecture after a break. This second half covered the kinds of company a founder can register in India, how a private limited company is set up and governed, how to choose and vest a co-founder, how to deal with advisers, when to bring agency work in-house, and how to answer investors who demand fixed metrics. Much of it was answers to questions from the room.

## What was said

### Kinds of company

- Sonali Srungaram says the Companies Act of 2013, with its 2015 amendment, sets out the kinds of company possible in India, and tells founders to read it.
- She says a one person company gives limited liability and total control, but is less attractive for outside funding.
- She explains limited liability as a separation between the person and the entity, so personal assets such as a flat or car cannot be seized for the company's troubles.
- She says the paperwork and compliance are the same whichever form is chosen, so registering an entity as a placeholder costs time and money.
- She says a limited liability partnership suits service work such as consultants, accountants and lawyers, where the partners deliver the service themselves and investment needs are low.
- She says in a partnership each partner takes what they bring in and shares the upside on joint work, and that the partnership can distribute what is left at the end of each year and start afresh.
- She says a private limited company is the form that makes sense for most founders who want to raise money.
- She says private limited companies attract outside funds because they face more statutory filings and external audits, which makes investors trust them more.
- She says the difference from a public limited company is that a private company has many restrictions on buying and selling shares.

### Investors who ask for a new entity

- She says investors are fine using a recent entity as the vehicle, but may ask an entity older than three or four years to start a clean new one.
- She says moving to a new entity means moving shareholders, R&D, assets and people, and that it takes a full quarter and not the one month investors promise.
- Her advice is to be open to a new entity but to be able to justify to yourself why it is needed.

### Setting up a private limited company

- She says a private limited company must have at least two directors and two shareholders, with a maximum of 15 directors and 200 shareholders.
- She says the same person can be both a director and a shareholder, which is the usual case for a founder.
- She says the paperwork to start the company takes two to three weeks, a month at most.
- She says the 2015 amendment removed the one lakh rupee minimum paid-up capital, but one lakh is still needed in the bank as authorised capital to issue shares.
- She says a founder pitching without a company should say it is in the process of being created, because saying nothing has been done looks unprepared.
- She says every share in a new Indian private limited company has a face value of 10 rupees, and anything above that in the share price is the premium.
- She says a founder who takes money at a premium needs a valuation certificate from a certified valuer to justify the share price, or the tax department can treat the money as income.
- She says a five year discounted cash flow projection is one way a valuer justifies the share price.

### MOA, AOA and share transfers

- She describes the Memorandum of Association as the charter for the work the company will do and the Articles of Association as how it functions internally.
- She says these documents restrict share transfers, so shares cannot be sold without the board's approval.
- She says a founder can hold a right of first refusal, so an angel who wants out must offer the shares to the founder first.
- She says a nominee can inherit a founder's shares, but the board can still block that person from joining the company.
- She says these documents run to 30 or 40 pages each and founders should read them and not just sign where the company secretary points.
- She says a company presented as a family business has less chance of outside funding and of hiring good talent, because a senior hire will not join where a family member will get the top job.

### Community funding against venture funding

- She says founders from business communities often keep high ownership because they raise debt from family and community and do not sell equity.
- She says that route needs the ability to service the debt, or a trade of some other asset such as land.
- She says equity from investors creates a legal obligation while money from family creates an emotional one, and she calls the investor route the cleaner one.
- She says funding has never been as readily available as in the last five to ten years, and that it comes at a cost.
- She says business communities do not expect a first venture to succeed, and that a founder can keep more ownership in a second or third company.

### General meetings

- She says a private limited company must hold an annual general meeting once a year with all shareholders, and the founder chairs it and opens with an address on what the company did that year.
- She says shareholders must get 21 working days notice of the AGM, and that many are now held online.
- She says small companies should treat the AGM as seriously as large ones do, and ask a shareholder who cannot attend to send a proxy.
- She says an emergency general meeting is for sudden decisions, such as a chance to buy a failing competitor, and can be called on shorter notice, even one day.

### Choosing a co-founder

- She says to induct directors and co-founders deliberately, because directors vote on the board. Her phrase is not to hand seats out "like Diwali gifts".
- She says co-founders should complement each other, such as a healthcare founder with a technology co-founder, and that two or more founders doing the same job get in each other's way.
- She says speed is what matters most for a startup, and founders who spend months clashing lose it.
- She says to ask a prospective co-founder directly how long they intend to stay, and says she regrets not asking her own.
- She says to question why a person needs to be a co-founder and not an employee, contractor or paid adviser.
- She describes a co-founder as the person who shares the pressure of investor calls and acts as a sounding board on hard deals.
- She recommends a trial period of three to six months of working together before making someone a co-founder.
- She says not to take on a co-founder just to satisfy an investor who wants to see one.
- She says the timelines others set for founders are not fixed, and a founder who is uncomfortable with a decision can wait.
- She says equity is a very expensive currency and to be careful before spending it.
- She says very few people have more than two real talents, so a founder should hold a co-founder to the one thing they were brought in for.
- She says the co-founder is also interviewing the founder, and a founder who changes their mind every day affects the people around them.
- She says a technical co-founder must still attend investor meetings and be able to state the business result of their work, such as a customer acquisition cost below the industry average.

### Vesting and clawback

- She says a co-founder's stake can vest over time when they bring sweat equity. Her example for a 20% stake is 10% in year one, 5% in year two and 5% in year three.
- She says a co-founder who pays money upfront will expect their shares upfront.
- She says investors often re-vest the founder's own shares over three or four years, with a clawback if the founder leaves before the promised period.
- She says shares already vested belong to the co-founder even if the two stop working together, and that the vesting contract should make unvested shares conditional on the person continuing to work.
- She says taking shares back from someone who has served their time is hard, and the company must buy them at the share price or risk a court claim.
- She says within the first year the share price has rarely moved, so returning the person's money in exchange for the shares is workable.
- She says a company that cannot pay market salary makes it up in shares. Her example is a person worth 4 lakh a month taking 2 lakh in salary plus shares worth the 24 lakh a year they gave up.
- She says a co-founder who leaves for genuine family reasons after good work can be allowed to keep their shares, and that this is the founder's choice.
- She says partners who leave a limited liability partnership have their share taken back by the remaining partners, while a private limited company can let leavers keep shares.
- She tells the cohort to compare deals with each other, asking what terms each got and how they negotiated.

### Advisers

- She says in India people do not pay for advice unless there is a tangible deliverable, and most senior people will give one or two conversations free.
- She suggests offering an adviser a small quarterly retainer before any equity.
- Her company never paid its advisers. In its early days the founders visited them every quarter to report progress and thank them.
- She says a well-known adviser has already made their money, so the conversation is about their time more than about payment.
- She says if an adviser asks for 1% or 2% of the shares, the founder should work out what that percentage is worth and expect that much advice in return.
- She says not to give an adviser more than 1.5% to 2%, because five advisers at 2% each cost 10% of the company.
- She says vesting 1% or 2% is hard to justify because the paperwork is the same as for vesting 20%.
- She recommends meeting a possible adviser in person first, and walking away from one who arrives late, watches the clock and leaves in ten minutes.
- She says professional advisers quote an hourly rate upfront.
- She says a board of advisers is worth its cost if it makes the company more attractive to investors.

### Agency or in-house

- She says founders usually start with an agency because it is cheaper, and should bring a skill in-house once it is core to the company.
- She says an agency with 15 to 20 clients gives each about half a day a week and rarely its best people.
- Her suggestion is to hire the agency for about a year, learn what they do, then build the skill inside slowly.
- She says a feature that drives what the company sells, such as an insurance comparison calculator on a site like Policybazaar, should not be built by an outsider.
- She says software vendors take customisations one client paid for and sell them to the next client, and that this is why her company began building its own hospital revenue software.
- She says an investor who questions outsourced core technology should be told the founder knows the risk and will bring it in-house over about a year.

### Metrics and pushing back

- She says investors have no fixed metric they can hold an early founder to, and that she would always find a way out of being pinned to one.
- She says when asked about capital efficiency, a founder should move to showing how revenue will build up and let the investor do the calculation.
- Her test for the next raise is to go backwards: a 4 crore valuation at a 10x revenue multiple is justified by 40 lakh of annual revenue.
- She says healthcare companies work to an EBITDA margin of 14% of revenue, and that investors asked her for 20% because her company was new.
- She says revenue figures can be made up to meet metrics with no cash realised, and that a good investor would rather wait for a founder who is building honestly.
- She says investors know founders run late, so they ask for results two years early.
- She puts an institution's success rate at 15% to 20% of its investments, and says the two companies in ten that work return about five times its total investment.
- Her advice is to be as non-committal as possible on dated targets, because missing a number given in enthusiasm makes the founder look unreliable.
- She says to listen to an investor's view on metrics when they have invested in the sector before, and not when an analyst picks numbers from thin air.
- She says founders should learn to push back, and that this takes time and a thick skin.

## Examples and cases used

- McKinsey: she says the firm used to pool profits globally and distribute them among partners each year, as an example of how a partnership works.
- Ghadi detergent: her example of an Indian brand built without parting with equity, by borrowing, because the founders meant the next generation to take over.
- Coca-Cola formula: her example of the kind of secret that would justify clawing back everything from someone who leaves.
- Policybazaar-style calculator: her example of a core feature that must be built in-house.
- Her own company: ESOPs given to doctors who stay shareholders after they leave, quarterly visits to unpaid advisers, in-house revenue software, and a 14% EBITDA norm that investors tried to raise to 20%.

## Numbers given

- 2 directors and 2 shareholders at least, 15 directors and 200 shareholders at most, for a private limited company as she states it.
- 10 rupees, the face value of each share at incorporation.
- One lakh rupees, the authorised capital she says must be in the bank to issue shares.
- 21 working days, the notice she gives for an AGM.
- 3 to 6 months, her suggested trial period for a co-founder.
- 10%, 5% and 5% over three years, her example vesting schedule for a 20% co-founder stake.
- 1.5% to 2%, her ceiling for an adviser's equity.
- 14%, the EBITDA margin she says healthcare companies work to.
- 15% to 20%, the success rate she gives for an institution's investments.
