# Orientation day 3 on how a VC fund's maths works, Sarthak Ahuja and Piyush Shah

Typed notes from the third orientation day. Sarthak Ahuja, an author and chartered accountant, walked through the arithmetic of a 100 crore rupee venture fund to show what return a startup must give. Piyush Shah, co-founder of InMobi and president of Glance, then answered how to use the six months of the programme.

## What was said

### Innovation in consumer products, Sarthak Ahuja

- Sarthak Ahuja says the big problems are already solved, so innovation is in small tweaks, such as packaging or making a product relatable for a particular consumer.
- He says millennials did not have enough pocket money growing up, so now they want a better lifestyle and do not keep themselves away from experiences or products.
- He says chocolate with Indian flavours is booming at the moment.
- He says big companies cannot take the risk of experimenting with new products, because a failure takes money away from the existing product, so they invest in startups that are doing well.

### How a VC fund is built, Sarthak Ahuja

- He says a VC fund works like a mutual fund. Limited partners put in capital and do not run it, and general partners run it, are responsible for returns and put in some of their own money.
- He says general partners raise from family offices and large corporates by offering to manage their funds.
- He explains the 2 in 2 and 20 as 2% of the total fund size each year for the fund's expenses such as salaries.
- In his example of a 100 crore rupee fund with an eight-year lock-in, 16 crore goes to operating expenses and 84 crore is deployed.
- He says that when a newspaper reports a fund's size, only about 84% of it can be deployed.
- He explains carry with a hurdle. If the fund delivers up to 14% the manager takes nothing, and above that the manager takes 20% of the excess.
- His carry example is a fund that delivers 18% against a 14% hurdle, so the manager keeps 20% of the 4% above the hurdle.

### What return the fund needs, Sarthak Ahuja

- He says 5% of managed funds are able to beat the return of the index, and he cites Warren Buffett's bet on the index.
- He says startups have too many variables, such as government and policy changes, war and founder mindset.
- He gives the rule of 72. Divide 72 by the number of years to get the yearly rate needed to double the money.
- He says doubling money in eight years is a 9% return and tripling it is about 14%, while the index gives 12 to 14%.
- He says that for the higher risk a fund must deliver 20% returns.
- He says a fund that returns 400 crore on 100 crore, four times the money, is a good fund but still not the best.

### What that means for one startup, Sarthak Ahuja

- He says a fund invests in about 20 companies because its outsized return depends on a few winners.
- In his example each of 20 startups gets 2.1 crore rupees first, and the 10 that do well get a further 4.2 crore, for 6.3 crore in total.
- He says a VC keeps capital back for follow-on rounds in the companies that have done well.
- He says that if only one of the 20 startups gives the return, that startup alone has to deliver a 400 crore exit to the fund.
- He says that with the fund holding about 20%, the company must be valued at around 2,000 crore rupees.
- In his example the fund invested at a 30 crore valuation when revenue was 6 crore, and sales have to go to 400 crore.
- He says the first thing a founder should understand is what sort of return they can give an investor.
- He says the real game in consumer brands is to understand whether the business is VC fundable.
- He says family offices invest in startups after making a lot of money, partly as a social symbol and to enable other founders.
- He says family offices use VC lawyers who put in five-year exit clauses, but a founder can take two to three years extra.
- He says that statistically a person is better off with a job, and a founder is betting on being in the 5%.
- He says a VC fund is the investor's own entrepreneurial journey, built on network, better access to deals, pedigree and intuition.

### Working habits, Sarthak Ahuja

- He says to track MIS every week, because only what gets measured can be improved.
- He says he reads for about four hours every day and records an insight as soon as he gets it, which he calls building a second brain.
- For content he says to open with a hook, give facts and back them up by the 20th second, and give a reason to believe and a reason to share.

### Using the programme, Piyush Shah

- Piyush Shah says founders in India should not worry about capital, and that what is needed is belief and resilience.
- He says InMobi had four near-death experiences, and that all the top companies today went through very tough times.
- He says idea-stage founders should spend the six months sharpening the problem statement by talking to customers and consumers.
- He says to talk to alumni to validate your thinking, not to get funding.
- He says not to mistake revenue for product-market fit, and to spend more time on the problem, the customer and the community.
- The notes for this answer record a case of three pivots to find product-market fit, where founders with 20 crore rupees of revenue pivoted because the growth rate was not there.
- He says speed of learning and speed of insight matter in the long run.
- He says co-founders are needed for a shared vision and for judgement that covers your blind spots, not only for complementary skills.

## Examples and cases used

- Mamaearth and Boat: Ahuja names them as consumer companies that reached the needed scale by tapping new distribution channels, namely marketplaces, the pandemic and influencer marketing.
- Alpenliebe: his example of a large brand that cannot risk a new chatpata flavour, so large companies back startups instead.
- InMobi: Shah says it had four near-death experiences.
- Rembrand: the notes record its founder Rishav Jalan saying the company was still figuring out product-market fit after two years.

## Numbers given

- Ahuja's 100 crore rupee fund: 2% a year for eight years is 16 crore of expenses, leaving 84 crore to invest.
- Ahuja's deployment: 2.1 crore each into 20 startups, then 4.2 crore more into the 10 that perform.
- Ahuja's return yardsticks: 9% a year doubles money in eight years, about 14% triples it, the index gives 12 to 14%, and a fund should give 20%.
- Ahuja: 5% of managed funds beat the index.
