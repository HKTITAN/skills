# Founder equity, vesting and written agreements, Anil Advani

A remote session with a US based startup lawyer, identified from the content as Anil Advani of Inventus Law. He is not named in the recording. The recording starts partway through his explanation of vesting and then runs as questions and answers with the cohort on founder mistakes, company structure, angel rounds and trademarks. He refers several detailed India questions to a colleague's follow-up session.

## What was said

### Founder vesting

- Anil Advani says vesting protects the company if a founder does not work out, and also protects the remaining founders from carrying on working for the equity of someone who has left.
- He says founders should push to be issued founder stock on day one, when the value is very low, pay for it outright, and then owe no tax until they sell, at which point it is a capital gain.
- He explains reverse vesting: founders own all their shares and have full shareholder rights from day one, and agree that if they leave the company can buy back the unvested portion.
- The buy back is at the original price the founder paid.
- He treats four year vesting as the standard.
- He says the way it works differs for employees, advisors and consultants, who receive options and not founder stock.
- He says the concept applies equally to founders of an Indian company. The tax consequences and the terms differ, for example founders subscribe to equity shares at formation, but the logic is the same and it is now standard practice for Indian startups.

### Mistakes he sees founders repeat

- He names starting with fully vested stock, because the co-founder is an old friend and nobody expects anyone to leave, as a common mistake.
- He says three founders who split equity into equal thirds simply because there are three of them will end up in a conflict, two against one, every time.
- He says many legal slips can be fixed later as long as everyone is still around the table, but founder equity terms not set right on day one become very expensive or impossible to fix once there is a dispute.
- He says founders often fail to define who is CEO and who decides what. Everyone should get the same information, but one person should be the face for matters such as negotiating with investors and dealing with lawyers.
- His phrase for this is "good fences make good neighbors".
- He warns that a friend brought in for a few months of help who believes they are a co-founder, with nothing on paper, can later claim a large share, and no investor will invest in a company with that claim hanging over it.
- He says nobody should do actual work for the company, give formal advice, invest or build, without a written agreement that defines the scope and the compensation in equity or cash. That includes interns.
- He says a simple agreement with each contributor is the difference between no claim and a dispute that can cost thousands of dollars and 6 to 12 months of legal hassle.
- He says writing things down builds trust between friends and does not undermine it.
- He says if co-founders cannot agree on terms on day one, when the shares are worth little, they will not agree later when every percentage point is valuable and egos are involved. His advice then is to walk away and stay friends.
- He does not ask for a legal agreement in every early conversation. First check personal fit, then discuss roles, time and conflicts, reach a verbal agreement on equity and vesting, have a lawyer put it in writing, and only then let the person start work.
- If a prospective co-founder works at a large company, he says to check first whether that employer could claim ownership of what they build for you.
- He says these disputes are financial and not just legal, because they go to the value of the company and consume founder time.

### Protecting the name

- He says a founder who is investing in a brand name should do the basics early: get the domain, check that the name can be registered and file for trademark protection.
- He describes founders who run under a name for two years, then receive a complaint from a third party around a Series A and have to rebrand, which is expensive and damages the brand they built.
- He says a registered trademark does two things: it lets you use the name without worry and it lets you stop others using it.
- He says a founder can file a trademark in a personal name, but the company should own it, because investors will require that.

### LLP or private limited, India or Delaware

- He says investors will not invest in an LLP, so a business that starts as one will have to restructure before raising outside money.
- He says a private limited company shields founders from personal liability for the business, except in cases of fraud.
- He accepts using an LLP for about six months to test traction, and recommends starting as or converting to a private limited company once the founders believe it is a real business and plan to raise money.
- He tells Indian founders not to assume they must be an Indian company just because they are based in India. A Delaware company can raise from global investors.
- He says that choice should follow where the customers, the growth and the investors will come from.

### Equity allocation and the option pool

- He says the first equity discussion is how 100% is divided between the founder pool and the option pool.
- He says to work backwards from the option pool: how many engineers and advisors are needed, their profile and their market value, plus any large hire still to come.
- He gives 10 to 20% as the typical option pool.

### Angel rounds

- He says an angel round has no legal definition.
- He advises raising nothing if the founders have the cash to build the product, and otherwise raising just enough to win some reference customers that make a larger institutional round possible.
- He says to keep the angel round as simple as possible: equity shares with few preferential rights, or compulsorily convertible debentures, and no special rights beyond basic information.
- Between the two he prefers equity shares, because the value is set today. He says CCDs are also straightforward to document and are not pure debt that must be repaid if things go badly.
- He says institutional investors will almost always require preference shares, and that is not something founders can argue much about.
- He says the angel who invests the least tends to create the most problems later, and he advises having none or very few of them.
- He advises no more than 3 to 5 angels at the start, because anyone who invests gains rights in the company.

### Leaving an old venture behind

- To a founder in the room weighing obligations to an earlier venture's backer, he says founders owe it to themselves to build a successful company, and an investor who had no cap on the upside cannot have a cap on the downside.
- He says continuing to run a failed company alongside a new idea only complicates matters. His advice is to shut it down, start a fresh company, and offer the earlier investor some equity in the new one if the founder wants to be fair, which the law does not require.

### Legal tech

- He says legal tech is a very crowded market and he has not come across a startup tool attractive enough for his firm to adopt.
- He sees the incumbents that already hold the data, LexisNexis and Thomson Reuters, as the big winners, building AI tools on top of it and signing firms to multi year contracts.
- He says the better opportunity is niche tools for problems that lawyers themselves name, such as billing for small law firms, and not a product that claims to replace lawyers.

## Examples and cases used

- Facebook: he says the people who believed they had brought Mark Zuckerberg in to write code for them never had him sign a paper saying his work belonged to their company, and that one missing paper decided who owned Facebook.
- Google: his example of an employer that could claim what a moonlighting co-founder builds, because it works in almost every area.
- QuickBooks: his model for an easy tool that could be tailored to how small law firms bill.

## Numbers given

- His vesting illustration: two founders with 50% each buy 5 million shares each for 50 dollars each. A founder who leaves after two years of a four year vest has 25% of the company vested, and the company can buy back the other 25%.
- Typical option pool, in his words: 10 to 20%.
- Angel rounds, his comparison: 100,000 dollars was a good angel round 20 years ago, and a 5 million dollar round is now called an angel round.
- Angel cheque sizes, his experience: angels who put in 250,000 to 300,000 dollars do it for a living and want you to succeed, and those who put in 5,000 to 10,000 dollars cause the problems.
- Number of angels he advises at the start: 3 to 5 at most.
