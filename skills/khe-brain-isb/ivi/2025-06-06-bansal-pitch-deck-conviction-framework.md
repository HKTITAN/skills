# Pitch deck conviction framework, Mohit Bansal

A hands-on pitch deck bootcamp led by Mohit Bansal of DeckRooster. He set out a framework for deciding what a deck must say, then had founders write their own messages on sticky notes against each part of it while he answered questions. The recording opens with a short exchange on positioning from before the session proper. Flow and slide wireframes were planned for later in the workshop and are not in this recording.

## What was said

### Positioning, before the session

- Mohit Bansal says a pitch feels better to give as it gets closer to the truth.
- He says to avoid a pitch built on how things should be. Show that the consumer is already educated and already demanding the product.
- He says positioning means laying out the landscape from the consumer's point of view, as their needs or actions and not as a set of companies, and finding a space in it.
- He says behaviour can contradict a stated need, as when someone says they want healthy and drinks something unhealthy, and the positioning space lies in why they do that.
- He says what consumers say can be supporting proof in a pitch but cannot be its base.
- He says a founder may be defining the category too narrowly. His example is a drink that belongs in breakfast or on-the-go food, where the need gap is clearer and other products can follow.
- He says the words used with the customer differ from the category in the founder's head, because the customer does not describe the need in category terms.

### What a deck is for

- He splits the work into three steps: what to say, in what order, and how to put it on slides.
- He says the objective of a deck is conviction and belief. Clarity is only the communication part.
- He says most founders skip the first requirement, which is well-defined future outcomes that the pitch promises.
- He says conviction comes from three layers, strategic narrative, operational soundness and proof, and that conviction plus a plan is what makes the outcomes believable.
- He says "Deck is nothing but messages", and the exercise is to get every message out of the founder's head and onto paper first.

### Future outcomes

- He says a future outcome can be two, seven or ten years out, and not everything has to fit an 18-month window.
- His examples of outcomes are 3x revenue growth within 18 months, a clear path to 100 crore, and higher retention.
- He says the founder must be clear on the outcomes even if they are not spelled out in the deck, because the rest of the narrative exists to create conviction that they will happen.
- He says outcomes can be quantitative or qualitative and both matter.
- He says to write down where the business is today next to each outcome.
- He says the outcome has to be exciting enough for the listener, balanced against what the founder believes is realistic.
- He says every message in the deck must support some future outcome. If one does not, an outcome is missing from the list.

### Strategic narrative

- He says an investor passes when the business does not fit their view of where the world is going, so the founder must frame the business inside a larger worldview and state it.
- His form for it is that the world is changing, here is what it needs now, and we are the best ones to meet it.
- He says a good narrative makes the company feel like a high-upside, timely, category-defining play before any number is shown.
- He says the more commodity the business, the less the narrative matters, because everyone shares the worldview. The more innovative it is, the more it matters.
- He says the narrative is the foundation the rest of the pitch is built on.
- He says changes in the industry that help the company belong in the narrative and not in operational soundness.

### Operational soundness

- He says a business is a combination of engines, and an investor wants to understand and validate each one.
- The engines named in the session are customer acquisition or go-to-market, retention, content creation, engagement, monetisation and supply-side acquisition.
- He says to talk only about the engines that matter for your business, and to know how you think about the rest.
- He says a claim of expanding to many cities needs a visible playbook for copying one city to the next. The goal sits in future outcomes and the playbook in operational soundness.

### Proof, plan and ask

- He says proof works through momentum. If customer acquisition cost has fallen month after month, the listener assumes it will continue.
- He says the plan is how the company will bridge the gap between today and the promised outcomes.
- He says the plan decides the ask, and the two must align. If there is nothing special in the plan there is nothing to raise for.
- He says a pre-launch company has little proof, so its deck rests mostly on strategic narrative with some operational soundness.
- He says stated interest from big brands counts as proof for a pre-launch company, while things the founder intends to do belong in the plan.

### Working through investor concerns

- For each layer he asks three questions: what concern or scepticism an investor might have, what the response is, and what they would find exciting.
- He says each concern paired with its response gives a message for the deck.
- He says arguments are built from two things, logic and data, and every claim needs supporting information.
- He says the process is meant to make founders think like an investor and expose gaps before the meeting, since the gaps cannot be fixed on the day.
- He says his firm avoids early-stage clients because the process often sends them back to rethink the business, which is good for them and bad for the engagement.
- He says a risk that is a deal-breaker has to be either reduced first or argued down with enough reasoning and data.
- He says an appendix is a set of backup slides to pull out when a question comes. It should not be sent with the deck.

### Teams with little experience

- He says a team with little experience has to do more work and more planning, study the industry deeply and show what it has learned.
- He says that knowledge shows in the quality of the founder's answers.
- He says at early stage the idea is not always what moves an investor. The depth of thought shown about it is, and that matters more the earlier the company.

## Examples and cases used

- A drink repositioned as breakfast or on-the-go food: redefining the category around the real need gap.
- Falling customer acquisition cost: momentum as proof.
- City-to-city expansion: a replication playbook as evidence of operational soundness.

## Numbers given

- 3x revenue in 18 months and a path to 100 crore: his sample future outcomes, not benchmarks.
- 10 days against 2 days: he says he proposed a 10-day workshop and it was run in 2.

## Not covered

- The order of slides and the flow of the story. Planned for later in the workshop and not in this recording.
- Slide wireframes and design.
- Valuation, deal terms or how much to raise.
