# Design thinking part 2 of 3, authority bias and getting an investor's attention

This is the middle third of a design thinking class recorded on 14 May 2025. The instructor is not named in this slice. Almost all of the slice is one short passage repeated by the transcription tool, so the usable content is a few minutes of talk: the end of his point on authority bias in customer interviews, and his answer to a founder's question about investors.

## What was said

### Authority bias when you interview people

- The instructor says you should not assume a person is right just because they are in a position of authority.
- He says to take what an authority figure tells you for what it is worth: take the data, then validate it against the other data points you have.
- He says not to interview only one person, and to interview multiple people.
- His example is a founder with a path-breaking idea who takes it to a senior person and hears "I have been in this company 15 years, I know how this works". He says that reply shows the senior person is not listening to the idea, only asserting that they know the company and you do not.
- He says a person's 15 years in a company does not mean your idea has no merit.
- His rule for a senior person's opinion is to consider it and not discount it, and also not to assume it is 100% right.
- He says data from other customers can show you that the world does not work the way the authority figure said.

### Incumbents are not automatically right about their own industry

- The instructor says he works closely with automobile companies, including TVS and Ather.
- He contrasts the two: by his account TVS has been in the automobile industry for more than 100 years and Ather for less than 8 years, and the two think differently.
- He says both views can be right, and that TVS saying something does not mean that is how the automobile world will move.
- He says TVS did not predict electric vehicles and should have got into them three years ago.

### A founder's question: what about investors

- A founder asked how to counter authority bias when the person with authority is an investor giving you money. The instructor said investors are a different topic that needs more time and that someone else in the programme would cover it.
- He says investors often have no patience, so a founder has to convey the most important thing in the time the investor has.
- He says that for a VC you need to come through a trusted network and give them what they want in two minutes, among other things.
- He says that if he approaches a VC cold there is a very low chance they listen for ten minutes, and if he goes through someone they know there is a high chance of getting a few minutes.
- He says investors will listen to a few words and assume the rest.
- He classifies this as a communication strategy problem and not authority bias: the question is how you communicate your value proposition.
- He began a story from his own corporate career, where he led a division that incubated emerging products and needed a 50 million dollar investment. He could not get a meeting with the senior leader, and the leader's assistant offered him five minutes while the leader walked between two campuses. The recording loops before he finishes the story.
