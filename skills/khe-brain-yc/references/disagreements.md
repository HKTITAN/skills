# Disagreements and changes

Open this file before you answer. When a question touches a row, the reply gives both sides on a `Disagrees:` or `Changed:` line, each with its person and its piece, and never merges them into one smooth view. Y Combinator is many people writing and speaking over twenty years, so a difference between two of them is normal. Report it; do not resolve it.

The dates of written posts are library dates. Order in time is claimed only where an essay prints its own date or a talk has a library record. An undated post is never placed before or after anything.

## Disagreements

Each side is one claim with its person and its piece.

### Should co-founders split equally, and who holds the tiebreaker?

- Seibel's essay recommends equal or close to equal splits and wants them to become standard. (How to split equity among co-founders, https://www.ycombinator.com/library/5x-how-to-split-equity-among-co-founders)
- Seibel's essay lists the need for a tie-breaker among the cited reasons for unequal splits that it rejects. (How to split equity among co-founders, https://www.ycombinator.com/library/5x-how-to-split-equity-among-co-founders)
- Caldwell says in the 2021 talk that the closer to equal the better, then warns that a straight fifty-fifty deadlock kills companies and suggests the CEO hold one extra share as a tiebreaker agreed in writing. (Dalton & Michael: Co-founder mistakes that kill companies and how to avoid them, https://www.ycombinator.com/library/DZ-dalton-michael-co-founder-mistakes-that-kill-companies-and-how-to-avoid-them)
- Li says in the 2023 talk that the need for a tiebreaker is a fair point but does not have to be met through equity. (Keys To Successful Co-Founder Relationships, https://www.ycombinator.com/library/Im-keys-to-successful-co-founder-relationships)
- Taggar says in the 2021 talk that the default split is 50/50, while noting that other arrangements have succeeded and that a later-stage co-founder can justify a deviation. (How to find the right co-founder, https://www.ycombinator.com/library/8h-how-to-find-the-right-co-founder)
- How to report it: Seibel's essay, Caldwell and Taggar each start from an equal or near-equal split. The tiebreaker is where the pieces part: give Seibel's essay, Caldwell and Li as three positions, each with its piece and date wording, and do not choose among them.

### Can I start with a stranger or a matched co-founder?

- Kwon, answering an audience question in a talk the library dates to 2018, says not to take on a stranger as a partner or co-founder. (Startup legal mechanics, https://www.ycombinator.com/library/7R-startup-legal-mechanics)
- Carolynn Levy adds in that 2018 talk that involving a stranger stacks the deck against you. (Startup legal mechanics, https://www.ycombinator.com/library/7R-startup-legal-mechanics)
- Graham's 2007 essay says a cofounder could be met through a user's group or a conference but that he would not be too optimistic, because you need to work with someone to know whether you want them. (Why to Not Not Start a Startup, https://paulgraham.com/notnot.html)
- A note placed above Taggar's talk says the matching service launched after the video, that the advice to search your own network still holds, and that founders should now try the service in parallel. (How to find the right co-founder, https://www.ycombinator.com/library/8h-how-to-find-the-right-co-founder)
- Li says in the 2023 talk that it is too early to know whether matched teams last seven to ten years, and that over a dozen teams met there, applied together and got in. (Keys To Successful Co-Founder Relationships, https://www.ycombinator.com/library/Im-keys-to-successful-co-founder-relationships)
- Khemani and Manalac's post does not suggest starting a company immediately with someone met on the platform, and says the work of building trust remains. (Startups for students, https://www.ycombinator.com/library/D0-startups-for-students)
- How to report it: give the 2018 answer against strangers and the lines on matching side by side, with each person and piece, and do not order the undated post in time. Say that Kwon's line answers an audience question about a stranger, that Khemani and Manalac's post keeps the work of building trust, and that Li says it is too early to know whether matched teams last.

### Can a solo founder start a company?

- Graham's 2006 essay lists a single founder as the first of its mistakes. (The 18 Mistakes That Kill Startups, https://paulgraham.com/startupmistakes.html)
- Graham's 2007 essay reports having funded two single founders, both told that finding a cofounder was their first priority. (Why to Not Not Start a Startup, https://paulgraham.com/notnot.html)
- Taggar's default answer for someone keen to start now without a co-founder is to wait until they have one. (How to find the right co-founder, https://www.ycombinator.com/library/8h-how-to-find-the-right-co-founder)
- Bhat, in a talk the library dates to 2023, says you can start alone but that doing so makes a startup "more than twice as hard". (Keys To Successful Co-Founder Relationships, https://www.ycombinator.com/library/Im-keys-to-successful-co-founder-relationships)
- Caldwell says being a solo founder is not a reason to hold back from applying, while noting that the programme encourages founders to have co-founders. (How to apply and succeed at Y Combinator, https://www.ycombinator.com/library/6t-how-to-apply-and-succeed-at-y-combinator)
- Li, in the 2023 talk, says only four of YC's top hundred companies were started by a solo founder. (Keys To Successful Co-Founder Relationships, https://www.ycombinator.com/library/Im-keys-to-successful-co-founder-relationships)
- Khemani and Manalac's post for students gives a different figure, saying 7.9 percent of YC's top companies list had solo founders. (Startups for students, https://www.ycombinator.com/library/D0-startups-for-students)
- How to report it: give the range from Graham's two essays to Bhat and Caldwell, each with its year wording. Give both counts of solo-founded top companies with their pieces and do not reconcile them.

### How long should a co-founder trial run?

- Li recommends a trial project before committing, agreed in advance with a timeline of usually two to four weeks. (Keys To Successful Co-Founder Relationships, https://www.ycombinator.com/library/Im-keys-to-successful-co-founder-relationships)
- Taggar's 2021 version of the trial is a set period with a deadline, a month or three months full time for two people with no other commitments. (How to find the right co-founder, https://www.ycombinator.com/library/8h-how-to-find-the-right-co-founder)
- The Y Combinator post of ten questions says no questionnaire replaces time spent together in person, and that knowing for sure usually takes several months. (10 questions to discuss with a potential co-founder, https://www.ycombinator.com/library/Hj-10-questions-to-discuss-with-a-potential-co-founder)
- How to report it: give the three lengths with their pieces. Say that Li and Taggar describe a trial project and the Y Combinator post describes how long knowing for sure takes.

### Should I charge from day one?

- Seibel tells founders entering YC that starting with a price is almost always better than starting free, and adds that free is fine for users you never plan to charge. (Building product, https://www.ycombinator.com/library/7s-building-product)
- Rusenko, in a talk the library dates to 2018, says not to worry about monetization at first and to make sure the product is working for people, though he also says not to shy away from charging. (How to find product-market fit, https://www.ycombinator.com/library/7V-how-to-find-product-market-fit)
- Graham's 2012 essay says revenue is the best thing to measure growth in, and for startups not charging at first the next best is active users. (Startup = Growth, https://paulgraham.com/growth.html)
- Bhat, in a talk the library dates to 2023, says a founder who plans to charge should get paid from day one or at least leave free users out of the growth count. (Setting KPIs and goals, https://www.ycombinator.com/library/Iu-setting-kpis-and-goals)
- Graham's 2007 essay tells founders to make something great without worrying too much about making money. (Why to Not Not Start a Startup, https://paulgraham.com/notnot.html)
- Gupta, in a talk the library dates to 2026, says to charge real money early, with feedback and not revenue as the goal. (How To Get Your First Users, https://www.ycombinator.com/library/ND-how-to-get-your-first-users)
- How to report it: give Seibel against Rusenko, and Graham's 2012 line on counting active users against Bhat's line on leaving free users out. Say that Graham's line is for startups not charging at first and Bhat's for a founder who plans to charge. Name each piece and its year wording.

### How good does the first launch have to be?

- Seibel, in a talk the library dates to 2019, says the one thing to take from his whole talk is "launch something bad, quickly". (How to plan an MVP, https://www.ycombinator.com/library/6f-how-to-plan-an-mvp)
- The Y Combinator post says launching a mediocre product as soon as possible, then talking to customers and iterating, beats waiting to build a perfect one. (YC's essential startup advice, https://www.ycombinator.com/library/4D-yc-s-essential-startup-advice)
- Graham's 2006 essay says releasing early means releasing something minimal and not something full of bugs. (The Hardest Lessons for Startups to Learn, https://paulgraham.com/startuplessons.html)
- Rusenko, in a talk the library dates to 2018, prefers a minimum remarkable product, citing Paul Buchheit's advice to launch when the product is better than what is out there, and says not to launch before that point. (How to find product-market fit, https://www.ycombinator.com/library/7V-how-to-find-product-market-fit)
- Manalac, in a talk the library dates to 2023, lists the worst cases of launching too early as people finding it ugly, investors or competitors seeing it, or nobody caring, and says her answer is to iterate and launch again. (The best way to launch your startup, https://www.ycombinator.com/library/Ir-the-best-way-to-launch-your-startup)
- How to report it: give the bar each person sets, from bad or mediocre, through minimal without bugs, to better than what is out there, with person, piece and date wording. Do not average them into one bar.

### How soon should I pivot?

- Seibel, in a talk the library dates to 2018, defines a pivot as changing the customer or the problem and says a pivot should be rare. (Building product, https://www.ycombinator.com/library/7s-building-product)
- Seibel says finding the solution is more like a two-year process. (Building product, https://www.ycombinator.com/library/7s-building-product)
- Caldwell, in a talk the library dates to 2019, says that for founders before launch or just after it a pivot is only a change of idea, that changing ideas constantly is the norm, and that founders who are not doing so are likely moving too slowly. (All about pivoting, https://www.ycombinator.com/library/6p-all-about-pivoting)
- Caldwell says that on average more founders take too long to pivot than pivot too soon. (All about pivoting, https://www.ycombinator.com/library/6p-all-about-pivoting)
- Caldwell says stories of people who persisted for five years and won are true but, like stories of lottery winners, not something a founder can act on. (All about pivoting, https://www.ycombinator.com/library/6p-all-about-pivoting)
- Seibel, speaking as a Justin.tv founder in the recorded discussion of his post, which the library dates to 2018, says that each year a company does not die its chances of success go up. (The real product-market fit, https://www.ycombinator.com/library/5z-the-real-product-market-fit)
- How to report it: give both men with their talks and years. Say that the two define a pivot differently and that Caldwell's line is addressed to founders before launch or just after it.

### How many user interviews before I build?

- Rusenko, in a talk the library dates to 2018, says about five to ten customer interviews are what a founder needs. (How to find product-market fit, https://www.ycombinator.com/library/7V-how-to-find-product-market-fit)
- Alstromer says that after five or ten interviews, and before any MVP, a founder should form a hypothesis for the solution and start building an MVP as fast as possible. (How to talk to users, https://www.ycombinator.com/library/Iq-how-to-talk-to-users)
- Shear's method for winning users from competitors comes to about 50 interviews in all. (How to prioritize features, https://www.ycombinator.com/library/8p-how-to-prioritize-features)
- Seibel, in a talk the library dates to 2023, names the wrong answer as a hundred surveys, six hundred user interviews, contacting every competitor, a year of fundraising and a hundred hires before anything ships. (How to build an MVP, https://www.ycombinator.com/library/Io-how-to-build-an-mvp)
- Manalac, in a talk the library dates to 2023, says the DoorDash founders went on to interview more than 200 small business owners around the Bay Area and then built the MVP in a few hours. (The best way to launch your startup, https://www.ycombinator.com/library/Ir-the-best-way-to-launch-your-startup)
- How to report it: give each count with its person, piece and date wording, and say what the interviews were for in each case. Shear's count is for winning users from competitors, and Manalac's is one company's story.

### One metric, or several?

- Seibel, in a talk the library dates to 2018, says a company needs one number that shows how it is doing: almost always revenue if it will ever charge customers, and a usage measure such as DAU only if it never will. (Building product, https://www.ycombinator.com/library/7s-building-product)
- In the recorded discussion of Seibel's post, which the library dates to 2018, the speaker refuses to choose among growth, retention and profitability calls revenue that you keep the closest thing to a single metric, and says even that needs other numbers beside it. (The real product-market fit, https://www.ycombinator.com/library/5z-the-real-product-market-fit)
- The Y Combinator post says a startup should choose very early one or two key metrics to judge success by. (YC's essential startup advice, https://www.ycombinator.com/library/4D-yc-s-essential-startup-advice)
- Rusenko tracks three things, returning usage, NPS and paying customer renewal rates. (How to find product-market fit, https://www.ycombinator.com/library/7V-how-to-find-product-market-fit)
- Shear says Three Numbers Matter means naming the three qualities customers care about most, giving each a metric and giving priority to ideas that move them. (How to prioritize features, https://www.ycombinator.com/library/8p-how-to-prioritize-features)
- How to report it: give the count each piece uses, one, one or two, or three, with its person and piece. Say that Shear's three numbers are a way to prioritize features, and do not pick a count.

### Is Net Promoter Score worth tracking?

- Alstromer, in a talk the library dates to 2019, calls Net Promoter Score a worse way to measure product-market fit, saying it tracks how a company is perceived and does not necessarily track whether the product is good. (Growth for startups, https://www.ycombinator.com/library/6k-growth-for-startups)
- Altman, in a footnote to an undated post, says Net Promoter Score can also be a good way of measuring whether users love a product. (Before growing your startup, https://www.ycombinator.com/library/4p-before-growing-your-startup)
- Rusenko says some people set the NPS bar at 40 and he puts it at about 50. (How to find product-market fit, https://www.ycombinator.com/library/7V-how-to-find-product-market-fit)
- How to report it: give Alstromer against Altman and Rusenko, each with his piece. Altman's post is undated, so do not say which view came first.

### Is the growth goal a rate or an absolute number?

- Graham's 2012 essay says an answer like a hundred new customers a month gets the reply "That's not a rate." because what counts is new customers as a ratio of existing ones. (Startup = Growth, https://paulgraham.com/growth.html)
- Graham's 2012 essay, describing how growth is measured during the Y Combinator batch, states the target as a weekly percentage, his example being 7 percent a week. (Startup = Growth, https://paulgraham.com/growth.html)
- Hariharan's guide, written for a growth team's first year at a company that has already proven retention, says the goal must be an absolute number and cannot be a percentage or rate change. (How to set up, hire, and scale a growth strategy and team, https://www.ycombinator.com/library/59-how-to-set-up-hire-and-scale-a-growth-strategy-and-team)
- Bhat, in a talk the library dates to 2023, says to set a KPI goal for the week, her example being 10 more paying customers by next week. (Setting KPIs and goals, https://www.ycombinator.com/library/Iu-setting-kpis-and-goals)
- How to report it: give Graham's rate and Hariharan's absolute number with the stage each is written for, the batch and a growth team at a company with proven retention. Give Bhat's weekly example beside them.

### How much of the value do I charge, and how do I raise the price?

- Hale's rule of thumb, in a talk the library dates to 2019, is that the value should be ten times the price. (Startup pricing 101, https://www.ycombinator.com/library/6h-startup-pricing-101)
- Blomfield, in a talk the library dates to 2024, charges B2B customers between 25 and 50% of the value the product delivers. (How To Price For B2B, https://www.ycombinator.com/library/LI-how-to-price-for-b2b)
- Hale, in a talk the library dates to 2019, tells founders who have any price to practise raising it by 5% at a time and to keep going until they lose 20% of their deals. (Startup pricing 101, https://www.ycombinator.com/library/6h-startup-pricing-101)
- Blomfield, in a talk the library dates to 2024, tells B2B founders who cannot work out a price to raise it by 50% with each new customer pitched, and to treat losing more than 25% of potential deals on price alone as the sign the number is about right. (How To Price For B2B, https://www.ycombinator.com/library/LI-how-to-price-for-b2b)
- Epstein, in a talk the library dates to 2022, says to keep raising prices in small steps until users push back. (YC guide to business models, https://www.ycombinator.com/library/Gh-yc-guide-to-business-models)
- How to report it: give both numbers for the share of value and both step sizes, each with its person, talk and year. Say that Blomfield's figures are for B2B, and do not blend the percentages.

### Should I work unpaid for a first business customer?

- Graham's 2013 essay says he sometimes advises founders of B2B startups to choose one user and behave like consultants building for that user alone. (Do Things that Don't Scale, https://paulgraham.com/ds.html)
- Graham's 2013 essay says this is safe only while the startup is not paid for the attentiveness, because a customer who starts paying by the hour expects everything to be done. (Do Things that Don't Scale, https://paulgraham.com/ds.html)
- Blomfield, in a talk the library dates to 2025, says design partnerships are often far too long, three or six months, poorly scoped, and short of engagement because the customer is not paying for the founders' time. (The Sales Playbook For Founders, https://www.ycombinator.com/library/Mo-the-sales-playbook-for-founders)
- Blomfield says getting paid for the pilot upfront makes the customer take it much more seriously, since they are now paying for the founders' time. (The Sales Playbook For Founders, https://www.ycombinator.com/library/Mo-the-sales-playbook-for-founders)
- Alstromer, in a talk the library dates to 2022, says B2B startups should not offer free trials and should offer a money-back guarantee or a way out of the annual contract instead. (How to get your first customers, https://www.ycombinator.com/library/Ip-how-to-get-your-first-customers)
- How to report it: give Graham's 2013 essay against Blomfield's 2025 talk and Alstromer's 2022 talk. Say that Graham writes of one user served like a consultant and Blomfield of design partnerships and pilots.

### Do I sell first to whoever buys quickest?

- Graham's 2013 essay says in a note that when the choice is between users who will sign up quickest and those who will pay the most, it is usually best to pick the quickest. (Do Things that Don't Scale, https://paulgraham.com/ds.html)
- Koomen calls the idea of selling to companies that buy quickly even when they are poor customers a misconception: look for fast buyers, he says, but only ones with the problem, the budget and the authority to decide. (Enterprise Sales for Founders, https://www.ycombinator.com/library/LF-enterprise-sales-for-founders)
- Alstromer, in a talk the library dates to 2022, says your first customers should be your easiest: sell to people you know, drop leads that move slowly, and do not try the hardest customer first. (How to get your first customers, https://www.ycombinator.com/library/Ip-how-to-get-your-first-customers)
- How to report it: give Graham's note and Koomen's correction with their pieces. Say that Graham compares the quickest with those who pay the most, and that Koomen adds conditions to speed.

### Are conferences a waste of time?

- The Y Combinator post's pocket list says to avoid conferences unless they are the best way to get customers. (YC's essential startup advice, https://www.ycombinator.com/library/4D-yc-s-essential-startup-advice)
- Livingston lists going to conferences among the things she sees distract founders. (How not to fail, https://www.ycombinator.com/library/5l-how-not-to-fail)
- Altman, in a talk the library dates to 2014, says the most common way companies fail after Y Combinator is that founders start doing a lot of other things afterwards, such as advising companies and going to conferences. (Later stage advice for startups, https://www.ycombinator.com/library/4q-later-stage-advice-for-startups)
- Koomen says that when a startup's customers gather at industry conferences the founders should be there too, with the attendee list in hand and meetings set up in advance. (Enterprise Sales for Founders, https://www.ycombinator.com/library/LF-enterprise-sales-for-founders)
- How to report it: give the warnings and Koomen's line with their pieces. Point out that the Y Combinator post carries its own exception for conferences that are the best way to get customers.

### Should a small startup chase deals with large companies?

- The Y Combinator post says founders are tempted to chase big deals with large companies, and that such deals rarely end well for a tiny startup. (YC's essential startup advice, https://www.ycombinator.com/library/4D-yc-s-essential-startup-advice)
- Graham's 2013 essay says partnerships with big companies usually do not work for startups, and least of all as a way to get growth started. (Do Things that Don't Scale, https://paulgraham.com/ds.html)
- Alstromer, in a talk the library dates to 2022, says startups are the easiest category to sell to, because decisions there are quick and larger companies add bureaucracy and negotiating departments. (How to get your first customers, https://www.ycombinator.com/library/Ip-how-to-get-your-first-customers)
- Junee, a founder writing in the YC library, argues that hackers can sell to enterprises. (Enterprise sales for hackers, https://www.ycombinator.com/library/4m-enterprise-sales-for-hackers)
- Junee addresses early stage companies with no repeatable sales process yet, and assumes they are at or near product-market fit. (Enterprise sales for hackers, https://www.ycombinator.com/library/4m-enterprise-sales-for-hackers)
- How to report it: give the warnings and Junee's case with their pieces. Say what differs: Graham writes of partnerships, the Y Combinator post of big deals for a tiny startup, and Junee of companies at or near product-market fit.

### What goes on a deck slide, and is a deck needed?

- Ralston says the deck should work as a leave-behind, and that graphics, charts and screenshots are more powerful than many words. (A guide to seed fundraising, https://www.ycombinator.com/library/4A-a-guide-to-seed-fundraising)
- Hale, writing about Demo Day slides, says he usually hates screenshots because they are almost always illegible, complex and non-obvious. (How to design a better pitch deck, https://www.ycombinator.com/library/4T-how-to-design-a-better-pitch-deck)
- Hale says a simple slide expresses one idea, so a Demo Day deck ideally has only about 5 to 7 slides. (How to design a better pitch deck, https://www.ycombinator.com/library/4T-how-to-design-a-better-pitch-deck)
- Harris's template opens with a title slide and then runs through problem, solution, traction, what makes you special, business model, market, team, and the money you need. (How to build your seed round pitch deck, https://www.ycombinator.com/library/2u-how-to-build-your-seed-round-pitch-deck)
- Seibel says attaching a deck is fine and not required. (How to cold email investors, https://www.ycombinator.com/library/65-how-to-cold-email-investors)
- Flora says Retool's founder had no deck, opened his laptop, built a small internal tool in minutes, and that this convinced Flora to write a check. (How startup fundraising works, https://www.ycombinator.com/library/Il-how-startup-fundraising-works)
- How to report it: give Ralston against Hale on screenshots, and Hale's slide count beside Harris's template. Say that Hale writes about Demo Day slides and Harris about a seed deck, and that Seibel's line comes from his piece on cold emails.

### Raise as much as I can, or only what I need?

- Ralston says the ideal is to raise enough to reach profitability, so that you never have to raise again. (A guide to seed fundraising, https://www.ycombinator.com/library/4A-a-guide-to-seed-fundraising)
- Carolynn Levy, in a talk the library dates to 2019, says a founder offered five million dollars by a VC as a first fundraise in a priced round should take it if it makes sense for the company. (Modern startup funding, https://www.ycombinator.com/library/8F-modern-startup-funding)
- Seibel, in a talk the library dates to 2020, says that all things equal raising more than a company needs leads to less success, because a larger seed raises the Series A investor's expectations. (The 5 things that kill startups post seed rounds, https://www.ycombinator.com/library/8a-the-5-things-that-kill-startups-post-seed-rounds)
- Seibel reads a YC founder's note that says "Raise what you need and nothing more." because a company finds a way to spend whatever is in the bank. (Dalton & Michael: YC founders made these fundraising mistakes, https://www.ycombinator.com/library/DY-dalton-michael-yc-founders-made-these-fundraising-mistakes)
- Graham's 2006 essay says the trouble with raising too much is what comes with the money: investors want it put to work, the company hires, and it gets harder to change direction. (The 18 Mistakes That Kill Startups, https://paulgraham.com/startupmistakes.html)
- How to report it: give Ralston's ideal beside Seibel, the founder's note and Graham's 2006 essay, each with its piece and date wording. Say that Carolynn Levy's line is about taking a priced round as a first fundraise and sets no amount to raise, and that the note is a founder's, read aloud by Seibel.

### Should a seed pitch carry financial projections?

- Ralston's list of things not to do with investors includes creating detailed financials. (A guide to seed fundraising, https://www.ycombinator.com/library/4A-a-guide-to-seed-fundraising)
- Ralston says an investor asking for too much diligence or financials is almost certainly one to avoid. (A guide to seed fundraising, https://www.ycombinator.com/library/4A-a-guide-to-seed-fundraising)
- Nathoo, in a talk the library dates to 2019, says her inclination is against putting forecasts in a seed deck before product market fit, and that by Series A a company should have some plans. (Managing startup finances, https://www.ycombinator.com/library/8I-managing-startup-finances)
- The Y Combinator post lists business materials to consider, among them monthly historical financial statements and financial projections. (Data rooms and additional documents to prepare for seed fundraising, https://www.ycombinator.com/library/Da-data-rooms-and-additional-documents-to-prepare-for-seed-fundraising)
- How to report it: give Ralston and Nathoo against the Y Combinator post on data rooms, with each piece. Say that the post lists materials to consider for a data room and that Nathoo speaks of the seed deck.

### Do investors push founders to overspend?

- Graham's 2015 essay says founders who have just raised are often encouraged to overhire by their VCs. (Default Alive or Default Dead?, https://paulgraham.com/aord.html)
- Seibel's signs that an investor has become the boss include pressure to spend more than the founder wants and hiring faster than planned. (The 5 things that kill startups post seed rounds, https://www.ycombinator.com/library/8a-the-5-things-that-kill-startups-post-seed-rounds)
- In the 2022 conversation between Caldwell and Seibel, it is called extremely rare for an investor to demand that a company burn heavily, and the problem is said to be that founders need very little push to do it. (Dalton & Michael: Save your startup during an economic downturn, https://www.ycombinator.com/library/Ed-dalton-michael-save-your-startup-during-an-economic-downturn)
- In the 2022 conversation between Caldwell and Seibel, citing Graham's essay, founder and investor incentives on burn are called opposite. (Dalton & Michael: Save your startup during an economic downturn, https://www.ycombinator.com/library/Ed-dalton-michael-save-your-startup-during-an-economic-downturn)
- How to report it: give Graham's 2015 essay beside the 2022 conversation. Say that Graham writes of VCs encouraging overhiring and the conversation of an investor demanding heavy burn, and that the conversation cites Graham on incentives. Name the conversation and not one speaker, as the lines above do.

### Should a founder delegate?

- Epstein tells founders who are starting to manage a team to delegate, because they cannot focus on the highest-leverage work until things are off their plate. (Advice for new managers, https://www.ycombinator.com/library/2s-advice-for-new-managers)
- Rowghani says the CEO of a startup still working to build a product users love is the doer-in-chief, and "Delegation should not be a word in your vocabulary". (The second job of a startup CEO, https://www.ycombinator.com/library/3k-the-second-job-of-a-startup-ceo)
- Rowghani says that once a startup reaches 20 to 30 people the CEO has to spend more time leading, and a CEO who does not delegate ends up slowing everyone else down. (The second job of a startup CEO, https://www.ycombinator.com/library/3k-the-second-job-of-a-startup-ceo)
- How to report it: give Epstein and Rowghani with their pieces and say what differs, the stage. Epstein addresses founders starting to manage a team, and Rowghani splits his answer between a company still building a product users love and one of 20 to 30 people.

### Is hiring the founder's most important job?

- Epstein says hiring well is the most important thing a founder can do for the business and the team. (Advice for new managers, https://www.ycombinator.com/library/2s-advice-for-new-managers)
- Taggar says in the 2022 conversation that successful CEOs who give talks always name hiring as their most important skill, but that for the founders the partners advise it is not the most important muscle to build right now. (Inside the Group Partner Lounge: Don't make these hiring mistakes, https://www.ycombinator.com/library/J2-inside-the-group-partner-lounge-don-t-make-these-hiring-mistakes)
- Seibel says in the same 2022 conversation that nearly all hiring advice online is written for companies past product-market fit, and that applying the advice before it might speed up the company's death. (Inside the Group Partner Lounge: Don't make these hiring mistakes, https://www.ycombinator.com/library/J2-inside-the-group-partner-lounge-don-t-make-these-hiring-mistakes)
- Graham's 2006 essay advises not hiring at all if it can be avoided. (The 18 Mistakes That Kill Startups, https://paulgraham.com/startupmistakes.html)
- How to report it: give Epstein against the 2022 conversation and Graham's 2006 essay, each with its piece. Say that Seibel ties his answer to whether the company is before or past product-market fit, and that Taggar speaks of the founders the partners advise right now.

### What kills startups most often?

- Graham's 2006 essay says that in a sense only one mistake kills startups, not making something users want. (The 18 Mistakes That Kill Startups, https://paulgraham.com/startupmistakes.html)
- Graham's 2006 essay calls not trying hard enough the biggest mistake a founder can make. (The 18 Mistakes That Kill Startups, https://paulgraham.com/startupmistakes.html)
- The Y Combinator post says founders falling out is why most companies fail fast. (YC's essential startup advice, https://www.ycombinator.com/library/4D-yc-s-essential-startup-advice)
- Altman, in a talk the library dates to 2014, calls founders quitting when they should not the final cause of death for most startups. (Later stage advice for startups, https://www.ycombinator.com/library/4q-later-stage-advice-for-startups)
- Taggar, in a talk the library dates to 2021, works backwards: startups fail because founders give up, and founders give up because money or energy or both run out. (Why do startups fail?, https://www.ycombinator.com/library/9u-why-do-startups-fail)
- Seibel, in a talk the library dates to 2019, says a lot of startups that fail do so because the founders lose motivation to keep working on the company. (Biggest mistakes first-time founders make, https://www.ycombinator.com/library/66-biggest-mistakes-first-time-founders-make)
- Graham's 2015 essay calls hiring too fast by far the biggest killer of startups that raise money. (Default Alive or Default Dead?, https://paulgraham.com/aord.html)
- How to report it: give each named cause with its person, piece and date wording. Say that Graham's 2015 line is about startups that raise money, and do not rank the causes.

### Do startups die of overspending?

- Graham's 2006 essay reports that, as of its writing, few startups spent too much and none of the ones his firm had funded had. (The 18 Mistakes That Kill Startups, https://paulgraham.com/startupmistakes.html)
- Livingston, in a talk the library dates to 2016, says startups run out of money by spending too much, and that Y Combinator startups make this mistake constantly despite constant warnings. (How not to fail, https://www.ycombinator.com/library/5l-how-not-to-fail)
- The Y Combinator post's pocket list states that most companies do not die because they run out of money. (YC's essential startup advice, https://www.ycombinator.com/library/4D-yc-s-essential-startup-advice)
- How to report it: give all three with their pieces. Graham's 2006 line is a report as of its writing and Livingston's talk has a 2016 library date, so the years can be given; the Y Combinator post cannot be placed in that order.

### Should I talk to potential acquirers?

- Altman, in a talk the library dates to 2014, calls entertaining acquisition talks one of the biggest killers of companies that are starting to do well, so his rule is not to start a conversation unless a pretty low price would be acceptable. (Later stage advice for startups, https://www.ycombinator.com/library/4q-later-stage-advice-for-startups)
- Altman, in a post for founders whose company is good but not giant, writes "Companies get bought, not sold" and says beginning to build relationships with potential acquirers is a good idea because it usually takes a long time. (Navigating success for your startup, https://www.ycombinator.com/library/4s-navigating-success-for-your-startup)
- Livingston lists talking with potential acquirers among the things she sees distract founders. (How not to fail, https://www.ycombinator.com/library/5l-how-not-to-fail)
- How to report it: give both of Altman's pieces. Say that the talk is about companies starting to do well and the post about a company that is good but not giant. The post carries no year here, so do not order the two.

### Are my twenties for exploring or for a startup?

- Graham's 2014 essay says the early 20s allow things you cannot do as well before or after, such as plunging into projects on a whim and travelling cheaply with no deadline. (Before the Startup, https://paulgraham.com/before.html)
- Graham's 2014 essay says age 20 is not the optimal time and that most people should still be searching breadth-first at 20. (Before the Startup, https://paulgraham.com/before.html)
- Seibel's letter to college students rejects the idea that your 20s are for finding yourself and calls them your most valuable working years. (A letter to college students, https://www.ycombinator.com/library/4d-a-letter-to-college-students)
- Seibel's letter calls ages 22 to 32 your best startup years and says every year not spent doing a startup loses 10% of that prime time. (A letter to college students, https://www.ycombinator.com/library/4d-a-letter-to-college-students)
- How to report it: give Graham's 2014 essay against Seibel's letter, both written for college students. Seibel's letter carries no year here, so do not say which came first.

### Is university research a source of startup ideas?

- Graham's 2012 essay tells students to beware of research, saying a PhD dissertation is extremely unlikely to be a good startup idea, and a note limits the point to IT startups, saying biotech is different. (How to Get Startup Ideas, https://paulgraham.com/startupideas.html)
- Friedman writes that there has never been a better time to start a biotech or hard-tech company, and expects starting a company to become a third option for grad students and postdocs beside academia and industry. (How to spin your scientific research out of a university and into a startup, https://www.ycombinator.com/library/8f-how-to-spin-your-scientific-research-out-of-a-university-and-into-a-startup)
- How to report it: give both with their pieces and say what differs, the kind of company. Graham's note limits his warning to IT startups, and Friedman writes of biotech and hard tech.

## Changes over time

### Graham on students starting companies

- Graham's 2005 essay describes funding founders younger than most investors would back as an experiment to find the lower bound on founder age. (Why Smart People Have Bad Ideas, https://paulgraham.com/bronze.html)
- Graham's 2006 essay says Y Combinator at first encouraged people to start while still in college, and had since decided the line should be to wait until graduation. (A Student's Guide to Startups, https://paulgraham.com/mit.html)
- Graham's 2007 essay withdraws his earlier advice that founders be at least 23 and work at another company for a few years first, and advises people to go ahead and start right out of college. (Why to Not Not Start a Startup, https://paulgraham.com/notnot.html)
- Graham's 2014 essay, written for college students, answers plainly that you should not start a startup in college, and says age 20 is not the optimal time. (Before the Startup, https://paulgraham.com/before.html)
- How to report it: give the essays in order, each with its year. Never quote one year's line as his view without the others beside it, and say whether a line is about starting in college or right out of college.

### Seed round size and instrument

- Carolynn Levy, in a talk the library dates to 2019, describes the old way: a first round was a Series A preferred stock financing of about 1.5 to 2 million dollars that took months and could cost twenty-five to one hundred thousand dollars in legal fees. (Modern startup funding, https://www.ycombinator.com/library/8F-modern-startup-funding)
- Carolynn Levy, in a talk the library dates to 2019, says priced rounds still happen but are no longer how most startups raise their first money. (Modern startup funding, https://www.ycombinator.com/library/8F-modern-startup-funding)
- Nathoo, in a talk the library dates to 2018, says YC had announced a move to post-money SAFEs in the couple of weeks before the talk. (Understanding SAFEs and priced equity rounds, https://www.ycombinator.com/library/6m-understanding-safes-and-priced-equity-rounds)
- Ralston, in an undated post, says most first rounds seem to cluster around six hundred thousand, with sizes rising over the last several years. (A guide to seed fundraising, https://www.ycombinator.com/library/4A-a-guide-to-seed-fundraising)
- Flora, in a talk the library dates to 2023, says a typical seed round is five hundred thousand to maybe a couple of million dollars, closes in weeks if not days, and carries no legal fees. (How startup fundraising works, https://www.ycombinator.com/library/Il-how-startup-fundraising-works)
- How to report it: give every figure with its person and date wording. Ralston's post is undated, so it cannot be placed between the 2019 and 2023 talks. Send the reader to current sources for today's round sizes.

### Seibel on equal splits

- Seibel's essay recommends equal or close to equal splits and says a founder unwilling to give an equal share may be choosing the wrong partner. (How to split equity among co-founders, https://www.ycombinator.com/library/5x-how-to-split-equity-among-co-founders)
- In the video on the same page, which the library dates to 2019, Seibel says he used to say most companies should split equally, calls that an easy rule of thumb, and adds that it cannot always be applied. (How to split equity among co-founders, https://www.ycombinator.com/library/5x-how-to-split-equity-among-co-founders)
- In that 2019 video Seibel declines to prescribe an exact split and tells the CEO to choose the one that maximizes co-founders' motivation over the one a negotiation produced. (How to split equity among co-founders, https://www.ycombinator.com/library/5x-how-to-split-equity-among-co-founders)
- How to report it: the essay and the video sit on one page. Give the essay's rule and the video's qualification together, and do not quote the essay as his whole view.

### Manalac's two launch talks

- Manalac, in a talk the library dates to 2019, says a startup which took six months to launch is likely to be dead before its next chance to launch. (How to launch again and again, https://www.ycombinator.com/library/6i-how-to-launch-again-and-again)
- Manalac, in a talk the library dates to 2023, repeats the point in a softer form: if the first version takes six months to reach anyone, the startup may be dead before another chance to launch. (The best way to launch your startup, https://www.ycombinator.com/library/Ir-the-best-way-to-launch-your-startup)
- Manalac, in a talk the library dates to 2019, says a hardware or physical product company can launch with a pre-order campaign. (How to launch again and again, https://www.ycombinator.com/library/6i-how-to-launch-again-and-again)
- Manalac, in a talk the library dates to 2023, says there is more skepticism about crowdfunding and pre-orders than five years earlier, so a founder should think about whether it fits the company. (The best way to launch your startup, https://www.ycombinator.com/library/Ir-the-best-way-to-launch-your-startup)
- Manalac describes YC's internal Bookface with over 4,000 founders on it in the talk dated 2019 and over 6,000 in the talk dated 2023. (How to launch again and again, https://www.ycombinator.com/library/6i-how-to-launch-again-and-again; The best way to launch your startup, https://www.ycombinator.com/library/Ir-the-best-way-to-launch-your-startup)
- How to report it: give the 2019 line and the 2023 line together with their years. Most of the two talks is the same advice; these are the places where the wording or a number moved.

### Blomfield from 2024 to 2025

- Blomfield, in a talk the library dates to 2024, said of the opt-out contract that a founder can count it as recurring revenue pretty much straight away. (How To Price For B2B, https://www.ycombinator.com/library/LI-how-to-price-for-b2b)
- Blomfield, in a talk the library dates to 2025, adds a caution to be careful what is reported to investors as MRR or ARR while customers are still inside the opt-out period. (The Sales Playbook For Founders, https://www.ycombinator.com/library/Mo-the-sales-playbook-for-founders)
- Blomfield, in a talk the library dates to 2024, says to keep a free trial or pilot to a couple of weeks, maybe four. (How To Price For B2B, https://www.ycombinator.com/library/LI-how-to-price-for-b2b)
- Blomfield, in a talk the library dates to 2025, says a paid pilot should be only long enough for the customer to feel the full benefit, maybe seven or 14 days when the product is well tuned. (The Sales Playbook For Founders, https://www.ycombinator.com/library/Mo-the-sales-playbook-for-founders)
- How to report it: give the 2024 line and the 2025 line with their years. On opt-out contracts the later talk adds a caution to the earlier one; say that, and do not report the 2024 line alone.

### Share of a batch that had applied before

- Caldwell, in a talk the library dates to 2018, says about a third of the previous batch had applied before without getting in. (How to apply and succeed at Y Combinator, https://www.ycombinator.com/library/6t-how-to-apply-and-succeed-at-y-combinator)
- Khemani and Manalac write in an undated post that about 50 percent of the companies funded these days have applied more than once. (Startups for students, https://www.ycombinator.com/library/D0-startups-for-students)
- How to report it: give both figures with their pieces. The post is undated, so do not say the share rose or fell; say only that the two pieces give different figures.

### Tarpit ideas since LLMs

- Caldwell and Seibel, in a talk the library dates to 2024, define a tarpit idea as one of the idea spaces that keep attracting many applicants and never work. (Tarpit Ideas: The Sequel, https://www.ycombinator.com/library/LH-tarpit-ideas-the-sequel)
- Caldwell and Seibel give another mark of a tarpit: the idea has been tried many times, many people failed, and nothing has changed since. (Tarpit Ideas: The Sequel, https://www.ycombinator.com/library/LH-tarpit-ideas-the-sequel)
- Caldwell and Seibel say the list is not static: since LLMs arrived they are open to classic tarpit ideas no longer being tarpits, provided the founder can explain why the new technology makes the thing achievable now. (Tarpit Ideas: The Sequel, https://www.ycombinator.com/library/LH-tarpit-ideas-the-sequel)
- How to report it: give the definition and the 2024 opening together. Carry the condition with it, that the founder can explain why the new technology makes the thing achievable now.

### Seibel on whether everyone should start a startup

- Seibel says in a talk the library dates to 2018 that he first assumed anyone with the money, time and ability should start a startup, and that working at YC quickly showed him this was a bad idea. (Should I start a startup?, https://www.ycombinator.com/library/60-should-i-start-a-startup)
- Seibel sorts people into three groups, and guesses that those who can only work in a radically entrepreneurial job may be only one percent of the population. (Should I start a startup?, https://www.ycombinator.com/library/60-should-i-start-a-startup)
- How to report it: the change is one he reports himself. Give his earlier assumption and his later view from the same piece, in that order.

### Numbers that differ inside one piece or between two tellings

- Altman's written version of the 2014 talk says what works at 20 employees can be disastrous at 40, and the spoken transcript says it is disastrous at 30. (Later stage advice for startups, https://www.ycombinator.com/library/4q-later-stage-advice-for-startups)
- Seibel's post grades a medium idea as half a day for one person. (Guide to product development, https://www.ycombinator.com/library/4e-guide-to-product-development)
- Seibel, in the talk the library dates to 2018, gives the same three grades with medium as a day or two. (Building product, https://www.ycombinator.com/library/7s-building-product)
- Seibel, in the 2021 talk, puts three to four metrics beneath the top one, and later in the same talk gives the base of the pyramid as three to five. (How to choose a metric and set your KPI, https://www.ycombinator.com/library/9i-how-to-choose-a-metric-and-set-your-kpi)
- Hariharan's guide says teams running more than 100 experiments a year report only a third turning out positive, and in the next sentence puts the success rate at 20 to 30 percent. (How to set up, hire, and scale a growth strategy and team, https://www.ycombinator.com/library/59-how-to-set-up-hire-and-scale-a-growth-strategy-and-team)
- Li's post says of Kiwi Biosciences that David Hachuel was immediately interested in Anjie Liu's idea. (Does co-founder matching work?, https://www.ycombinator.com/library/Cq-does-co-founder-matching-work)
- Li's 2023 talk tells the Kiwi story differently: he thought her idea would never work. (Keys To Successful Co-Founder Relationships, https://www.ycombinator.com/library/Im-keys-to-successful-co-founder-relationships)
- Graham's April 2006 essay says he has seen a lot of startups die from releasing too slowly and none from releasing too quickly. (The Hardest Lessons for Startups to Learn, https://paulgraham.com/startuplessons.html)
- Graham's October 2006 essay allows that launching too early is possible and can ruin a reputation. (The 18 Mistakes That Kill Startups, https://paulgraham.com/startupmistakes.html)
- How to report it: give both figures or both tellings and name where each sits, the written or the spoken version, the post or the talk, the earlier or the later essay. Never choose one figure and drop the other.
