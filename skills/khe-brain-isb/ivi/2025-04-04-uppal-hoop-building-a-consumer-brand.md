# Building a consumer health brand, Twinkle Uppal

Typed notes from an orientation talk by Twinkle Uppal, founder of Hoop and an ISB alumna. She described how Hoop launched cooling pain relief patches and sprays, and answered questions on vision, competition, first customers, inventory, packaging and digital marketing.

## What was said

### Hoop's start

- Twinkle Uppal says Hoop's core consumer insight was that consumer needs such as fatigue and backaches differ, but different products for them did not exist.
- She says Hoop's first products were cooling pain relief patches and a spray, and that Hoop was the first to make these in India.
- She says Hoop spent 18 months on R&D and branding before launch, and now has over 6 lakh customers, in tier 2 and tier 3 towns.
- She says Hoop received a grant from Sequoia and inbound interest from P V Sindhu, who used Hoop during the Olympics.
- She says she funded the first 18 months from personal savings while building with her husband, raised a seed round for R&D, and had no team for 12 months.
- She says her former partners at McKinsey were her angel investors.
- She says Sequoia gives women founders a 100,000 dollar equity-free grant.

### Selling the vision

- She says a founder sells the vision to the team, partners, marketing companies and to themselves.
- Her tip is to write the vision down. For a year she wrote down different parts of her vision and noticed how people reacted to each part.
- She says her current articulation of the vision is about the twentieth version.
- She says to listen to founders talking about their vision on podcasts, and to test your idea and vision again and again to see what clicks.

### Competition and moat

- She says competition is natural and is a sign of a good, mature market, and that competitors will copy you.
- She says you will have at most a one year head start, so try to get one.
- She says Hoop convinced its manufacturers not to make the product for anyone else, and that six players launched after Hoop.
- She says Hoop plans to keep innovating and launching, since it may lose leadership as the market grows.
- She says to think about legacy players about once in two months, and otherwise to focus on your goals and obsess over your product.
- She says there is no moat in her industry, and that the true moat is execution, meaning how long the business survives and how it grows.

### Managing time

- She says her co-founder handles operations, efficiency and accounts while she works on return on ad spend, acquisition cost, consumer insights and long-term thinking.
- Her advice to a solo founder is to make every Friday a long-term thinking day and keep Monday to Thursday for efficiency.

### First customers

- She says the first 100 customers come from hustling, such as a Meta campaign, a few stalls, or cold outreach for a B2B founder.
- She says to find the path of least resistance. For her that was a website and Amazon, which needed the least investment.
- She says focus is critical in the early days. Hoop ignored other channels, and she calls offline too much investment.
- She says B2B founders should use warm connections and tackle the market sector by sector.
- She says Hoop built a customer waitlist of 1 lakh people by marketing and building its social accounts before launch.

### Inventory and packaging

- She says to keep inventory and the money tied up in it minimal, and that Hoop killed three products that did not work.
- She says to convince a manufacturer that they may lose money on the first two batches but will make it back in the long run.
- She mentions minimum order quantities of 2,000 and 3,000 against 10,000, and says to keep inventory within a 60 to 90 day sales cycle.
- She describes three types of packaging printing. Digital has a minimum order of a few hundred and a higher unit cost, which keeps the money needed low.
- She says offset printing has a minimum order of about 10,000, and roto printing means over 50,000 labels at once and brought her cost down by 90%.

### Marketing and consumer insight

- She says she spent five to six months studying how to optimise return on ad spend and do digital marketing herself.
- She says an agency charges 6 to 7% of your revenue.
- She says to follow the people who lead Meta and Instagram online to keep up with new updates.
- She says consumer emotion is the key to content, and that she calls consumers every week to ask which ad they liked and why they use the product over others.
- She says going to stalls was another way to understand consumers.
- She says to understand why someone is buying and then A/B test that reason, with different keywords or copy such as relaxing before bed, sleeping better or relaxing after the gym.
- She says Hoop invested heavily in visuals, photographers and videographers.
- She says the best signals are acquisition cost going down, return on ad spend going up and people searching for the brand by name. She also says to track CM2.

### When to launch

- She says that if a product is bad, customers judge all your other products by it, so make the first impression strong.
- She says either you spend the money now or you spend double two years later fixing it.
- She says Hoop's idea-level pivots took six months and R&D took nine months, with registration, dermatological testing, packaging and shipping taking a few more.
- She says most of the time went into registration and R&D, and that the haircare line took 15 months to launch.
- She says it is a resilience game, and a founder should be in it for the long term and survive long enough.

## Examples and cases used

- Hoop: cooling pain relief patches launched after 18 months of R&D, with six copycats afterwards.
- The Whole Truth: she points to its founder as someone who understands the finer details of his product.
- Nike and New Balance: her example that the top businesses in a category keep changing.

## Numbers given

- Hoop, as told by Twinkle Uppal: over 6 lakh customers, 18 months of R&D and branding, a waitlist of 1 lakh before launch.
- Uppal: agencies charge 6 to 7% of revenue.
- Uppal: roto printing at over 50,000 labels cut her packaging cost by 90%.
- Uppal: a 100,000 dollar equity-free grant from Sequoia for women founders.
