# Negotiation class on BATNA, first offers and concessions

A faculty member who teaches negotiation at the school took the second class of a negotiation series. She recapped fairness and power from the first class, worked through single-issue distributive negotiation using a coffee contract simulation the class had played, and then opened integrative negotiation with an arm exercise and a two-party role play over a rare flower. She is not named in the recording. A multi-issue case was set for the next day.

## What was said

### Fairness

- The instructor says negotiations often derail because a negotiator prepares an offer that looks good for the other side while still thinking only about their own interests.
- She says fairness is subjective. In the ultimatum game the class played, some people were happy with 10 and some found even 49 offensive.
- She names three kinds of fairness: distributive, procedural and interactional. She says to work on the last two, the process of reaching the outcome and how you treat the other party, because people differ on what a fair split is.
- She says people agree to things for those they like and trust, and often do not know what a good offer is.

### Power and alternatives

- She says power does not come from being the buyer or the seller. The biggest source of power in a negotiation is alternatives.
- She says the party that is willing to walk away has power, and you can walk away only when you have alternatives.
- She says perception of power matters more than power itself, and the two sides can read the same situation differently.
- She says even when you believe you have more power, do not use it unless necessary.
- She says power is relative. The other party's alternatives matter as much as yours, so do not undersell yourself because your own alternatives look weak.
- She says the other party's alternatives are your competitors, and you estimate them by knowing the competition and talking to people in your network.

### BATNA

- She defines BATNA as the best alternative to a negotiated agreement and calls it the biggest source of power in a negotiation.
- She says to generate as many alternatives as you can before the real negotiation, as two entrepreneurs who spoke to her class said they do.
- She says even "phantom alternatives", conversations that may not materialise, give psychological power to ask for more.
- She says the purpose of a BATNA is psychological power. It is not a threat, and beating it is not the goal.
- She says research on salary negotiation shows people with a BATNA could demand a lot more than people without one.
- She says not to reveal your BATNA, because once the other side knows it you get only a small amount above it.
- She says a BATNA can be revealed towards the end when you only want a little more, or to rescue a talk heading for no deal, and even then it is better to signal it than to state the number.
- She says telling the other side you hold several good offers invites the answer that you should take them.
- She says if you want one unique thing and nothing else will do, you have hardly any power. Her line to students is not to fall in love with one car, one house or one job.
- She says asking why you want something uncovers the underlying interests, and then alternatives that serve those interests become visible.

### Reservation price and target price

- She says the reservation price is the walk-away point, and it sits close to the BATNA adjusted for the cost and effort of taking the alternative.
- She says the reservation price must be a number. Non-monetary issues have to be quantified, for example by spreading 100 points across the issues.
- She says without a quantified reservation price you get anchored on whatever number the other side says.
- She says you never go beyond your reservation price, because that is worse than your current situation.
- She says the common mistake is being satisfied as soon as an offer beats the reservation price or the alternative.
- She says to set a target price from an estimate of what the other party is willing to pay, and to keep your mind on the target during the negotiation.
- She says the purpose of a target price is to keep you motivated to negotiate after you have already beaten your alternative.
- She says the target should be aggressive but not ridiculous, and can be estimated from market benchmarks and the other party's past deals.
- She says a deal can happen only inside the zone of possible agreement between the two reservation prices, unless issues are added.
- She says people accept deals worse than their alternative because of agreement bias: once you are negotiating, no deal feels like a loss. The goal is the best deal for you, not a deal.

### Questions about limits

- She says not to ask the other party for their reservation price. They will probably lie, the question is uncomfortable, and they may ask for yours in return.
- She says not to reveal your own reservation price, because it becomes the anchor.
- She says to learn the other side's limits through hypotheticals, such as asking whether a contract designed a certain way would work for them.
- She says when asked for your best offer you can decline to give the number and ask about the other side's concerns and interests instead.
- She says when a first offer is already above your target, you can call it a good offer and still negotiate other issues, such as equity, without pushing hard.
- She says research shows that when an offer is accepted immediately the other party does not feel satisfied, so negotiate where the context allows it.
- She says context matters. In academia negotiating salary is not well regarded, so people negotiate research and IT budgets.

### Preparation

- She says preparation covers three assessments: your own goals, alternatives and numbers, the other party's interests, alternatives and goals, and the situation, including the issues, the relationship, short or long term, and the norms.
- She separates perspective taking from empathy. Perspective taking means understanding how the other party sees the situation. Empathy means feeling what they feel, and she cites research that empathetic negotiators do not get good outcomes.
- She says to put all of this in a written planning document, because writing it down shows what matters most to you.
- She says to add as many issues as possible, including issues only the other party cares about, because more issues give more room to trade.
- She says information comes from talking to people who know the context, published data and company accounts.
- She says knowing the other party as people matters, and describes a former sales boss who met clients outside work and talked about their interests, not business.

### First offers and anchoring

- She says to make the first offer when you are prepared, and to start close to your target price.
- She says first offers work through anchoring: the other side adjusts from the first number, even when they think it is ridiculous.
- She cites a meta-analysis, which she recalls as covering about 150 studies, that found final outcomes highly correlated with the first offer across cultures and types of negotiation.
- She says people settle near the midpoint between the opening offers, so the first offer is how you pull the midpoint towards your number.
- She says the longer the first offer stays under discussion, the stronger the anchor.
- She says to legitimise a first offer with data and a rationale, and to give the justification before the number.
- She says a coherent story makes a number believable.
- In a job negotiation, she says to explain the reasons and your other offer before quoting a low previous salary, because the first number on the table is the most powerful.
- She says an aggressive offer that is not legitimate can offend and backfire, citing a paper co-authored by the school's dean.
- She says not to make the first offer when you have not done the homework and do not know the bargaining zone.

### Responding to an extreme first offer

- She says not to negotiate from an extreme first offer, because any adjustment still starts from that anchor.
- She says to ask how the other side arrived at the number. If they cannot legitimise it, the anchoring effect weakens.
- She says another response is a different offer, framed around an interest, that is hard to refuse. Her example is a child offered five minutes at a playground who asked only to try each swing once.
- She says an extreme counter-offer can leave both offers outside the bargaining zone, and then no deal happens.

### Concessions

- She says both sides expect concessions, so plan them before the negotiation: how many, how large and with what rationale.
- She says never to give a large concession, because it shows the first offer was not genuine and that you have a lot of room.
- She says several small concessions are better than one large one, because people want to feel they have squeezed a lot out of the other party.
- She says every concession needs a stated rationale, ideally one about how it benefits the other party, because people do not notice a concession unless you point it out.
- She says every concession should be reciprocated, and in a multi-issue negotiation the return can come on another issue.
- She says to shrink the size of each concession as you near your limit, because equal concessions tell the other side nothing and shrinking ones signal that you are reaching your limit.
- She says to reduce concession size moderately. A very large step followed by a tiny one does not work.
- She says when the other side's concessions shrink, accept only if the deal is good for you against your target.

### Multi-issue offers and contingent contracts

- She says all business negotiations are multi-issue, and in multi-issue negotiation you negotiate packages, not single numbers.
- She says to offer several packages at once that are worth the same to you, which she calls multiple equivalent simultaneous offers. This shows you are considering the other side's interests and gives them a choice.
- She describes the decoy effect: adding an option that nobody is meant to pick so that the option you want chosen looks better, as with coffee cup sizes at Starbucks.
- She says a contingent contract ties part of the deal to a future outcome, such as a performance bonus or an early performance review, and suits situations with risk about the future.
- She says any contingent arrangement should be put in writing.

### Integrative negotiation

- She says the belief that you can win only by defeating the other party is wrong. Most business negotiations have some common interest.
- She says differences in preferences are useful, because they are what allow trades that leave both sides better off.
- She says how a situation is labelled changes behaviour. In an experiment she describes, the same task was played more competitively when it was labelled a competitive game.
- She says people focus on numbers because numbers are easy to process, and miss subjective information about interests.
- She says people tend to compromise in the middle on every issue, and both sides lose value that way.
- She says, "do not negotiate on positions. Negotiate on interests."
- She says revealing your interests is fine even though you do not reveal your numbers, and if both sides misrepresent their interests both end up worse off.
- She says to ask questions about why the other side wants something and then listen to the answer, since people often reveal what they want while the listener is busy planning a reply.
- She says the goal in integrative negotiation is still the most for yourself, but the pie can be made bigger first.
- She says telling the other party that your product is better or their cause matters less does not work.
- She says to ask for reciprocity with words like "you would do the same for me" instead of "you owe me".
- She says business negotiations are usually long-term relationships, so how you treat the other party matters.

## Examples and cases used

- Ultimatum game, played in the previous class: used to show that what counts as a fair split differs from person to person.
- Coffee contract simulation: a single-issue price negotiation where each side was given a reservation price and a target price. She uses it to show large concessions and a fixation on the reservation price.
- A watch at a campus stall against the same watch at a mall: her example of adding the cost of travel and time when setting a reservation price.
- Real estate brokers experiment, from the book Negotiating Rationally: brokers who knew the market were shown random list prices and their estimates moved towards those prices.
- Multiplication exercise from the previous class: the true answer was 40,320 and most estimates were far lower, which she attributes to anchoring.
- Jobs in Singapore: she says employers hiring a foreigner must post the job with a salary on a government site and cannot pay above the posted maximum, which gives the candidate a benchmark.
- Arm exercise: pairs score each time the other's hand touches the table. Most pairs treat it as arm wrestling and score little, when cooperating would score more for both.
- Flower role play: two parties bid for the same rare flowers. One needs the petals and the other the stem, so both can get everything they need and share the cost.
- Camp David accords on the Sinai Peninsula: both Egypt and Israel demanded the land. Egypt's interest was ownership and Israel's was security, so ownership went to Egypt and Israel's security need was addressed.
- A Coca-Cola bottler squeezed on margins: asking about the factory showed that its land was very valuable, and the owners sold the factory.

## Numbers given

- 40,320: the correct answer in her anchoring exercise, which most people underestimate by a wide margin.
- About 150 studies: her recollection of the size of the meta-analysis on first offers and final outcomes.
- 100 points: the total she suggests spreading across issues to quantify what matters in a multi-issue negotiation.
