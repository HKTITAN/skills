# Building a 10 on 10 hiring process, Praveen Nair

Praveen Nair leads leadership hiring at xto10x, a startup advisory firm in Bangalore started by former Flipkart leaders. He says he has recruited for a little over two decades. He spoke online to the cohort for about an hour, walking through slides on why hiring matters and a staged hiring process, then took questions.

## What was said

### Why hiring quality matters

- Praveen Nair says the calibre of people in a company is one of its most important differentiators, at the start, while scaling and when it is large.
- He says most founders make the mistake of not paying enough attention to hiring, assuming it will get done through friends and network.
- He says high quality people in a company attract high quality applicants, so a good team makes later hiring easier.
- He says his firm's employee net promoter score work shows that people who feel they are not working with talented colleagues score lower on eNPS, and that having good people to work with is one of the strongest drivers of the score.
- He says a startup needs a person who is relevant for its stage, because a leader with experience far beyond the stage will not do the zero to one work.
- He says some roles need people in numbers, and if the ten people a business needs do not show up when they must perform, that alone can decide whether the business succeeds.

### Hiring choices that went wrong and right

- His first story is an e-commerce startup that hired too many people too quickly for a hyper-local grocery delivery pilot and then had to lay off about 2,000 of them when costs shot up.
- He says the layoff caused a storm in social media and newspapers and damaged the talent brand, so the hiring team spent about six to eight months getting good candidates to apply again.
- His second story is a company that between 2014 and 2016 brought in five or six senior technology leaders from the US in a short time without planning for how they would settle in. He says they did not get the right teams or enough cultural context.
- His third story is a large Indian conglomerate whose chief executive, hired through an internal board process, fell out with the chairman. He puts it down to missing trust and says the company took a hit in the market.
- His positive example is Diageo in India, which he says took two to three years to bring the people of an acquired Indian company up to the global brand and planned succession for the rest.
- He says Diageo's senior exits over the last four to five years have been planned successions and not sudden departures.

### Step 1: define the need

- He says the job description is the easy part and can be drafted with AI tools. The part that matters more sits behind it, which is the success factors.
- He defines a success factor as the gap between where a thing is today and where it must be, with a time frame. He also calls success factors the jobs to be done.
- His sales leader example lists three success factors: revenue from 1 crore to 10 crore, a team from one person to five or ten, and leads from two to ten.
- He says each success factor should be mapped to the competencies that will deliver it, and each competency to the level needed, such as practitioner, advanced or expert.
- He describes a practitioner as someone who has done the work for a while and seen the situation play out but is not yet an expert.
- He says all three of success factors, competencies and levels must be done, because missing one weakens the ability to test a candidate in depth.
- He says the more granular this document is, the easier it is to find the right person, and that many hiring managers skip it and end up back at the drawing board.

### Step 2: assess for fit

- He says the sheet has a fourth column for the panel, naming trusted interviewers and assigning each one specific competencies to test.
- He says the hiring manager must check whether each interviewer is able to judge the competency and the level before the interviewer meets the candidate.
- He says a founder who is the only person meeting a candidate does not have enough data points.
- He says interviewers should not mix their views before giving their own rating, so each view of the candidate is unbiased.
- He counts interviews, business case presentations, case studies and a panel conversation over lunch as forms of assessment, all meant to gather data points on the competencies.
- He suggests asking a candidate to describe a time when they did not know what the job was supposed to be and how they approached it, to see how they deal with ambiguity.
- He says competence should never outweigh a behavioural red flag, and a red flag seen during the interview should be a no-go.
- His example of red flags is a finance head candidate who arrives late, has not read about the company and is rude to restaurant staff.
- He says hiring a competent person despite such behaviour is a risk that sometimes pays off but mostly does not.
- He says the interviewers, hiring manager and recruiter should hold an open and objective debrief on each candidate, and that collecting information without debating it wastes the process.

### Closing the loop with candidates

- He says founders often fail to tell candidates the outcome, and the candidate then goes back and talks to the market about the founder.
- He says to close the loop with every candidate, hired or not, because that person may later be a customer or cross the founder's path again.
- He says feedback to a rejected candidate should be structured, for example praising their understanding of the market and saying what was being looked for instead.

### Step 3: measure and optimise

- He says a hiring manager who meets too many people without reaching a conclusion should suspect the competencies, the process or the interview length.
- He says to take feedback from candidates about the process, such as too much time on the same questions or too little business context for a case.
- He says to take feedback on interviewers as well, including whether they show up on time and introduce themselves, because interviewers often take candidates for granted.

### Step 4: onboarding and talent brand

- He says onboarding covers keeping the candidate engaged from offer to joining, helping them assimilate, and giving them enough time to immerse in the role.
- He says every candidate is a brand ambassador because each one speaks to about ten other people in the market.
- He says case assignments given to candidates should reflect the company's current business problems, since candidates who feel challenged engage more and think better of the process.
- He says employee stories put out on social media and websites help build the talent brand.
- He says recruiting partners help sell the company and its vision and create a buzz in the market, not only find people.

### Answers to founders' questions

- On roles that are not yet clear, he says ambiguity in a startup is normal and candidates understand it, but the founder must still state where things are today and where they need to get to.
- He says a job description can admit that the founder does not know what the role will look like in six months, because candidates value honesty from hiring managers.
- He says startups expect superhumans and will not find them, so the founder should split competencies into must-haves and can-haves and prioritise.
- He says the longer a founder waits for the perfect person, the longer the seat stays vacant, and the founder should weigh what that vacancy costs the business.
- He says a founder should decide which jobs they can let go of, and that those delegated jobs become the success factors for the hire.
- On freelancers and contract hires, he says hiring is hiring whatever the form, and the two things to tie together are what is expected and what is paid for it.
- He says that when both sides are clear and serious, an email, a contract or a signed paper all work, and disputes usually mean one side was not clear about what it signed up for.
- On pay, he says the gap between what the founder will pay and what the candidate wants is closed by negotiation, and market benchmarks can be used.
- On OKRs, which he says is not his own area, he describes the idea as aligning the last person in the company to the company goal by breaking the goal down to each person's jobs to be done.
- He says his firm believes performance management should run continuously through the year and not as a yearly or six-monthly exercise.

## Examples and cases used

- Diageo India: planned succession and a two to three year effort to bring an acquired company's people up to the global brand.
- Google and Facebook: people want to work there because they know great people work there, which is what a talent brand means.
- Unnamed e-commerce startup: over-hiring for a grocery delivery pilot, then a layoff of about 2,000 people that hurt hiring for six to eight months.
- Unnamed company, 2014 to 2016: senior technology hires from Google and Microsoft brought in without a plan for assimilation.
- Unnamed Indian conglomerate: a chief executive who fell out with the chairman.

## Numbers given

- About 2,000 people laid off in his e-commerce story, and six to eight months for the hiring team to recover applications.
- Two to three years for the Diageo integration he describes.
- He says xto10x has been around for about seven years and that his practice has hired about 120 mid to senior leaders for 50 to 60 clients in three years.
- His sales leader example: revenue from 1 crore to 10 crore, team from one to five or ten, leads from two to ten.

## Not covered

- How to write interview questions for a given competency, or a scoring scale for the debrief.
- Equity or ESOP offers for early hires.
- Where to source candidates, beyond the mention of recruiting partners.
- OKRs and performance management in any depth. He said these belong to colleagues.
