# Value pricing, Madhu Viswanathan

Madhu Viswanathan taught a class on value pricing as part of his marketing strategy sessions, following earlier classes on go to market and behavioural pricing. He lectured with frequent questions from the room, worked two numerical examples, showed a short Bain video on elements of value, and closed with a group exercise pricing a made up skincare serum.

## What was said

### Price is not set by supply or cost

- Viswanathan opens by asking which costs more per litre, bottled water or petrol. Premium bottled water costs more than petrol, which he uses to show that price does not follow supply constraints.
- He says an economist's idea of a commodity describes how markets and trade work, not how a person buys. People choose a particular rice from a particular shop because of how it cooks.
- He defines value pricing as moving from a supply oriented view to a consumer oriented view.
- He says people often pay for perception, emotion and brand.
- His line for the class is "you're not selling molecules, you're selling meaning". Water is sold as trust, purity or quality.
- He says that treating a product as a commodity is a self fulfilling prophecy, and that no commodity is really a commodity.
- He says a product looks undifferentiated mainly because the firm is internally focused, and it often takes an outsider to see the value.

### The four steps under a price

- He calls the list price and discounts the tip of the iceberg, with four steps underneath: value discovery, value quantification, value communication and value governance.
- Value discovery is finding out why customers buy and how much they are willing to pay. Quantification turns that into numbers.
- Value communication is making sure the customer understands the value, which is where sales and marketing come in. Governance is keeping price discipline.
- He calls negotiating on price a bad habit. He says to move the conversation to bundles, added benefits and the money you are saving the customer.
- He says that once you negotiate on price, everything else you offer stops holding value in the buyer's eyes.
- He says competing on price means playing a cost game, and that being a low cost provider takes real skill on the supply side.

### Three common pricing anchors and what is wrong with each

- He lists three anchors firms commonly use: cost based, competition oriented and sales oriented.
- On cost plus, he says people use it because costs feel understandable, but they get the costs wrong and then add a margin that is a random number.
- He says small producers he works with leave out interest on money borrowed from community funds, and leave out their own wages. He says entrepreneurs do the same when they subsidise their own wages in their pricing.
- He tells the class not to start with cost plus.
- On matching competitors, he says you are not the same as your competitor, so the competitor's price cannot define yours.
- He says price matching only works when shoppers are in a deal seeking mode. Done all the time, it gives money away.
- On selling for volume, he says discounting means the customer was willing to pay more and you gave money back.
- He says customers won through discounts tend to be very price sensitive, and leave when the price changes.
- He says discounts make sense for clearing inventory, such as flash sales, factory outlets and end of season sales in apparel.
- He says these three anchors still matter as inputs. You can never go below cost, and the competitor's price feeds the customer's reference price.

### Trials, discounts and subsidies

- He says many firms have moved from freemium to a limited offer: full access for about two weeks, or a limited version, long enough for the user to see the value.
- He says an opening discount should come with a clear statement of what the price will be after two or three months.
- He says free or discounted entry is for new market segments. Once value is proven and customers come asking, the offer can be withdrawn slowly.
- He describes a founder he knows who sold a new risk insight product to banks by giving it free for two months, and only to banks judged likely to convert.
- In B2B, he says free work is for showcasing value, not routine business. Give it to early adopters whose use will create a ripple effect, and do not give discounts to everyone.
- He says pricing for early adopters does not have to mean a lower price. It should promote experimentation among the people likely to advocate for the product.
- He calls quick commerce pricing unsustainable. He says it is cross subsidised by investor money or by another profitable business.
- He says Uber and Ola launched with massive discounting, and customers flinched as soon as prices changed.
- He says there is always a price sensitive segment, which he calls penny pinchers, at around 20 to 30% of the population, and fewer in B2B.
- He says serving that segment is a valid strategy, but then the firm must put everything into supply side efficiency.

### Pricing myths

- Myth one in his list is that cost is the price. He says price should reflect the value brought to the customer, not production cost.
- Myth two is that small price moves do not matter. He says even a 1% change in price has a substantial effect on profit.
- Myth three is that customers only care about price. He says B2B buyers prioritise value, reliability and total cost of ownership.
- Myth four is that products cannot be differentiated. He says even commodities can.
- Myth five is that market share equals profit. He says volume without margin destroys value, and tells the class to think in unit economics and profit.
- He adds that a startup need not be unit positive all the time, but should set a norm such as where it must be in two years and check that its pricing gets it there.
- Myth six is that managing price means changing price. He says good salespeople first point the buyer to what the competitor charges.
- He says price, channel and product are sticky and hard to change back, while promotions can be changed freely and have no lasting effect.
- He says pricing a high quality product low creates a perception problem, because buyers expect quality to cost more.
- He says premium brands never discount, though they may run trial offers.
- He says a product shown permanently at a marked down price is read by buyers as simply having the lower price. His example is Naaptol, which people came to see as a discount channel. He says a firm should decide its price and not masquerade as premium.

### Three ways to do value based pricing

- He gives three approaches: total cost of ownership, perceived value pricing and performance based pricing.
- He says total cost of ownership fits products with a high upfront cost and a low lifetime cost, such as heavy equipment and electric vehicles.
- He says perceived value pricing compares your product with a reference, which need not be a direct competitor. Bisleri, he says, positions against unsafe water and not against other drinking water.
- He says performance based pricing starts from the measurable impact the product has for the customer and what the competitor does.
- He says value pricing needs deep customer insight and quantified differentiation, and cites studies that it can deliver up to five times the profit of cost plus in B2B settings.

### Maximum willingness to pay

- He defines maximum willingness to pay as reference value plus positive differentiation value minus negative differentiation value.
- Reference value is the price of the customer's best alternative, adjusted to comparable units. It does not have to be a direct competitor.
- He says any switch to a new product carries negative differentiation value automatically, starting with the cost of switching.
- He says the customer's alternative depends on the segment, which is why knowing the segment comes first.
- He separates three levels of value. Use value is benefit nobody can put a number on. Economic value is what the firm can compute. Perceived value is what the customer believes, and it sets their willingness to pay.
- He says some value is always left on the table because use value cannot be quantified.
- He says the price can sit anywhere between cost, the minimum, and perceived value, the maximum. Choosing where is the pricing strategy.
- He says the two things a firm can work on are perceived value and the reference price. BMW, he says, never asks buyers to compare it with Maruti.
- He says the calculation gives a range and not a single price.
- He says a common move is to find the segment that would pay more, add a minor feature that costs little, and sell it as a separate higher priced product. His example is a business tier of Zoom.

### Total cost of ownership in practice

- He says sellers of electric vehicles choose carefully which costs to include in the comparison. They talk about fuel and leave out maintenance, and some subsidise maintenance for price sensitive segments.
- He says solar panel subsidies tied to a minimum period and leasing agreements are built around the same idea of getting the buyer to total cost of ownership.
- He says closing the gap between economic value and perceived value depends on communication that changes what the buyer looks at.

### Elements of value

- He shows a Bain video that identifies 40 elements of value in B2B buying, grouped as table stakes, functional, ease of doing business, individual and aspirational.
- The video says Bain surveyed over 2,000 decision makers in IT infrastructure and commercial insurance, and that delivering on more elements predicts future revenue and net promoter score better.
- Viswanathan says an acceptable price is a table stake that anchors to what the industry charges, and is only the start of the conversation.
- He says some elements, such as time savings, can be measured, and others cannot.
- He gives four kinds of value: monetary, functional, experiential and social, with social the hardest to capture. He places Louis Vuitton in social value.

### The skincare pricing exercise

- The class priced a made up direct to consumer vitamin C serum by computing maximum willingness to pay, proposing a launch price and pitching an Instagram ad.
- He says production cost does not matter in the exercise as long as the price is above it.
- He set the reference value at 799 rupees, the average of the competitor prices in the case, and says a firm can choose the minimum or maximum instead.
- On the claimed saving in salon visits, he took only a small share of the 1,000 to 2,000 rupee saving, about 200 rupees, because the product cannot claim the whole saving.
- He says that every aggressive value assumption also assumes your marketing and product are strong enough for buyers to see that value.
- His rule is to be conservative when there is no data behind an assumption, and to go all in when there is.
- For a product that lasts longer, he added 5 to 10% of the reference value.
- He says a money back guarantee signals trust and reduces friction for a quality product that lacks a reputation. It is not necessarily a positive differentiator.
- He says the negative differentiation in the case is that the product is brand new and has no customer reviews.
- To put a number on trust, he takes the price gap between a competitor known for trust and a baseline competitor, then removes the part explained by feature differences.
- He says conjoint analysis is the research method for finding what buyers will pay for a single attribute, such as a brand or a country of origin label, and that founders should usually hire someone to run it.
- He judges the three candidate prices. At 699 rupees the product gets volume and loses its premium perception. At 999 it stays under 1,000 and is still the highest in its set. At 1,199 it is above maximum willingness to pay and sells to very few.
- He says a 999 price needs heavy value communication through influencers and celebrity endorsers.
- His own answer is to launch two products, one at 999 and a near identical special edition at 1,199 with one small added feature. The dearer one works as a decoy that makes 999 look attractive.
- He says the 1,199 version is never discounted and is not expected to sell much.
- He says pack sizes do not create a premium. Exclusivity does.

## Examples and cases used

- Bottled water and petrol: premium water sells for more per litre than a constrained fuel.
- Sona Masoori rice: a so called commodity that buyers still choose by source, and that one producer group positioned on low glycaemic index.
- Shell: sells petrol above other stations and stays in business, which he puts down to better service set ups.
- US gasoline stations: the same grade sold side by side with prices that differ by 30%.
- Starbucks: coffee priced as more than coffee.
- Walmart: low cost built on a very efficient and tightly squeezed supply side.
- A large US retailer he studied: daily price matching against rivals lost millions of dollars, because its customers were not switching anyway and, where they were, it matched the wrong competitor.
- Amazon and Flipkart: both scrape and match each other's prices daily.
- Uber, Ola and quick commerce: subsidy led pricing that customers do not stay with.
- Netflix: moved from mail to digital while the old business was profitable, and raises prices knowing it loses some customers.
- LED and incandescent bulbs in the US in 2013: a total cost of ownership comparison.
- Samsung printer advertisement in India: it says the printer pays for itself after 30,000 pages, built on a lower printing cost per page. The printer costs far more than rivals, so the ad shifts what buyers look at.
- Bisleri: positioned against unsafe water.
- BMW and Maruti: choosing your own reference point.
- Zoom: a business tier built from low cost additions.
- Naaptol: permanent discounts reset the perceived price.
- Bain elements of value: the B2B framework shown on video.

## Numbers given

- Premium bottled water at 140 to 150 rupees a litre against petrol at about 94 rupees a litre, the opening comparison.
- 20 to 30% of the population as price sensitive penny pinchers, his general figure.
- Up to five times the profit of cost plus in B2B settings, from studies he cites.
- A worked example: reference value 75,000, positive differentiation 19,000, negative differentiation 12,500, giving a maximum willingness to pay of 81,500 and a pricing range from 75,000 to 81,500.
- LED example: an LED bulb at $36 against an incandescent at $1.25 in the US in 2013. Over 50,000 hours one LED replaces about 42 incandescent bulbs, so bulbs alone cost $36 against about $52. Electricity at 10 cents a kilowatt hour for 500 against 3,000 kilowatt hours brings the LED total to $86.
- Samsung example: 1.30 rupees a page against 1.70 for the competitor, a 40 paise gap that over 30,000 pages comes to 12,000 rupees. He notes the ad counts only the printing cost per page.
- Bain: 40 elements of value, from a survey of over 2,000 decision makers.
- Serum exercise: reference value 799 rupees, candidate prices 699, 999 and 1,199 rupees, and his own estimate of maximum willingness to pay at 1,049 rupees.
