# Customer lifetime value and customer management, Siddharth Shekar

Class 5 of the Marketing and Market Readiness course, on customer lifetime value. The instructor is not named in this recording. The notes for Class 1 of the same course name him as Siddharth Shekar, DLabs director and marketing professor at ISB. He explained what customer lifetime value is, why it ties marketing to company value, how to estimate it, and how to build a customer management strategy on it.

## What was said

### Net benefit and lifetime value

- Siddharth Shekar says that in every transaction the company gets something from the customer and gives something to the customer, and the difference is the net benefit to the company.
- He says net benefit is his own term, and that founders are better served by thinking in net benefit than in gross margin.
- He says customer lifetime value is the net present value of the net benefits from a customer, and that it always looks at the future.
- He says that when you evaluate a prospect and not an existing customer, the acquisition cost goes in as an outflow.
- He says a company whose customers have a higher lifetime value is worth more on the stock market, and that showing a positive lifetime value is important.
- He says the total lifetime value of a cohort of customers is called customer equity.

### Why it links marketing to company value

- He says that decades ago marketers were not found in boardrooms, because nobody could tie marketing directly to shareholder value, which is the board's concern.
- He cites research by Sunil Gupta and co-authors at Columbia, who estimated the lifetime value of current and future customer cohorts for a few real companies and added them up.
- He says that sum, after adjusting for assets and liabilities, was very highly correlated with the companies' stock market value.
- He says this was the first bottom-up way to value a company, and the first to show a direct link from every marketing activity to shareholder value.
- His example is a promotion worth 100 rupees of net benefit if accepted, offered to a customer with a 10% chance of accepting. The expected value of 10 rupees is a direct change in that customer's lifetime value.
- He says this approach is not used in mainstream finance but has become very common in startups.
- He says not to underestimate judgement and prior belief when there is no data. You hold a belief and update it as the data comes in.

### Estimating it

- He defines the retention rate for a period as the customers left at the end divided by those at the start, within the same cohort and not counting new acquisitions.
- His example is 100 customers of whom 80 remain after period one and 40 after period two. Retention is 80% then 50%, and survival over both periods is 40%.
- He says the retention rate is the probability that the customer survives to the end of the period, and each future margin is weighted by that probability and discounted.
- He says cash flows are assumed to come at the end of the period to give a more conservative estimate.
- He says the full formula can be adapted to any context, such as insurance, and should be preferred to shortcuts.
- He says that if margin and retention are assumed constant, the formula reduces to a simple back-of-the-envelope version, which he allows only for quick estimates.
- He says the strength of the framework is one-to-one marketing at the individual level, so it should not be boiled down to an average.
- He says retention matters most because it enters the simplified formula in both the numerator and the denominator.
- He says finance manages the discount rate, and asks the class who manages retention.
- He says two companies with the same acquisition rate end up very different when their retention rates differ, because of compounding.
- He says the effect of a five point rise in retention on lifetime value differs by industry, so there is no rule of thumb and each company should estimate from its own data.
- He says it is very hard to estimate lifetime value where there is no contract, because you cannot observe when a customer has left.
- He says a model he co-developed was up to 70% more accurate than the earlier standard model, but is too complex to estimate for industry to have adopted it.

### Getting more accurate

- He says the 80-20 rule exists because it is a quick way to identify the top 20% of customers so that you can treat them better.
- He says the only difference between a tribal chief's common sense and today is that technology identifies who is valuable more accurately.
- He says to start with whatever method is available and keep pushing to make the estimate more accurate.
- He says the key to accuracy is accounting for more cost and benefit elements at the level of the customer transaction, such as technical support and returns.
- He says companies today ignore returns and support costs and simply take the raw margin.
- The typed notes add that if you identify the wrong 20% of customers the strategy fails.
- On attributing acquisition cost across channels he gives the common methods: all credit to the last touch, or equal credit to every touch.
- He says that if attribution matters to the business, run an experiment.

### Retention and satisfaction

- He says it is long established that as customer satisfaction scores rise, customer retention rises.
- He gives the chain as satisfaction raising retention, retention raising lifetime value, and lifetime value raising shareholder value.
- He says customer satisfaction surveys are far better than the Net Promoter Score, which he says has huge problems that recent research is questioning.

### Customer management strategy

- He says a customer management strategy has three necessary parts: a CRM system, the analytics to estimate lifetime value, and a loyalty programme on top.
- He describes a loyalty programme as a policy for how each kind of customer behaviour will be rewarded or penalised.
- He says the company then picks its top 5, 10 or 15% of customers, studies their past behaviour and creates value for them through upselling and cross-selling.
- He says some customers have a negative lifetime value, such as those who buy only on promotion and return most of what they buy.
- He says adding an AI layer makes continuous one-to-one marketing possible at the cost of mass marketing.
- He says this is strategic marketing, a different way of doing business, and not a tactic.
- He cites a McKinsey Quarterly article from the early 2000s that found most loyalty programmes at the time were failures.
- He says the strategy does not suit every market definition. If your market is a one-time purchase such as buying a house, it will not work.
- He says a reward should be something the customer values deeply and that costs you proportionately little, and the wider that gap the better.
- He calls ordinary loyalty rewards nothing but a delayed promotion.
- His example of a creative reward is a bank's service for non-resident Indians that invited valued customers to a special film screening, built on ego and the wish to network.

### Value has more than one dimension

- He says CRM databases measure only spending value, and that this will not work going forward.
- He contrasts three customers. One spends a lot, one spends little but gives free technical support to others, and one spends nothing but has a large social media following.
- He says a company should understand which value each customer creates and reward each differently, for example coupons for the spender and early access to new products for the supporter.
- He says companies today keep the traditional CRM system and handle the other kinds of value separately, which is inefficient but the best available.

### Competing with low prices

- He says research he co-authored shows a customer management strategy can compete successfully against an everyday low price strategy such as Walmart's.
- He says Walmart serves a whole region and cannot stock everything for everyone, while Kroger studies its customers one to one and shapes store layout, assortment and service for them.
- He says people who want only the cheapest go to the low price store and people who want a better solution come to you.
- He says you cannot be everything to everybody, so be very good at what you are.

## Examples and cases used

- Gupta and co-authors at Columbia: summing customer lifetime values across cohorts to match stock market value.
- Kroger and Walmart: winning high value customers from an everyday low price rival by serving them better.
- A bank's service for non-resident Indians: a film screening as a low cost, highly valued reward.
- Three customer types: the big spender, the unpaid technical helper and the social media influencer.

## Numbers given

- The instructor's retention example: 100 customers, 80 after one period and 40 after two, giving 80%, 50% and 40% overall.
- The instructor's promotion example: 100 rupees net benefit at a 10% response gives an expected 10 rupees.
- The instructor: his team's model was up to 70% more accurate than the earlier standard model.
