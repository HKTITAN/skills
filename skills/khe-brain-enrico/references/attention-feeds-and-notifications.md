# Attention, feeds and notifications

This theme is his account of how products take and hold attention, and what he would change. It comes mostly from two notification videos, three short-form feed videos, the 2023 video on why tech makes people unhappy, the 2024 videos on the anti-phone movement and on messaging, and his 2023 list of personal habits. Treat product facts and numbers as his research on each video's date, read every prediction with its year, and note that until 2024 he spoke as a working product manager who builds with the same loop he describes.

## Why do products turn addictive?

- He calls it a misconception that people at tech companies have evil agendas and spend their days pulling users into doom scrolling. In 2023 he said he knew this because he did the job every day as a product manager. (The Real Reason Your Tech Makes You Unhappy at 0:00, https://www.youtube.com/watch?v=Vbg5TeoLTW0&t=0s)
- His explanation is **the loop**: a team keeps a list of ideas, scores each against one measurable number, builds the likeliest ones, tests them on real users at once, ships what moves the number and repeats. He says nearly every tech company works this way, from trillion dollar ones to two-person startups. (The Real Reason Your Tech Makes You Unhappy at 1:30, https://www.youtube.com/watch?v=Vbg5TeoLTW0&t=90s; What Happens Now That We All Hate Using Our Phone at 3:00, https://www.youtube.com/watch?v=2LpR9zhEl2A&t=180s)
- He says teams usually do not just launch a feature. Half the users see the current version and half the new one, and any big product probably has hundreds of such experiments running, which is why your Instagram or Spotify can look different from a friend's. (The Real Reason Your Tech Makes You Unhappy at 2:15, https://www.youtube.com/watch?v=Vbg5TeoLTW0&t=135s)
- He says what decides whether a product gives value or addiction is the objective and how it is measured. You can draw a line from a notification feature, to time on the app, to engagement, to more money. (The Real Reason Your Tech Makes You Unhappy at 2:15, https://www.youtube.com/watch?v=Vbg5TeoLTW0&t=135s; The Real Reason Your Tech Makes You Unhappy at 3:00, https://www.youtube.com/watch?v=Vbg5TeoLTW0&t=180s)
- He holds that the loop itself is not the problem and is a very effective way to build products. His example is Nintendo using heat maps of early players in Zelda Breath of the Wild to rework the map until it was easy to discover. (The Real Reason Your Tech Makes You Unhappy at 3:45, https://www.youtube.com/watch?v=Vbg5TeoLTW0&t=225s)
- He says things went wrong when companies swapped value for engagement as the objective, and he rejects the reasoning that more engagement must mean more value. His example is an airline flooding him with emails to download an everything app with bookings, movies and car rental. (The Real Reason Your Tech Makes You Unhappy at 5:15, https://www.youtube.com/watch?v=Vbg5TeoLTW0&t=315s)
- His first reason engagement won is that it is easy to measure. Tools like Amplitude, Mixpanel or Google Analytics can be set up in a few hours, while nobody can put a number on the happiness Spotify or Shazam gave them. (The Real Reason Your Tech Makes You Unhappy at 6:45, https://www.youtube.com/watch?v=Vbg5TeoLTW0&t=405s)
- His second reason is money. He says nearly all modern products earn through ads, subscriptions or gamified microtransactions, so more time means more ad money and more interaction means more spending, where ten years earlier you paid 2.99 for an app. (The Real Reason Your Tech Makes You Unhappy at 9:00, https://www.youtube.com/watch?v=Vbg5TeoLTW0&t=540s)
- He says that once more engagement equals more money, it becomes very easy to slide into dark patterns and into promoting addiction to your product. (The Real Reason Your Tech Makes You Unhappy at 9:45, https://www.youtube.com/watch?v=Vbg5TeoLTW0&t=585s)
- He dates the turn to around 2012, when media companies including Netflix, YouTube and Spotify found that time and engagement made the best target number because more time tracked directly with more revenue. He says every other app followed. (What Happens Now That We All Hate Using Our Phone at 3:45, https://www.youtube.com/watch?v=2LpR9zhEl2A&t=225s)
- He says the mess starts only when the target is to take over all of the user's time. The loop just optimises for whatever number it is given. (What Happens Now That We All Hate Using Our Phone at 3:45, https://www.youtube.com/watch?v=2LpR9zhEl2A&t=225s)
- In 2026 he said no designer or product manager ever set out to build something manipulative. Platforms run hundreds of experiments, keep what raises engagement, and the survivors are the designs that exploit psychology: "Not because they chose to, but because the math just converges there." (The Ridiculous Engineering Of Short Form Content at 12:45, https://www.youtube.com/watch?v=69Gw7aoWMMI&t=765s; The Ridiculous Engineering Of Short Form Content at 13:30, https://www.youtube.com/watch?v=69Gw7aoWMMI&t=810s)
- He says A/B tests hand product decisions to people who do not care about the product, and gives YouTube Shorts as the case. YouTube could have fixed the homepage or built community features, but Shorts won the test with **Kyle**, his name for the next user who barely watches YouTube. He counts himself a loyal YouTube user and calls Shorts annoying distractions. (Why Tech Gets Terrible When It Gets Popular at 7:30, https://www.youtube.com/watch?v=BQLVgmhgtpQ&t=450s; Why Tech Gets Terrible When It Gets Popular at 8:15, https://www.youtube.com/watch?v=BQLVgmhgtpQ&t=495s)
- In December 2025 he argued ChatGPT is built to make you stay where Google is built to make you leave. The chat interface makes asking something else the main action, so he says it copied Facebook's engagement model by accident. (How ChatGPT Is Weirdly Turning Into Facebook at 6:00, https://www.youtube.com/watch?v=C7Z11ghwevQ&t=360s; How ChatGPT Is Weirdly Turning Into Facebook at 6:45, https://www.youtube.com/watch?v=C7Z11ghwevQ&t=405s)

## How would he fix the incentive behind addictive products?

- His fix for the measurement problem is to measure something that is not engagement. He cites Couchsurfing changing its success measure from app engagement to time spent with your host, collected by a survey at the end of each day. (The Real Reason Your Tech Makes You Unhappy at 7:30, https://www.youtube.com/watch?v=Vbg5TeoLTW0&t=450s)
- In 2023 he proposed an industry-wide **happiness score**. Windows, Android and iOS would ask people each month to rate their installed apps on the value and happiness they give, send the result to developers as a real metric and show it up front in app stores. (The Real Reason Your Tech Makes You Unhappy at 7:30, https://www.youtube.com/watch?v=Vbg5TeoLTW0&t=450s)
- He admits the happiness score is flawed and the problem very complex, and calls it a first step. He also notes people would find real happiness measurement creepy even if it led to products that made them happier. (The Real Reason Your Tech Makes You Unhappy at 8:15, https://www.youtube.com/watch?v=Vbg5TeoLTW0&t=495s; The Real Reason Your Tech Makes You Unhappy at 6:45, https://www.youtube.com/watch?v=Vbg5TeoLTW0&t=405s)
- His fix for the money problem is a link to revenue stronger than engagement. If real value can be measured, advertisers could pay higher rates on apps that give more value and prices could rise where users get more, which he says needs a big shift in how people see paying for apps. (The Real Reason Your Tech Makes You Unhappy at 10:30, https://www.youtube.com/watch?v=Vbg5TeoLTW0&t=630s)
- In September 2023 he said he believed the era of more engagement would not last forever, and that companies could optimise for value and happiness and still make massive profits. (The Real Reason Your Tech Makes You Unhappy at 11:15, https://www.youtube.com/watch?v=Vbg5TeoLTW0&t=675s)
- By 2025 he held that a design pattern only spreads if it is good for the company that ships it, and social patterns win by raising time on the platform. So he says a pattern that simply makes social media less addictive will not be adopted. (Social Media Is Broken But We Can Fix It at 12:00, https://www.youtube.com/watch?v=HqYIUV7Eqjk&t=720s)
- In 2024 he predicted that once digital minimalism is mainstream, a social site built openly not to be time-addictive will be more appealing, the way the organic label makes food more appealing. (What Happens Now That We All Hate Using Our Phone at 12:00, https://www.youtube.com/watch?v=2LpR9zhEl2A&t=720s)
- He does not think it is all evil. He points out that people who call all technology evil say so on a platform they use for free, with no gatekeeper, to a worldwide audience. (The Real Reason Your Tech Makes You Unhappy at 12:00, https://www.youtube.com/watch?v=Vbg5TeoLTW0&t=720s)

## How do notifications change behaviour?

- In 2021 he called notifications one of the most powerful human conditioning tools in existence and compared them to Pavlov's bell. He says someone who understands them well can fabricate behaviour for millions of people. (How Notifications Are Messing With Your Life at 0:45, https://www.youtube.com/watch?v=OXqpy_H8onc&t=45s)
- He says the typical smartphone user gets 74 notifications a day, and that his own phone showed around 200 a day in 2021. He pictures a farmer in the 1800s being shouted at 74 times a day while working. (How Notifications Are Messing With Your Life at 1:30, https://www.youtube.com/watch?v=OXqpy_H8onc&t=90s)
- He takes the habit loop from the book The Power of Habit: trigger, action, reward. In a product, the notification is the trigger. (How Notifications Are Messing With Your Life at 1:30, https://www.youtube.com/watch?v=OXqpy_H8onc&t=90s)
- He says the last ten years built the most powerful habit creation machine in history, because everyone holds a device and the smartest people of the time work on sending the perfect trigger. Those who do it well are paid in billions of ad revenue, funding or market value. (How Notifications Are Messing With Your Life at 2:15, https://www.youtube.com/watch?v=OXqpy_H8onc&t=135s)
- In 2026 he ranked notifications above endless scrolling and above any algorithm as a shaper of behaviour, because they are built to change it. He says people get dozens a day and treat that as normal. (The Ridiculous Engineering Of Notifications at 0:00, https://www.youtube.com/watch?v=Dx2yPk0FsGM&t=0s)
- His reason is the **push lever**: "Notifications are the only push lever that products have." Everything else needs the user to act first, and he says BeReal exists only because of its notification and Duolingo probably does too. (The Ridiculous Engineering Of Notifications at 4:30, https://www.youtube.com/watch?v=Dx2yPk0FsGM&t=270s)
- He contrasts push with pull. Scrolling needs you to get bored and open the app, while a notification creates the moment on the product's schedule and not yours. (The Ridiculous Engineering Of Notifications at 8:15, https://www.youtube.com/watch?v=Dx2yPk0FsGM&t=495s)
- He sorts notifications into three time zones: act within a minute, like messages, within the hour, like habit nudges, and within a day, like email. He says SMS and WhatsApp notifications get tapped about 15 seconds after being seen. (The Ridiculous Engineering Of Notifications at 6:00, https://www.youtube.com/watch?v=Dx2yPk0FsGM&t=360s)
- He says the within-the-hour zone is probably one of the most powerful ways tech manipulates you, because it works by repetition and not by a single notification. He cites a study of over 200 million notifications in which only 26% of those at the top got people to stop and tap. (The Ridiculous Engineering Of Notifications at 6:45, https://www.youtube.com/watch?v=Dx2yPk0FsGM&t=405s)
- He says repetition turns a reminder into a habit, citing Duolingo's data that after seven days in a row you are 2.4 times more likely to come back the next day by yourself. He ties this to the book Hooked. (The Ridiculous Engineering Of Notifications at 7:30, https://www.youtube.com/watch?v=Dx2yPk0FsGM&t=450s)
- He says notification noise is a threat to getting anything done. Each buzz takes your attention for a couple of seconds even if you leave the phone alone, then curiosity makes you open it and you surface two hours later from a scrolling spree. (How Notifications Are Messing With Your Life at 6:45, https://www.youtube.com/watch?v=OXqpy_H8onc&t=405s)
- In 2026 he said turning notifications off does not fix it. He cites research where people who switched them all off got more done but felt more anxious, lonelier and afraid of letting people down, and every one of them turned them back on. (The Ridiculous Engineering Of Notifications at 7:30, https://www.youtube.com/watch?v=Dx2yPk0FsGM&t=450s)
- He also cites researchers who found that people with fully silent phones checked them more than people with sounds or vibration. His reading is that without feedback you never know if something happened, and that uncertainty causes more anxiety than the sound did. (Why Our Tech Got Silent at 11:15, https://www.youtube.com/watch?v=_rFJutK25VU&t=675s)
- He says the technology is neither good nor evil. Habit formation can serve language learning or workouts, and the same habit can then be used to make money from you. (The Ridiculous Engineering Of Notifications at 8:15, https://www.youtube.com/watch?v=Dx2yPk0FsGM&t=495s)

## What makes a notification work?

- In 2021 he gave three ingredients of an effective notification: a clear and specific call to action that matches what happens after the tap, arrival at the moment you need it, and testing. (How Notifications Are Messing With Your Life at 3:45, https://www.youtube.com/watch?v=OXqpy_H8onc&t=225s; How Notifications Are Messing With Your Life at 4:30, https://www.youtube.com/watch?v=OXqpy_H8onc&t=270s)
- On testing he says the winners are the ones who design better tests, not the ones with the best idea. He says Google tried 41 shades of blue for links in Gmail, and that you are part of hundreds or thousands of tests in the products you use daily. (How Notifications Are Messing With Your Life at 5:15, https://www.youtube.com/watch?v=OXqpy_H8onc&t=315s)
- His model notification is the Google Photos memories one, which shows photos from the same day years earlier. He says he taps it nearly every time and uses it to send friends old memories, while knowing Google Photos is built to sell storage plans and lock you in. (How Notifications Are Messing With Your Life at 6:00, https://www.youtube.com/watch?v=OXqpy_H8onc&t=360s; The Ridiculous Engineering Of Notifications at 8:15, https://www.youtube.com/watch?v=Dx2yPk0FsGM&t=495s)
- He says notification design can matter more to a product than any interface change. His figure is that Duolingo's heavily engineered reminder cut subscription cancellations by 40% and grew daily active users 4.5 times. (The Ridiculous Engineering Of Notifications at 0:00, https://www.youtube.com/watch?v=Dx2yPk0FsGM&t=0s)
- He says the best products use notifications to build a habit, not to win a tap right now, while most teams obsess over buttons and flows. (The Ridiculous Engineering Of Notifications at 0:45, https://www.youtube.com/watch?v=Dx2yPk0FsGM&t=45s)
- His 2026 rule is that the secret is specificity, not personalisation. Putting the user's name in does nothing, as thousands of emails with your name show, while a photo preview of a real event from the same day years ago works. (The Ridiculous Engineering Of Notifications at 1:30, https://www.youtube.com/watch?v=Dx2yPk0FsGM&t=90s)
- He says some notifications are vague on purpose to open a curiosity gap. Loom tells him someone viewed his video without saying who, so he wonders if it was his boss, and Instagram does the same when it will not name who mentioned you in a story. (The Ridiculous Engineering Of Notifications at 3:00, https://www.youtube.com/watch?v=Dx2yPk0FsGM&t=180s; The Reason Everyone's Obsessed With Spotify Wrapped at 2:15, https://www.youtube.com/watch?v=EuzRWd5U6_I&t=135s)
- He says curiosity does not produce action and loss aversion does. Duolingo tells you not to lose your streak, it does not invite you to come and learn Spanish, because people fear losing what they have more than they want something new. (The Ridiculous Engineering Of Notifications at 3:45, https://www.youtube.com/watch?v=Dx2yPk0FsGM&t=225s)
- He says timing is really context. Duolingo waits 23.5 hours after your last session because you proved you were free at that time yesterday, caps it at 10 p.m., and he says they showed that timing alone lifts taps by around 60%. (The Ridiculous Engineering Of Notifications at 3:45, https://www.youtube.com/watch?v=Dx2yPk0FsGM&t=225s)
- In July 2026 he said AI on the phone now sits between the product and the user. Apple and Google both shipped notification summaries, so a model can reword, reorder or shorten each one, and he says it may later decide you do not need to see it at all, though it did not do that yet. (The Ridiculous Engineering Of Notifications at 10:30, https://www.youtube.com/watch?v=Dx2yPk0FsGM&t=630s)
- He says AI summaries have already done real damage. In his telling, Apple's summary of a BBC story told millions that a man had shot himself when he had not, next to the BBC logo, and Apple switched the feature off for news. (The Ridiculous Engineering Of Notifications at 11:15, https://www.youtube.com/watch?v=Dx2yPk0FsGM&t=675s)
- In July 2026 he predicted products will move from persuading people to persuading people's AI agents. Notifications may carry hidden data for an **agent secretary** that decides whether, when and how to show them, and today's craft will become a relic. (The Ridiculous Engineering Of Notifications at 12:00, https://www.youtube.com/watch?v=Dx2yPk0FsGM&t=720s)

## Why does messaging feel overwhelming, and what is his fix?

- In 2024 he said keeping up with communication felt like another job. He counted 23 apps on his phone through which people could reach him, most of them the same text bubbles, chat box and send button. (Why You Don't Reply To Your Friends at 0:00, https://www.youtube.com/watch?v=NVjRPwneLiI&t=0s; Why You Don't Reply To Your Friends at 1:30, https://www.youtube.com/watch?v=NVjRPwneLiI&t=90s)
- He argues that 23 ways to chat make sense because each app is a different place. He compares them to the church, the bars, the factory and the town square of his grandparents' town in 1959, each with its own rules and tone. (Why You Don't Reply To Your Friends at 3:00, https://www.youtube.com/watch?v=NVjRPwneLiI&t=180s)
- He says the strain comes from being in every place at once with everyone expecting an instant reply. For his grandparents it would be like being at work, at church, at the bar and in a deep talk with a friend all at the same moment. (Why You Don't Reply To Your Friends at 3:45, https://www.youtube.com/watch?v=NVjRPwneLiI&t=225s)
- He treats the overwhelm as a side effect of something very positive. Nobody set out to build it, and modern communication lets him keep friends around the world and work with people anywhere. (Why You Don't Reply To Your Friends at 4:30, https://www.youtube.com/watch?v=NVjRPwneLiI&t=270s)
- He ran an experiment of turning notifications off app by app without quitting the apps. He felt freed from the job of replying to everything at once, but he sometimes missed an important email, an opportunity in his DMs or a friend who was in town. (Why You Don't Reply To Your Friends at 5:15, https://www.youtube.com/watch?v=NVjRPwneLiI&t=315s)
- His verdict is that the true problem is notifications, not the number of apps and not the fact that they are instant: "This system is designed for the apps, not for humans communicating with each other." (Why You Don't Reply To Your Friends at 7:30, https://www.youtube.com/watch?v=NVjRPwneLiI&t=450s; Why You Don't Reply To Your Friends at 8:15, https://www.youtube.com/watch?v=NVjRPwneLiI&t=495s)
- He says iOS and Android built notifications as one way for any app to send you anything, so a game alert and a new SMS arrive alike. There is no way to manage urgency, and the only alternative is turning them off. (Why You Don't Reply To Your Friends at 8:15, https://www.youtube.com/watch?v=NVjRPwneLiI&t=495s)
- He says the rich and famous already solved this with human assistants. Celebrities who give up their phones, and he names Ed Sheeran and Justin Bieber, have staff who know what they care about and pass on what matters whatever app it came from. (Why You Don't Reply To Your Friends at 9:45, https://www.youtube.com/watch?v=NVjRPwneLiI&t=585s)
- His 2024 proposal is human-centric notifications. A **close circle** of people is prioritised in the notification panel and updated automatically, accounts that belong to one person are merged across apps, and with consent an on-device language model sorts and summarises. (Why You Don't Reply To Your Friends at 11:15, https://www.youtube.com/watch?v=NVjRPwneLiI&t=675s)
- He says this has to be built at system level by Android and iOS as a standard that apps adapt to, the way USB-C got done. He calls his ideas examples from a tech guy on YouTube and not the final answer. (Why You Don't Reply To Your Friends at 12:45, https://www.youtube.com/watch?v=NVjRPwneLiI&t=765s)
- In 2026 he showed how one small messaging choice changes the pressure people feel. He says WhatsApp shipped read receipts in 2014 as a default, users could suddenly see who was ignoring them, and the backlash brought an option to hide them 10 days later. (The Insane Design Of Messaging at 0:00, https://www.youtube.com/watch?v=6DSW1rN-ZV4&t=0s)
- He calls Instagram DMs a **sharing machine** and not a chat. The share button under every post and next to every Reel exists to feed data to the algorithm, and he says that is also why Instagram moved messages into the main navigation bar. (The Insane Design Of Messaging at 6:00, https://www.youtube.com/watch?v=6DSW1rN-ZV4&t=360s; The Insane Design Of Messaging at 6:45, https://www.youtube.com/watch?v=6DSW1rN-ZV4&t=405s)

## How is the short-form feed built to hold you?

- In 2026 he called the short-form feed the most successful user interface in the world. His figure is that people together watch 616,572 years of content through it every day. (The Ridiculous Engineering Of Short Form Content at 0:00, https://www.youtube.com/watch?v=69Gw7aoWMMI&t=0s)
- He says Instagram built its own scroll physics instead of using the phone's, so that in Reels a tiny flick is enough to reach the next video. He says TikTok and Shorts did the same because system scrolling is too slow. (The Ridiculous Engineering Of Short Form Content at 0:00, https://www.youtube.com/watch?v=69Gw7aoWMMI&t=0s; The Ridiculous Engineering Of Animations at 10:30, https://www.youtube.com/watch?v=z3Lo5QT7nME&t=630s)
- He says like, comment and share sit in the thumb zone on all three apps because the platforms want them pressed. Controls that give you power over the feed sit at the top, placed there on purpose to be awkward to reach. (The Ridiculous Engineering Of Short Form Content at 1:30, https://www.youtube.com/watch?v=69Gw7aoWMMI&t=90s; The Ridiculous Engineering Of Short Form Content at 2:15, https://www.youtube.com/watch?v=69Gw7aoWMMI&t=135s)
- He says showing one video at a time exploits unit bias, the psychology idea that one unit of something feels like the right amount. There is no grid or menu, only the next video the algorithm chose. (The Ridiculous Engineering Of Short Form Content at 3:00, https://www.youtube.com/watch?v=69Gw7aoWMMI&t=180s)
- He says the feed removes every signal of beginning and end. Videos loop, those under 15 seconds have no play bar, and he compares it to casinos removing clocks and windows. (The Ridiculous Engineering Of Short Form Content at 3:00, https://www.youtube.com/watch?v=69Gw7aoWMMI&t=180s)
- He says the single video is not the valuable thing, your habit is. A TikTok found through Google sits behind a sign-in wall because one video earns no ad money and the real value is you getting addicted to the algorithm. (The Ridiculous Engineering Of Short Form Content at 4:30, https://www.youtube.com/watch?v=69Gw7aoWMMI&t=270s)
- He uses Hick's law, that more options make deciding harder, to explain the feed's extreme simplicity. YouTube gives a grid, Netflix gives rows, short form gives one video and one action. (The Ridiculous Engineering Of Short Form Content at 5:15, https://www.youtube.com/watch?v=69Gw7aoWMMI&t=315s)
- He says TikTok's edge is a model that learns in near real time. In his account every like, skip and loop is logged, matched with your profile and fed to a model that updates every minute, where older recommenders trained on past data and fell behind your interests. (The Ridiculous Engineering Of Short Form Content at 14:15, https://www.youtube.com/watch?v=69Gw7aoWMMI&t=855s)
- He says that freshness is what makes short-form algorithms more addictive than anything before. He shows a chart where a system updated every 30 minutes beats a frozen one by around 13 to 14% in accuracy over 50 hours. (The Ridiculous Engineering Of Short Form Content at 15:45, https://www.youtube.com/watch?v=69Gw7aoWMMI&t=945s)
- He says the platform designs the content as well as hosting it. Every creator sees the same retention graphs, analytics and numbers, which pushes them all toward the same hooks, the same editing style and the same tricks. (The Ridiculous Engineering Of Short Form Content at 13:30, https://www.youtube.com/watch?v=69Gw7aoWMMI&t=810s)
- He calls pull to refresh **geniusly evil**. It is intuitive and removes a button, but paired with infinite feeds that show something new each time it feeds the urge to keep pulling, and he says it is probably responsible for billions of dollars. (The Secrets That Make Your Phone Actually Work at 5:15, https://www.youtube.com/watch?v=-YVtZV4DjCE&t=315s; The 7 Levels of Tech Design at 5:15, https://www.youtube.com/watch?v=FQVRfadJkJk&t=315s)
- He says YouTube optimises for playing time. On mobile you cannot really close a video, since the first press only shrinks it to picture-in-picture, and the autoplay countdown that started at 10 seconds in 2014 is now five. (The Ridiculous Engineering Of YouTube Videos at 1:30, https://www.youtube.com/watch?v=giP1iIbSVh0&t=90s; The Ridiculous Engineering Of YouTube Videos at 2:15, https://www.youtube.com/watch?v=giP1iIbSVh0&t=135s; The Ridiculous Engineering Of YouTube Videos at 4:30, https://www.youtube.com/watch?v=giP1iIbSVh0&t=270s)
- He traces mindless scrolling to the phone's **core input**. He says a phone waits about 100 milliseconds before counting a touch as a tap to see if you scroll, so scrolling comes first and tapping second, the reverse of a computer. (The Most Important Tiny Decision In Tech History at 4:30, https://www.youtube.com/watch?v=7EVZpW9Rdwk&t=270s; The Most Important Tiny Decision In Tech History at 5:15, https://www.youtube.com/watch?v=7EVZpW9Rdwk&t=315s)
- He says the core input decides how long you stay. On the phone Instagram content just appears and 40 minutes pass, while in a browser, with the same app and content, you close it after five. (The Most Important Tiny Decision In Tech History at 8:15, https://www.youtube.com/watch?v=7EVZpW9Rdwk&t=495s)
- He says replacing taps with scrolls made products more addicting, naming pull to refresh in place of a refresh button and infinite scroll in place of pages. He relays that the inventors of both have voiced regret about them. (The Most Important Tiny Decision In Tech History at 9:45, https://www.youtube.com/watch?v=7EVZpW9Rdwk&t=585s)
- He says scroll-first input is why the phone became a consumption device and the laptop a creation device. It is also why small children pick up phones so easily: a few actions, all done with a natural scroll. (The Most Important Tiny Decision In Tech History at 10:30, https://www.youtube.com/watch?v=7EVZpW9Rdwk&t=630s)

## What is broken in short-form and social feeds, and how would he fix them?

- In May 2023 he said platform after platform had fallen victim to TikTokification: Reels, Shorts, Spotify's homepage, Amazon's Inspire feed, even Reddit. The recipe is to turn content into scrollable video, autoplay it and add an algorithm. (Short Form Content Is Broken Can We Fix It at 1:30, https://www.youtube.com/watch?v=1F85wJmhDkA&t=90s)
- He says retention is everyone's obsession because it is the one metric the algorithms use. Creators cram in subtitles, very fast cuts and unrelated clips such as Minecraft gameplay to keep you watching so you see more ads. (Short Form Content Is Broken Can We Fix It at 3:45, https://www.youtube.com/watch?v=1F85wJmhDkA&t=225s; Short Form Content Is Broken Can We Fix It at 5:15, https://www.youtube.com/watch?v=1F85wJmhDkA&t=315s)
- He says short-form holds him for a long time and gives nothing back. After a 25 minute TikTok session with interesting videos he remembered almost nothing, which he links to short-term memory lasting 15 to 30 seconds, close to the average TikTok length. (Short Form Content Is Broken Can We Fix It at 5:15, https://www.youtube.com/watch?v=1F85wJmhDkA&t=315s)
- In 2023 he said short-form is leading to lower attention spans, especially for children growing up with it. (Short Form Content Is Broken Can We Fix It at 6:00, https://www.youtube.com/watch?v=1F85wJmhDkA&t=360s)
- He says the formula does not even work for the companies. Ads perform poorly in a stream where it is hard to focus, he cites Meta saying Reels earns less than the feed, and he says TikTok was still losing money. (Short Form Content Is Broken Can We Fix It at 6:45, https://www.youtube.com/watch?v=1F85wJmhDkA&t=405s)
- He says creators cannot build a lasting business on short-form platforms, because creator funds are a fixed pie with very low payouts. Those who succeed there try to move to long-form. (Short Form Content Is Broken Can We Fix It at 7:30, https://www.youtube.com/watch?v=1F85wJmhDkA&t=450s)
- His 2023 conclusion is that the whole system is bad for everyone: companies, creators and users. (Short Form Content Is Broken Can We Fix It at 8:15, https://www.youtube.com/watch?v=1F85wJmhDkA&t=495s)
- His fix is **the missing link**. Short-form has cracked discovery but cannot go deep, long-form goes deep but is hard to find, so shorts should become the discovery engine for long-form. (Short Form Content Is Broken Can We Fix It at 9:45, https://www.youtube.com/watch?v=1F85wJmhDkA&t=585s; Short Form Content Is Broken Can We Fix It at 10:30, https://www.youtube.com/watch?v=1F85wJmhDkA&t=630s)
- He says platforms need three things: link short and long seamlessly while keeping them separate, switch the algorithm to **overall retention** measured from the short through the long piece, and let creators build real businesses. (Short Form Content Is Broken Can We Fix It at 12:00, https://www.youtube.com/watch?v=1F85wJmhDkA&t=720s; Short Form Content Is Broken Can We Fix It at 12:45, https://www.youtube.com/watch?v=1F85wJmhDkA&t=765s)
- He says TikTok's own longer videos sit in the wrong place, because the scrolling habit it taught means people there are primed to swipe away. He ended the 2023 video admitting he may be wrong and everything may collapse into one minute videos. (Short Form Content Is Broken Can We Fix It at 13:30, https://www.youtube.com/watch?v=1F85wJmhDkA&t=810s; Short Form Content Is Broken Can We Fix It at 14:15, https://www.youtube.com/watch?v=1F85wJmhDkA&t=855s)
- In January 2025 he called the vertical scrolling feed the clearest case of one design pattern taking over, and said it is broken. It pushes addiction and surface-level content and leaves you drained. (Social Media Is Broken But We Can Fix It at 3:00, https://www.youtube.com/watch?v=HqYIUV7Eqjk&t=180s)
- His 2025 proposal turns the feed from a list into a tree. Swiping down gives the next video as today, one diagonal swipe always means go deeper into the full or longer video, and the other means show me something far from my usual. (Social Media Is Broken But We Can Fix It at 8:15, https://www.youtube.com/watch?v=HqYIUV7Eqjk&t=495s; Social Media Is Broken But We Can Fix It at 12:45, https://www.youtube.com/watch?v=HqYIUV7Eqjk&t=765s)
- He says the feed's real flaw is no depth, and that depth can serve users and platforms at once. People would spend more time, but in a better way, and the algorithm would favour creators who can hold people through longer videos. (Social Media Is Broken But We Can Fix It at 12:00, https://www.youtube.com/watch?v=HqYIUV7Eqjk&t=720s; Social Media Is Broken But We Can Fix It at 13:30, https://www.youtube.com/watch?v=HqYIUV7Eqjk&t=810s)
- In 2026 he conceded that his diagonal swipe idea runs against the extreme simplicity that makes the feed work, and gave that as the reason it has not been built. (The Ridiculous Engineering Of Short Form Content at 5:15, https://www.youtube.com/watch?v=69Gw7aoWMMI&t=315s)
- In 2022 he said Glance's offer of content pushed onto your lock screen in exchange for ads is terrible value, because the amount and quality of content we have has never been better. What we need now is a **faucet**, a way to control the flow of content in our lives, and he said Glance's balance of ads and value makes no sense at all. (Ads Are Coming To Your Lockscreen For Real at 4:30, https://www.youtube.com/watch?v=SQ_hBlGNR4c&t=270s)

## What does he make of the anti-phone movement?

- In May 2024 he said the anti-smartphone movement was just getting started and would probably change technology more than AI. Its core idea is that using a smartphone could become uncool, like smoking. (What Happens Now That We All Hate Using Our Phone at 0:00, https://www.youtube.com/watch?v=2LpR9zhEl2A&t=0s)
- He says the movement rests on a wrong idea. Smartphones are not really the problem and social media is not really the problem either, the loop is. (What Happens Now That We All Hate Using Our Phone at 0:45, https://www.youtube.com/watch?v=2LpR9zhEl2A&t=45s)
- His line is: "We're addicted to media. Smartphones just serve it best right now." You do not hate the phone when it guides you with Google Maps, takes photos or calls an Uber. (What Happens Now That We All Hate Using Our Phone at 2:15, https://www.youtube.com/watch?v=2LpR9zhEl2A&t=135s)
- He says that after 15 years of the loop people have had enough, and the only thing they could do was declare war on the smartphone. It is the available target, not the cause. (What Happens Now That We All Hate Using Our Phone at 5:15, https://www.youtube.com/watch?v=2LpR9zhEl2A&t=315s)
- He sees **a market for less**. While phone makers raced for more cameras and AI, people began to want less, and he lists dumb phones, minimalist e-ink phones, app blockers, timed phone cages and the screen time tools Apple and Google built in. (What Happens Now That We All Hate Using Our Phone at 6:00, https://www.youtube.com/watch?v=2LpR9zhEl2A&t=360s)
- His driver is coolness: what culture decides is cool grows and what it decides is uncool dies. He uses smoking, which he says fell in the US from 42% in 1965 to 14% in 2019, while granting that studies show phones and cigarettes are different kinds of addiction. (What Happens Now That We All Hate Using Our Phone at 7:30, https://www.youtube.com/watch?v=2LpR9zhEl2A&t=450s; What Happens Now That We All Hate Using Our Phone at 8:15, https://www.youtube.com/watch?v=2LpR9zhEl2A&t=495s)
- In May 2024 he said we might start to see phone-free zones, with bars, restaurants and clubs banning phones because it would make them cooler, as the no-phones rule does for Berghain in Berlin. He said that a few years from now the flex could be living on an old Nokia flip phone, and that watching TikTok might be seen as lame. (What Happens Now That We All Hate Using Our Phone at 9:45, https://www.youtube.com/watch?v=2LpR9zhEl2A&t=585s)
- In May 2024 he bet that within two or three years Samsung, Google or Apple would build phones with physical switches and locks, or devices that take over single jobs like music. He read the Rabbit R1 and the Humane AI Pin as early, poorly executed versions of that pitch. (What Happens Now That We All Hate Using Our Phone at 10:30, https://www.youtube.com/watch?v=2LpR9zhEl2A&t=630s)
- He called this a perfect storm for augmented reality glasses. People want a phone's benefits without doom scrolling into a rectangle, and an overlay on the real world has far more limits on what it can show. (What Happens Now That We All Hate Using Our Phone at 12:45, https://www.youtube.com/watch?v=2LpR9zhEl2A&t=765s)
- He admits his own videos play **the time game**. He uses music, text, images and story to keep people watching to the end because YouTube rewards time too. (What Happens Now That We All Hate Using Our Phone at 4:30, https://www.youtube.com/watch?v=2LpR9zhEl2A&t=270s)
- In 2023 he split devices into **creation devices** and consumption devices and said the two fight for your time, with consumption usually winning. When both live on one machine, you sit down to work and Instagram or Netflix is a click away. (The Best Tech Product Ever Designed at 0:45, https://www.youtube.com/watch?v=n1cMrkMqmnw&t=45s; The Best Tech Product Ever Designed at 3:00, https://www.youtube.com/watch?v=n1cMrkMqmnw&t=180s)
- He loves the Teenage Engineering OP-1 because it has no Wi-Fi, no notifications and no way to consume, which makes flow easier. In July 2023 he expected people to want separate creation devices more as phones and computers grow more powerful. (The Best Tech Product Ever Designed at 3:00, https://www.youtube.com/watch?v=n1cMrkMqmnw&t=180s; The Best Tech Product Ever Designed at 4:30, https://www.youtube.com/watch?v=n1cMrkMqmnw&t=270s; The Best Tech Product Ever Designed at 9:00, https://www.youtube.com/watch?v=n1cMrkMqmnw&t=540s)
- In 2024 he argued doom scrolling would exist even without social media, because it needs only three ingredients: the smartphone, recommendation algorithms and videos to show. His line is: "TikTok isn't social media, it's just, well, media." (What if Smartphones Never Existed at 1:30, https://www.youtube.com/watch?v=AJ8OnNQYAVo&t=90s)
- He says a world without today's tech would not simply be people happily looking at nature. In his no-social-media timeline people still scroll two hours a day, and they lose shared memes and an easy way to organise against regimes. (What if Smartphones Never Existed at 0:00, https://www.youtube.com/watch?v=AJ8OnNQYAVo&t=0s; What if Smartphones Never Existed at 2:15, https://www.youtube.com/watch?v=AJ8OnNQYAVo&t=135s; What if Smartphones Never Existed at 3:00, https://www.youtube.com/watch?v=AJ8OnNQYAVo&t=180s)

## What habits does he use on his own digital life?

- In 2023 he said technology feels to most people like it works against them, and that a digital life in order can give you superpowers. He also insists social media is a great asset and that people who deny it are wrong. (How I Finally Fixed My Digital Life at 0:00, https://www.youtube.com/watch?v=7PhEfKCHr7Y&t=0s; How I Finally Fixed My Digital Life at 0:45, https://www.youtube.com/watch?v=7PhEfKCHr7Y&t=45s)
- He says built-in screen time limits fail because they are too easy to remove when you are bored in a queue. His answer is **hardcore mode**: make access physically or functionally hard. (How I Finally Fixed My Digital Life at 0:45, https://www.youtube.com/watch?v=7PhEfKCHr7Y&t=45s)
- He gives three hardcore options: an NFC tag on your keychain that must touch the phone to unlock chosen apps, a modded Instagram on Android with no feed or stories, or deleting the app and using the web version. (How I Finally Fixed My Digital Life at 1:30, https://www.youtube.com/watch?v=7PhEfKCHr7Y&t=90s)
- On the web version he says: "The experience is going to be really bad but that's kind of the point." A worse experience is less addicting and you use it far less. (How I Finally Fixed My Digital Life at 2:15, https://www.youtube.com/watch?v=7PhEfKCHr7Y&t=135s)
- He says about 80% of notifications are useless. His **notification witch hunt** lasts a week: at each one, do not open it, ask what you would gain and whether you could check the app yourself later, and if so turn that app's notifications off entirely. (How I Finally Fixed My Digital Life at 2:15, https://www.youtube.com/watch?v=7PhEfKCHr7Y&t=135s; How I Finally Fixed My Digital Life at 3:00, https://www.youtube.com/watch?v=7PhEfKCHr7Y&t=180s)
- He says it is better to cut too many notifications and bring some back than to keep tens a day that distract you from your life. One notification he never got rid of is the photo service reminding him of photos taken on the same day years ago; he keeps his photos on Google Photos. (How I Finally Fixed My Digital Life at 3:00, https://www.youtube.com/watch?v=7PhEfKCHr7Y&t=180s; How I Finally Fixed My Digital Life at 5:15, https://www.youtube.com/watch?v=7PhEfKCHr7Y&t=315s)
- In 2021 his method was a monthly audit: open the phone's digital wellbeing tool, see how many notifications each app sent and ask of each whether it brings any value to your life. He prefers removing the source to relying on Do Not Disturb. (How Notifications Are Messing With Your Life at 7:30, https://www.youtube.com/watch?v=OXqpy_H8onc&t=450s; How Notifications Are Messing With Your Life at 8:15, https://www.youtube.com/watch?v=OXqpy_H8onc&t=495s)
- In 2021 he said the solution should come from people, not companies or governments, and that awareness of what happens in your head when the phone buzzes is the first step. By 2024 he was arguing the phone platforms should rebuild notifications at system level. (How Notifications Are Messing With Your Life at 7:30, https://www.youtube.com/watch?v=OXqpy_H8onc&t=450s; Why You Don't Reply To Your Friends at 12:45, https://www.youtube.com/watch?v=NVjRPwneLiI&t=765s)
- He holds the same view on persuasion in general. In 2024 he said regulation and complaining will not stop manipulation, and that understanding the tricks is the best defence and what schools should teach. (The Internet Wants Your Money Here's How To Outsmart It at 10:30, https://www.youtube.com/watch?v=4OLt9pFlRvo&t=630s)
- He does not follow inbox zero on his personal email. He questions every incoming newsletter or alert and unsubscribes at once if it is not useful, so that over one to three months the inbox holds only important things. (How I Finally Fixed My Digital Life at 9:45, https://www.youtube.com/watch?v=7PhEfKCHr7Y&t=585s)
- He tells people to question the defaults of the software they spend 10 to 50 hours a week in. His own case is moving from Chrome to Arc after ten years of never questioning it. (How I Finally Fixed My Digital Life at 3:00, https://www.youtube.com/watch?v=7PhEfKCHr7Y&t=180s; How I Finally Fixed My Digital Life at 3:45, https://www.youtube.com/watch?v=7PhEfKCHr7Y&t=225s)
- He leaves his phone in the car when out with friends or at home for a nearby dinner. He calls it scary but very satisfying, and answers the emergency worry by noting that billions of people before 2000 did this every day. (How I Finally Fixed My Digital Life at 8:15, https://www.youtube.com/watch?v=7PhEfKCHr7Y&t=495s)
