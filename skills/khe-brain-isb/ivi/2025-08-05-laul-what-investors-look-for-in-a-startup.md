# What investors look for in a startup, Varun Laul

Varun Laul introduced himself as a private equity investor since 2007, a partner at Investcorp focused on growth investments in India, and a founder of a micro VC platform started by ISB alumni. He spent the first third of the session on four questions investors ask, then had founders answer the four questions for their own businesses and commented on how to think about each. This note keeps the teaching and leaves out the individual pitches.

## What was said

### Four questions behind every framework

- Varun Laul says every investor and firm offers its own framework, but fundamentally investors are trying to answer only four questions.
- He lists the four as the size of the addressable and serviceable market, founder market fit, the uniqueness of the value proposition, and the monetisation potential and model.
- He says founders should use the same four questions when designing their own ventures.

### Question 1: the market you can serve

- He says 80 percent of the investments that go wrong get stuck at the first question, because the market being served was not truly estimated.
- He says it is easy for a skincare brand to claim that everyone in India is addressable, and that is not how to think about it.
- He says to slice the market by who can afford the product, then map behaviour and estimate usage, such as whether the product builds a daily regimen.
- He gives the chain as usage into pricing, or volume into pricing, which equals revenue.
- He says even managers and investors in businesses that have run for 10 to 15 years get this first question wrong.
- He says that in his own industry a bottom-up estimate works much better than top-down because it gives a more realistic picture.
- He says quoting everyone who plays a sport, or all the people who eat, is too broad to be a market definition. What matters is what the company will be able to address and service.
- He says market size should be seen through the profit pool and not only the number of users: 25 million users paying 10 paise each does not count for anything.
- He says some ventures have ready market definitions and others need computation and primary work, because an investor will ask how the founder knows customers will pay the stated price.
- He says a small set of customers who have each stated their willingness to pay is the kind of proof that answers that question.

### TAM, SAM and SOM

- His example of a premium food product starts with everyone who eats that kind of food, a market that grows by number of people, frequency and spend.
- He says that if the product is priced above an existing premium brand, about three quarters of that market is not addressable and the remaining quarter is the serviceable market. He stresses these numbers are made up.
- He says the serviceable obtainable market is smaller again, because distribution cannot be set up in all 25 top cities on day one and not every neighbourhood can afford the product.
- He says quick commerce platforms will not list a product everywhere at once, and the product first has to get into their stores.
- On whether the obtainable market should be counted over three or five years, he says not to get caught up in definitions.

### Question 2: founder market fit

- He defines founder market fit as whether the founders have what it takes to deliver on the problem: the experience, the skill set and the knowledge base.
- His example is a procurement platform for restaurants and hotels, where the founder would need to know how to reach them, what they buy and where to source it.
- He says it is sometimes more important to know where a business loses money before learning where it makes money.
- He says founder market fit also includes behavioural attributes needed to make a venture succeed.
- He says investors discount this variable the most, yet it is the one that decides whether a venture succeeds.
- He points to listed banks, where a chief executive's resignation can cut the share price by 10 percent, as a sign of how much the person matters.
- He says an investor first works out what the venture takes and then asks whether this team has it, for example prior experience of selling to enterprises or someone who really understands AI.
- He says owning a patent or having built the technology speaks to the uniqueness question and is not founder market fit.
- He says a startup has a 1 in 100 chance of success and that founders have to be "borderline delusional".

### Question 3: is the value proposition unique

- He says this is the hardest of the four questions to answer.
- He says most founders will not invent something new, but that does not stop them from building a differentiated business model.
- He says Gillette holds one of the strongest consumer monopolies in the world, harder to displace than other large consumer goods companies.
- He says Dollar Shave Club sold the same kind of product and on the surface had no differentiation. Its twist was a subscription model for a routine activity.
- He says that twist gave Dollar Shave Club the chance to reach a billion dollars.
- He says a growing market is not a value proposition, and that a founder who claims nobody else is doing this should expect the investor to assume many others are trying.

### Question 4: monetisation

- He says the fourth question is the business model: how the company will charge, how much it will make and whether that is enough.
- He says the thing to be careful about is the difference between ability to pay and willingness to pay, and that confusing the two is a big mistake.
- He says this mistake shows up very strongly in B2B businesses.

### What investors ask at each stage

- He says angels and syndicates ask only whether these are good founders with founder market fit and whether the problem is large enough.
- He says at the stage he calls pre-Series A, with an offering in place, the questions move to the value proposition and the business model.
- He says investors want validation at every stage, and none of the questions can be answered with "I think so".
- He says validation can be hard data, an example from a different market, or bottom-up consumer behaviour.
- He says a Series A investor looks for enough consumer validation and early signs of monetisation.
- He says at Series B the questions are whether product-market fit is turning into scale and monetisation, what the unit economics are, and whether there is a real profit and loss statement.
- He says going from seed to a Series B, C or D stage takes 5 to 10 years, with the fastest he has seen at five to six years.

### Micro VC, VC and private equity

- He says the simplest way to tell a micro VC from a VC is cheque size, about 500,000 dollars against about 3 million dollars.
- He says 500,000 dollars is what a company needs to develop an offering and 3 million dollars is what it needs to establish product-market fit.
- He says 80 percent of investments in the VC world do not go well, while private equity puts in far more capital per deal and can accept a very different level of losses.
- He calls it a fallacy that needing more capital makes a business a private equity deal and not a VC one. What matters more is the maturity of the business.
- He says an asset-heavy business can be made asset-light by moving the assets into a separate entity financed by asset financiers, and funding the core proposition separately.

### Rigour

- He says execution beats strategy, and founders should bring as much rigour and quantification into their thinking as they can, even if it never goes on a slide.
- He says someone will always ask how a number was reached when a founder pitches to investors.

## Examples and cases used

- Dollar Shave Club against Gillette: a differentiated business model, subscription, in a market with a very strong incumbent.
- A skincare brand in India: the wrong way to size a market is to count everyone.
- A procurement platform for restaurants and hotels: what founder market fit would require.
- A premium food product: how the total market narrows to the serviceable and then the obtainable market.
- Bank chief executives: share prices fall when they resign, showing how much one leader matters.

## Numbers given

- He says his firm manages about 60 billion dollars across 14 countries.
- 80 percent, his share of failed investments that trace back to a poorly estimated market.
- 1 in 100, his odds of a startup succeeding.
- 80 percent, his share of VC investments that do not go well.
- 500,000 dollars and 3 million dollars, his typical micro VC and VC cheque sizes.
- 5 to 10 years, his range for going from seed to Series B or later.

## Not covered

- Valuation, term sheets or how much equity investors take.
- How his own micro VC platform invests. A colleague covered that in a separate presentation the same day.
- A method for testing willingness to pay.
- How to build a pitch deck around the four questions.
