# Process mapping, SIPOC and when to automate

The second half of an operations management class, the fourth session of the course, taught by the course instructor, who is not named in the recording. He uses a coffee-making exercise to teach SIPOC, shows three kinds of process map, then has the class map a bank's account opening process from a handout. The session follows the one on lean operations and comes before the one on Six Sigma.

## What was said

### Customer before process

- The instructor asks the class to make coffee for a guest. They follow the process and the proportions correctly and the guest still dislikes it, because nobody asked what the guest wanted.
- His lesson is that once people see that process is important, they must also tell themselves that the customer is more important than the process.
- He says many organisations with legacy systems get trapped in process.
- The first question in visualising a process, he says, is not about the process. It is who the customer is and what the customer needs.

### SIPOC, starting from the customer

- The second step is the output, which he describes as the result visualised in your mind before it exists.
- Customers may not state the output, he says. A guest may only say they are diabetic or allergic to milk, and it is for you to visualise the black coffee.
- The third step is the process that will turn the visualised output into a real one. He says product development comes in only at this step.
- The fourth step is the inputs each process step needs. Boiling water needs an apparatus and the raw material.
- The last step is the suppliers who will give those inputs in good quality.
- He says quality starts from the inputs. However efficient the process, bad inputs ruin the output.
- He names the tool SIPOC and says it should be worked from the C, the customer.
- He recommends it as a first step whenever a founder designs a new product, service, ecosystem or website.

### Three kinds of process map

- In a flowchart a rectangle stands for transactional work, which he defines as work that can be written down on paper and executed by whoever reads it, with no decision making.
- Work that involves a decision gets a different symbol, the decision box.
- His flowchart example is a bookselling website. The customer enters the site, the system asks whether they are registered, and the flow goes on to sign in or sign up, old or new book, add to cart and payment.
- He says mapping a process is a healthy sign for a business, and he would insist on it even for an expert who has run the same business for 20 years.
- A user flow map differs from a flowchart because it maps only the actions the user performs. He names the tool for this as DILO, a day in the life of a user.
- A flowchart maps every task whoever performs it, so customer actions and system actions are mixed together.
- A swim lane process map is a normal flowchart placed in lanes, which adds accountability for each task.
- He says the lanes show entity ownership and not users. In his example the lanes are internal teams such as marketing.
- He names Microsoft Visio as the cheapest software for drawing these maps. He says workflow software can go further and model the process, so that each team sees only its next task on a dashboard.
- He says consultants do not have a firm's process knowledge and will charge the firm for the time they spend acquiring it.

### What to look for in a map

- The first thing to look at is the number of decision boxes. The more decision boxes, the more complex the process.
- Every decision box can create a non-linear loop, he says. Each time a check fails there is rework, and he calls rework the enemy.
- A process with many decision boxes is not a candidate for plain automation, he says. The patterns in the decisions have to be found first, which leads to a decision support system.
- The second thing to look at is the number of handoffs. More handoffs mean more dependency on other entities to get your work done.
- A process with many handoffs is complex and not yet ready for automation.

### Simplify before you automate

- His rule is to clean and simplify the process first and automate only after that.
- He says candidates for automation should not be chosen on the logic that wherever there is a file, automate it first.
- He says technology investments fail when digital transformation does not follow this sequence, and that he has seen many organisations make this mistake over the last decade.
- He says that if a firm does the right process mapping and puts the right people in the right work, 90 percent of organisational problems can be resolved.
- He calls both of these basic common sense that needs no degree.

### The bank account exercise

- The class maps a savings account opening process from a handout. A customer fills a form at a branch, the branch sends the package to a regional office within 48 hours, the regional office checks documents, a data entry team digitises the form, a quality analyst checks the entry and a regional manager screens the applicant against a blacklist before approving.
- Each check in that process sends work back to an earlier step when it fails.
- He says generating improvement ideas is the leader's obligation, and continuous improvement should not be treated as a bottom-up matter only.
- He says a leader whose team is not contributing ideas should nourish the team, not replace it.
- His method is to draw the process map, invite the team and start a brainstorm by asking why the process has so many decision boxes and so many checks.
- He says asking how to remove a decision box gives the team's innovation a focus.
- The gains he lists from crunching a process are a better customer experience, higher productivity and lower cost.
- He says the starting point for all of this is having the as-is process mapped.

## Examples and cases used

- Making coffee for a guest: a correct process still fails when nobody asked what the customer wanted.
- A bookselling website: a flowchart with transactional steps and decision points.
- A recruitment process: his example of a SIPOC.
- A bank's savings account opening: a process with several checks and handoffs, mapped by the class.

## Numbers given

- 90 percent: the instructor's figure for the share of organisational problems that right process mapping and right job matching can resolve.
- 48 hours: the time the branch takes to send an account opening package to the regional office in the handout.
