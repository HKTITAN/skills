# Digitising rural microfinance, Sasidhar Thumuluri

Sasidhar Thumuluri, who runs SubK, described how a roughly 15 year old microfinance channel business moved its collections, risk assessment and customer engagement from field visits to an app, a risk tool and recorded video calls. He talked through slides and took questions throughout, then spent the last half hour on open questions about regulation, rural markets and customers.

## What was said

### The business and its market

- Thumuluri says SubK has about $100 million of loans outstanding to about 350,000 customers and works in about 20 states.
- He says SubK is a channel partner and not a product maker. Banks are the lenders, and SubK chose this path so that it is not heavily regulated and stays free to innovate.
- He says most new businesses do not invent a new product. They deliver an existing product in a different way or to a different market segment.
- He says he applied for a personal loan at three banks that claim digital lending platforms and could not get a loan in ten minutes on a phone. He calls quick digital credit from banks a myth, while fintechs can do it using data they already hold on the customer.
- He explains why banks leave small loans to channels: a branch officer costs the bank the same whether he delivers a 50,000 rupee loan to a rural woman or a 50 lakh rupee loan to a business, so the bank prefers the larger loan.
- He says rural and low income borrowers borrow to repay, not to default, and that social standing with neighbours matters a lot to them. SubK lends in groups so that members watch over each other.
- He gives the current default rate as about 2 to 3%, and says it comes at a high cost: customers pay about 24.5% interest because of the field effort involved.
- He says the microfinance product is only for women, because women in villages already come together in informal rotating savings groups, and microfinance was built on that habit of trusting each other with money.
- He says the minimum loan is now 50,000 rupees and the usual range is 50,000 to 1 lakh. It was 10,000 when he started, and that size no longer works economically.
- He says SubK does not form the groups. Its officers go to a village or street, say they offer group loans, and borrowers bring their own people. Instant credit checks then decide who qualifies, and a group needs at least three or four people.
- He says SubK prefers to lend to people who are already running a business, not to someone who plans to start one, because relying on training to turn into income is very risky.
- He says kirana shops and agriculture are still the most common uses of the loans.

### Change management

- Thumuluri says the hard part of going digital was not customer behaviour. It was the behaviour of the people who deliver the service, his own staff.
- Before starting, SubK tested its belief that customers were ready by studying how many had smartphones at home, how many used WhatsApp, and how many used UPI.
- He lists who pushed back: partner banks that did not believe it was possible, staff who believed a rural woman could not use a phone, and investors who asked why he would challenge something that was already working.
- His answer to that pushback is that without the change a company can scale fast but will not go far. He compares it to running a sprint and not a marathon.
- He says a collection officer's job changed from collecting cash to getting customers to download the app and pay online. Once the officer changes, the officer's customers slowly follow.
- His lesson is that the channel is what you have to work on, not only the end customer.
- He tells early stage founders that they will hire experienced people who come from the old way of working, and that making them understand the new way is an investment the company has to make.

### Choosing what to digitise

- He says SubK broke its lending workflow into six steps: sourcing, enrolment, assessment, documentation, disbursement and repayment. He tells founders to break their own workflow into steps in the same way.
- He says SubK did not change the process. It changed the way the process is followed.
- He says you can digitise everything, but that does not make it a good investment, so SubK looked for where the real value was.
- SubK picked three interventions in order: repayments, then risk assessment, then continuous engagement with the customer. He says you cannot innovate everything at once and should double down on one or two really important things.
- He says repayments came first because SubK was collecting physical cash every month by travelling to each village.
- He says SubK had planned to start with sourcing, but when COVID stopped field visits it repurposed the app it had ready and launched it for collections.
- He says some technologies turn into commodities. His examples are eKYC, OCR and e-signatures, which once felt like innovation and now carry almost no value, so a company should choose carefully where it spends its time.

### The payments app and why not a QR code

- Thumuluri says SubK chose not to use QR codes for repayment even though they are the easiest option for customers.
- His reason is that a QR code is only a payment solution. It would not let SubK source loans, do appraisals or engage with customers, so only half the vision would be met.
- He says the one hard step is getting the app onto the phone. After that the app has to be as easy as a QR code.
- He says first year adoption of the app was only 10%, and it took two and a half to three years, until 2023, for more than 50% of customers to pay directly through it.
- He says the app is now on about its 50th iteration after four to five years.
- He says the app shows a borrower the other members of her group as well, because these are group loans.

### The risk tool

- He says the risk tool uses four kinds of data: macro data, data on the customer's geography, the customer's financial behaviour, and the channel, meaning the field officer.
- He says the field officer's profile is part of the risk model because the first assessment is still done by a person, and who that person is affects the quality of the loan. Education and years in the business are among the officer variables.
- He says SubK uses satellite night light data as a sign of how developed an area is. A customer sitting among dark spots carries seasonal risk because the area may have many migrants.
- He says the tool was built in collaboration with ISB.
- He says the tool does not make the decision. It sorts customers into colour bands so SubK knows where to spend field effort.
- He gives 80% of customers as falling in the green band, and they get only a remote appraisal. The other 20% fall in yellow, orange and red.
- He says the bank makes the final lending decision, because regulation does not allow KYC and underwriting to be passed to an outsourced party.
- He says 95% of the cases SubK sends to banks are approved, and that a rejected case means wasted work and higher cost.

### Video appraisals and AI

- He says SubK built a video interface into the app. The customer shows her house, shop and assets to an officer who sits elsewhere, after giving consent at the start.
- He says customers accept the video call because they do not want to wait at home for a field visit that may not arrive.
- He says the calls are live and recorded, so an auditor, trainer or supervisor can join or review them later.
- He says getting a video module to work in villages on basic phones and low bandwidth took 18 months of trials across many providers.
- He says SubK works with Google, through its accelerator, to analyse the videos with audio analysis, computer vision and sentiment analysis, across 22 parameters.
- He gives 95% for face match between the video and the photograph on file. The system also spots assets such as a television, refrigerator or motorcycle, which he reads as a sign the customer is stable and not about to migrate.
- He says the system flags a third person who is visible or heard prompting the customer.
- He says analysing a video costs roughly a dollar, which he calls high for this business, and he expects the price to fall once volume gives him room to negotiate.
- He says the video work is still at proof of concept stage and is being scaled up.
- His general point for founders is that you do not have to change radically how a business is done. You can keep the existing process and see what more you can get out of it with available technology.

### Results and measuring them

- He says customers who pay digitally show less than half the default risk of customers who pay in cash, on both 30 day and 90 day measures.
- He reads this as stickiness: customers behave better in an environment where their good behaviour is recorded and helps them later.
- He says staff who moved their customers to digital manage at least 20 to 30% more customers than staff who did not.
- He says video appraisals show at least a 1% difference in favour of video over physical visits, which he values at about a million dollars on his $100 million book.
- He says SubK started with a hypothesis of higher productivity, quicker turnaround and lower cost and risk, and that founders should keep checking such parameters as data builds up.
- He says a startup has no counterfactual of its own, so it should compare itself with industry metrics. "Otherwise how do you know you are winning the game?"

### How ideas are generated inside the company

- He says SubK set up a separate strategic initiatives team and four or five cross functional teams, one for each workflow, with people from business, marketing and finance.
- He says a customer facing person was always in each team, and that SubK sent behavioural scientists to talk to customers.
- He says the technology team then judges feasibility and cost, and management makes the call.
- He says SubK also started and stopped several initiatives. One, an individual lending journey, was dropped after two years.
- He says the innovation began with individual loans and moved to group loans because lenders were more comfortable with groups.

### External shocks and regulators

- He says every business will face external shocks it did not anticipate. His example is the 2010 Andhra Pradesh government action on microfinance, after which the industry took up responsible lending, codes of conduct and fair lending practices.
- He describes a case in Karnataka where 30,000 to 40,000 borrowers tied to one community association defaulted together, and the state then passed a microfinance law that restricted how and when lenders can collect.
- He says digital lending guidelines arrived in 2023 while SubK was mid way through its changes. SubK was little affected because it had started with collections and still uses people on the ground, so it does not fall under the definition of a digital lender.
- He says SubK is regulated indirectly as a business correspondent. Banks must ensure it follows the rules, the bank can penalise SubK, and the RBI can penalise the bank.
- He says self regulation, where the regulator gives an industry body the job of finding faults, is likely to come to almost all industries.
- He says a regulator always follows innovation and is never ahead of it, and that when a few players misbehave the regulator often restricts the whole area of work.
- He says an action against a large payments company can hit every business that routes payments through it. His example is Razorpay, which his own payments went through.
- He advises founders to be part of their industry body. He says he wants his competitors to survive too, because only an industry with a common voice can influence an authority.
- He says senior officials generally listen, and that the real difficulty in rural work is at the level of local police stations and panchayat offices.

### Working in rural markets

- Asked how hard small towns and villages are, he says there is no generic answer. It depends on how new the product is, how much demand there is and how trust works for it.
- He advises founders to spend one to two months living among the customer segment they serve before building the product. He calls it the hardest way to learn and the best.
- He says younger rural customers are more aspirational, mobile and digital, older customers are comfortable with cash and physical interaction, and SubK is preparing for the younger generation.
- He says SubK deals with the families of customers and not only the customer, because most of its products are household products.
- He says SubK also runs an agent network: existing shops are equipped to offer basic banking such as cash withdrawal, and the shopkeeper is paid a commission per transaction, which is far cheaper for a bank than a branch or an ATM.
- He says a loan usually comes bundled with insurance that pays it off on the death of the borrower or spouse. For other defaults, lenders can subscribe to credit guarantee funds, which he describes as a guarantee and not insurance.

## Examples and cases used

- Andhra Pradesh, 2010: his example of an external shock nobody in the industry had planned for.
- Karnataka community association default and the state microfinance law: his example of how political pressure turns into regulation.
- Razorpay: his example of regulatory action on one large entity hitting many dependent businesses.
- Paytm Payments Bank and similar payments banks: his comparison for the merchant outlet model SubK's agent network uses.
- Google accelerator: the partnership behind the video analytics work.
- Amazon: he says rural borrowers now take loans to become sourcing and stocking points for assisted commerce.
- Video scoring outside lending: he suggests a programme that assesses children's skills could record the assessment and let the system score it, so the result can be verified by a funder.
