# Founder equity, vesting, advisers and ESOPs

Speaker and date are withheld. This note keeps general teaching points only and leaves out the speaker's own frameworks, examples and materials. It covers choosing a co-founder, splitting founder equity, the co-founder agreement, vesting and reverse vesting, adviser and vendor equity, and how ESOP pools are sized, granted, taxed and handled when someone leaves, in an Indian setting.

## What was said

### Choosing a co-founder

- The instructor says the usual pairing is one industry or product expert and one person who does marketing or acquires customers.
- The instructor advises not taking on a co-founder without a working relationship of at least one year, because a friendship does not show how someone behaves when money and deliverables are on the line.
- The instructor finds teams of more than two founders very complex, and accepts that many three-founder teams work.
- The instructor says co-founders often want different things from the business, and the difference shows up only once money and targets arrive.

### Splitting founder equity

- The instructor says equity conflicts do not show when the company is small and escalate when the stakes are high.
- The instructor recommends an equal split in the early stages even when one founder has worked alone on the idea for a year, unless a difference in capital contributed explains the gap.
- The instructor's reasoning is that almost all of a company's value is created over the ten years after it takes off, so founders overvalue their first year of effort.
- The instructor says an idea is worth nothing until someone starts executing it.
- The instructor says an unequal split is acceptable, and suggests giving the founder with less a way to earn up to an equal share through milestones.
- The instructor says a milestone-linked split gives each founder a legitimate answer when outsiders question it.
- The instructor says some people favour 51 to 49 so that one person has the tie-breaker. The instructor prefers 50 to 50 with a written list of which founder has the deciding vote in which function.

### The co-founder agreement

- The instructor says most founders have no co-founder agreement, because the problems it covers have not occurred to them while they are busy building.
- The instructor says the agreement should settle job roles, expected salary, each founder's purpose in joining, when each would consider exiting, how equity transfers if one leaves, and whether to bring in an investor.
- The instructor points founders to the University of Pennsylvania's co-founder agreement template as a list of questions to work through together.
- The instructor says the co-founder agreement must be written into the company's articles of association, or it can be challenged in court.

### Vesting and reverse vesting

- The instructor defines vesting as earning equity over time, for example 25% a year over four years.
- The instructor says founders already hold their shares, so the concept that applies to them is reverse vesting, and a first term sheet is very likely to include it.
- The instructor explains the investor's logic: a founder who leaves early with a large stake is unfair to everyone still building, so the investor asks founders to keep working for four years.
- The instructor says unvested founder shares are locked, cannot be sold or transferred, and are sold to the other shareholders at face value if the founder stops contributing.
- The instructor recommends that founders set up reverse vesting between themselves early, because a departed co-founder sitting on equity is a very common problem.
- The instructor suggests milestone-based vesting in place of time-based vesting where a founder's contribution can be measured in deliverables.
- The instructor says a founder who has already built for years should ask for credit when an investor wants four years of reverse vesting, such as two years treated as vested.
- The instructor calls a clause that treats 25% of founder shares as vested on signing fair, and says most investors will not accept reverse vesting as short as one year.

### Adviser and vendor equity

- The instructor's benchmark is that total adviser equity should not exceed 2% over the life of the company, across at most four or five advisers.
- The instructor says never to give equity just to persuade an adviser to come in. Equity should be offered after the adviser has already added value.
- The instructor says adviser equity should always vest against deliverables, such as a number of meetings set up or a named customer introduced.
- The instructor says founders should not feel that asking an adviser to accept vesting is disrespectful.
- The instructor says it is a good sign when an adviser asks to earn the equity first.
- The instructor points founders to the Founder Advisor Standard Template, known as FAST, for the agreement.
- The instructor says a founder who does not need an adviser should not take one.
- The instructor says cash is rarely the incentive for senior advisers, so the founder has to work out what each person wants.
- For a vendor who discounts work in exchange for equity, the instructor says the equity should be worth 1.5 to 2 times the discount, provided the full price is the real market price.
- The instructor warns against tying someone to a small stake they do not value, because the founder then feels entitled to their time and the relationship sours.

### Sizing the ESOP pool

- The instructor says investors write a 10% or 15% ESOP pool into the term sheet by default, and most founders do not negotiate it.
- The instructor says the pool is created before the round closes, so it comes out of the founders' equity and does not dilute the incoming investor.
- The instructor says that when the pool is expanded in a later round, founders and earlier investors are both diluted.
- The instructor advises opening the conversation at 5 to 6% and expecting to settle at about 8%.
- The instructor's argument to the investor is that the round funds about two years while options vest over four, so the hiring plan will not use more than a small pool in that period.
- The instructor says that when a later investor asks to enlarge the pool, the founder should point to the unissued part of the existing pool, which is worth more at the higher valuation, and agree only a small increase.
- The instructor gives market standards for early hires as close to 1% for the first, about 0.75% for the second and about 0.5% for the third.

### Granting options

- The instructor says ESOPs compensate for cash given up, and suggests equity worth two to four times the salary an employee has forgone.
- The instructor says a grant is not shares. It is an option to buy shares later at a set price, recorded in a grant letter.
- The instructor says Indian law does not allow vesting before 12 months, so the cliff is one year. After that vesting can be monthly, quarterly, half-yearly or annual.
- The instructor says the vesting schedule is the founder's design choice and can be back loaded or front loaded.
- The instructor says the exercise price cannot be lower than the face value of the share.
- The instructor says options are issued over equity shares, while preference shares are reserved for investors.
- The instructor recommends that an outside adviser explain the ESOP policy to the team without the founder in the room, so that people ask openly.
- The instructor says an ESOP pool cannot be created without authorised but unissued share capital, so a small buffer should be kept.
- The instructor advises against a very large authorised capital, because filing fees are linked to it.

### Tax and when to exercise

- The instructor says that on exercise, the gap between fair market value and exercise price is taxed as salary at the employee's slab rate, and the employer deducts the tax.
- The instructor says the fair market value on exercise becomes the cost of acquisition, and any later gain on sale is taxed as capital gain.
- The instructor says employees cannot pay that tax out of salary on shares they cannot sell, so vested options should be exercised only when a liquidity event is near.
- The instructor advises writing into the ESOP policy that exercise happens just before a liquidity event, so that the tax is paid out of the cash received.
- The instructor says a founder can negotiate that about 5% of a funding round is used to buy back vested options, usually from Series A onwards.
- The instructor says options bought back return to the pool and can be granted again.
- The instructor says Startup India has a basic recognition certificate, which is easy to get, and a separate Inter-Ministerial Board registration, which is hard to get.
- The instructor says the board registration gives a tax holiday for any three of a company's first ten years and lets employees defer the tax on exercise until they leave.
- The instructor says the board judges applications on real innovation, employment generation and wealth created for the economy.

### When someone leaves

- The instructor says that on resignation or retirement, vested options stay with the person and unvested options lapse.
- The instructor says that on death, the standard is accelerated vesting, so the family receives the unvested options as well.
- The instructor says a bad leaver, meaning someone who left after fraud, joining a competitor or leaking information, loses vested options too.
- The instructor warns that using the bad leaver clause unfairly against one employee costs the founder the trust of the whole team.
- The instructor says that in an acquisition the more useful fight is for employees' jobs in the merged company and protection from being fired for two years.

## Numbers given

- 2% across at most four or five advisers: the instructor's lifetime ceiling for adviser equity.
- 10 to 15% asked, about 8% settled: the instructor's figures for the ESOP pool in a first term sheet.
- 13 to 20%, with about 15% as the average to plan for by IPO: the instructor's range for the total option pool.
- About 1%, 0.75% and 0.5%: the instructor's market standards for the first three hires.
- 12 months: the minimum cliff the instructor says Indian law requires.
- About 5% of a round: the instructor's figure for an ESOP buyback.
- 3 of the first 10 years: the tax holiday the instructor describes for board-registered startups.
