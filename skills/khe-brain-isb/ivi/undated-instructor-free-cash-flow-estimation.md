# Estimating free cash flow for an investment decision, part 1

A finance class taught by the programme's finance instructor, who is not named in the recording. This recording is the first half of the class, up to the break. She explains which cash flows count when a business weighs an investment, then works one example from operating profit down to free cash flow and takes questions on working capital. The second half, on NPV and IRR, is a separate recording.

## What was said

### The inputs to an investment decision

- The instructor says most business decisions need money upfront and return cash later, with no guarantee, so both time and uncertainty have to be handled, and the time value of money framework handles both.
- She lists three inputs for evaluating a project: the upfront expenditure, the cash flows expected in each period, and the discount rate.
- She describes the discount rate as the estimated opportunity cost, and uses one assumed number for it in this class.

### Two principles for estimating cash flows

- Her first principle is that only cash counts, and everything that is cash should appear in the estimate.
- Her second principle is that the cash flows must be incremental: cash flows with the project minus cash flows without it.
- Cash flows without the project are the status quo projection for the existing business.
- She says a new product can lift sales of older products, and that positive spillover belongs in the project's cash flows.
- She says a new product can also cannibalise existing ones, and that loss belongs in the project's cash flows too.
- To estimate cannibalisation she tells founders to combine market research with judgement about how similar the new product is to the old. A great deal of overlap, such as a premium version of an existing product, means more customers will switch.
- She says surveys of potential customers and the behaviour seen after earlier launches both help with these estimates.
- She says incremental cash flows are likely to be negative in the first few years of a new category, and the later cash flows then have to be that much higher to justify putting money in.

### From operating profit to free cash flow

- She starts from EBIT, also called operating profit, and subtracts tax on it to get unlevered net income.
- She explains that unlevered means borrowing is not being considered, because leverage is another word for borrowing.
- Depreciation is added back because no cash leaves the bank. The books only record that the asset is worth less.
- She says depreciation affects cash only by reducing tax, which is why it is first subtracted to get the correct tax and then added back.
- On a question about amortising a patent, she says you amortise the cost you incurred in filing it, on a straight line, and that for cash flow it hardly matters because amortisation is added back.
- Capital expenditure is subtracted in full when it is spent, because depreciation is not a cash outflow but the whole capital outlay is.
- Rent is already an expense in the income statement, so it needs no further adjustment.
- She sums up the adjustments this way: subtract what is a cash outflow but not an accounting expense, and add back what is an accounting expense but not a cash flow.

### Working capital

- She says inventory you have built but not sold is not in sales or cost of goods sold, yet you have paid for it, so a rise in inventory makes cash flow smaller than profit.
- The same logic applies to receivables: the sale is in profit but the cash has not arrived, so a rise in receivables lowers cash flow.
- Her rule to remember is that an increase in net working capital is a cash outflow and a decrease is a cash inflow.
- Her example of a decrease is revamping a supply chain: it needs upfront investment but then the business holds less inventory, which releases cash.
- She says net working capital typically increases as a business grows, though not always, because of economies of scale.
- She says the estimate of extra working capital needs detailed work on inventory levels, the terms given to customers and the terms given by vendors.
- A business whose customers pay upfront and whose vendors give time has negative working capital, and she says this is a good thing.
- For a negative working capital business, growth raises payables, so working capital falls further and cash flow rises. She says vendors are in effect financing part of the growth.
- She says a platform business with no inventory, receivables or payables skips the working capital adjustment but still has free cash flow from its sales and expenses.
- Content or IP that can be licensed for years is a long term intangible asset and not inventory, she says, because inventory stops being yours once sold and IP does not.
- She repeats that financial comparisons should only be made within the same type of business.

### Reading free cash flow

- She says negative free cash flow means you are still investing in the business, and positive free cash flow means the business has started paying back.
- A business turning cash flow positive means it no longer needs additional money put in.

## Examples and cases used

- Nirvana Juices and Beverages, a classroom example: an existing drinks business considers launching a strawberry smoothie, and the class builds year 0 and year 1 free cash flow for it.
- A creator launching a new video: the instructor uses it to show positive spillover, since a new video can raise views of older ones.
- A SaaS business paid upfront: used to show negative working capital and why growth then adds cash.

## Numbers given

- Classroom example, upfront investment: 10 lakh rupees, depreciated straight line over 5 years, so 2 lakh a year.
- Classroom example, year 1: 20,000 bottles at 100 rupees, cost of goods sold at 40% of revenue, other fixed expenses of 3 lakh, tax rate 20%.
- Classroom example, results: EBIT 7 lakh, tax 1.4 lakh, unlevered net income 5.6 lakh, depreciation added back 2 lakh, increase in net working capital 2.6 lakh, no capital expenditure in year 1, free cash flow 5 lakh.
- Her negative working capital illustration: payables rise from 10 lakh to 12 lakh, so working capital moves from minus 10 lakh to minus 12 lakh and cash flow is higher by 2 lakh.
