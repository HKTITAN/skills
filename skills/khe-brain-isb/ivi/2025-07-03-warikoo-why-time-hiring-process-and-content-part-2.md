# Why, time, hiring, process and content, part 2, Ankur Warikoo

This is the second part of Ankur Warikoo's afternoon with the cohort, run as open questions. He showed his own calendar, task tool, salary sheet and hiring forms on screen while answering. Topics were finding your why, contrarian bets, time management, how his team runs without managers, hiring, course building, personal brand, content and ads, community, and how he defines success.

## What was said

### Finding your why

- Warikoo says every choice should be made from awareness of the other options, not from ignorance. His example is choosing science, commerce or humanities in class 11 on marks and family background.
- He states his own why as helping people make choices from awareness, and says content in any form, a book, a course or a social media post, is only how he does it.
- He tells founders to watch Simon Sinek's Golden Circle talk first, and summarises it: everyone knows what they do, few know how, and very few know why.
- He says the common mistake is to start from the what, move to the how, and then invent a why that is not true.
- He repeats Sinek's line that people buy why you do something, not what you do, and says his audience would leave without being able to explain it if he turned salesy or prescriptive.
- He says to reach your real why you ask why at least five times, until the answer cannot be broken down further, and then test whether it explains everything you have done in life so far.
- He says this exercise can only be done alone and honestly, and there is no formula for it.

### Contrarian bets

- Warikoo says being contrarian does not lead to success. It leads to a different result, which can be worse than the average.
- He grounds this in investing: with the same information as everyone else you get the return everyone else gets.
- He says he speaks as a survivor, and warns against the survivorship bias of concluding that risk takers win.

### Using a cohort

- Warikoo says the first instinct in a mixed group is to look for people like yourself, and that at ISB groups of similar people formed within a week.
- He says he chose to spend time with people unlike him and had lunch with a new person almost every day, and calls it the best thing he did there.
- He says time with people like you is like a social media feed that only endorses your view.

### Time management

- Warikoo says he stopped trying to win the day, meaning doing everything every day, and started trying to win the week.
- He says he works in slots of at least four hours, so that each sitting has enough context, and accepts that not everything happens every day.
- He says he logged every waking hour from age 13 to 26 and calculated a daily productivity figure, and that this gave him a grip on where his time goes.
- He says he can look at anyone's calendar and tell how productive they are.
- He says he shoots all content on Mondays: four 30 minute YouTube videos, seven shorts each for his finance and career channels, seven Instagram reels and two podcasts, alone with a camera.
- He says content shot on a Monday is released two weeks later.
- He says he works about 15 to 20 hours a week, and that work he loves does not drain him.
- He says he was once a chief executive who filled every day with meetings, and his team told him he added little by attending.

### Process and accountability without managers

- Warikoo says every repeatable workflow is written as a process in a project management tool, Asana, with each step assigned and timed.
- He says finishing his step automatically notifies the people who do the next steps, so no email or call is needed.
- He says he designed the processes himself and the team audits each one every six months, asking whether it is the best use of everyone's time.
- He says ad hoc work also runs on checklists that are switched on when the trigger occurs.
- He says the two things everyone must be able to see are what is to be done and by when.
- He says a weekly email shows the whole team each person's completed and delayed tasks by name. His figure for one week is 239 tasks for the content team.
- He gives last quarter's figures as 3,532 unique tasks with 51 delays.
- He says each person's share of the 10% profit pool is 40% determined by whether they kept their timelines.
- He says he pays people to do the work and does not care when or how long they take, so early finishers and last minute workers both fit.
- He says each person fills a monthly self-assessment and he gives his own rating, and that the two now usually match.
- He says he does not review work before it is published, because people who know someone above will check their work lose the incentive to get it right.
- He contrasts this with a 21 year old on his team who knows her work will be seen by 16 million people.
- He traces this to his first consulting manager, who told him "if I cannot trust you, it does not matter how smart you are".
- He says he runs one weekly business review on a single business outcome, an idea he says he took from Amazon, and separate short weekly check-ins with each team that are about roadblocks, not business review.

### Pricing, refunds and piracy

- Warikoo says a business that earns only when a student gets a job makes its income depend on someone else's motivation and context, and he does not think that is a smart model.
- He says he promises the best learning for the price and a refund without questions, and has refunded people even a year and a half later.
- He says the refund rate stays at 6%, and that refunds do not worry him because serving one more student costs close to nothing.
- He says he does not fight piracy of his courses or books because he wants them read, and argues that a pirated book printed for 50 rupees shows the publisher could price lower.

### How a course is built

- Warikoo says a course starts with a curriculum designer who is a practitioner in the subject, who drafts the outline, and a small group refines it.
- He says every course first runs live for a paying founding batch, and all classes and community sessions are recorded.
- He says the founding batch gives feedback and its recordings become the raw material for the final course, so later students feel part of a cohort.
- He says the cohort feel raises completion, because a sense of community is the biggest driver of finishing a course.
- His example is a freelancing course delivered live until August, edited for about a month and launched at the end of September.
- He says the question he thinks about every day is what the university of tomorrow will look like.

### Pay and perks

- Warikoo says his team's salaries are on a sheet that is open to the public.
- He says salary bands come from market sources such as Glassdoor and Michael Page: the team records the minimum, median and maximum for each role and level and pays the median.
- His example band for a fresher video editor is a minimum of 2 lakh, a median of 3.8 lakh and a maximum of 4.5 lakh rupees a year.
- He says a hire's level is set by how they performed in the hiring process, not by their stated experience or past salary.
- He says pay is base salary, then perks, then the profit share, which depends on timeliness and quality of work.
- He says each person gets 72,000 rupees a year to spend on wellness, subscriptions and wifi.
- He says he started in March 2020 with six interns at 10,000 rupees a month, choosing well-intentioned people who would commit over experienced stars he could not afford.
- He says people stay for the environment, and that a good environment does not cost money.

### Hiring

- Warikoo says every applicant for every role first answers the same set of personal questions that have nothing to do with the job, such as how often they get angry and three things they have learnt about life.
- He says the only thing he decides at that stage is whether this is a person whose values match his and whom he would like to work with.
- He says he never looks at a resume, because it creates a strong bias, more often a negative one.
- He says applicants then take two assessments, one for emotional regulation and one for attachment style, and he goes ahead only with people who score as well regulated and securely attached.
- He says a lower score on his emotional regulation test means better regulation, and his cut-off is 30.
- He says the last step is a task assignment for the specific role, built from live problems the team faces, which tests whether they can do this job and not what they did before.
- He says he has hired this way for 13 years, after failing with hiring by pedigree, and accepts that it does not scale because the first five people decide whether a startup survives.
- He says two people have left his team in five years.
- He says he fires fast, usually within the first three months, and does not carry people who are not working out.
- He says that at Nearbuy he could not match the pay of large firms on campus, so he offered graduates two years of work that would build their profile for ISB, and nine people from Nearbuy went to ISB between 2015 and 2019.

### What the market taught him

- Warikoo says his first startup launched a used car listing product in 2009 without the founders ever speaking to a used car dealer, and nothing worked for three months.
- He says that when they went to the market they found dealers already bought and sold cars instantly for cash through their own network.
- He says the biggest lesson he learnt as a founder is that nobody buys a product because of the college the founder went to, and that entrepreneurship strips away every badge except the product.

### Personal brand and content

- Warikoo says personal branding rests on two things: consistency, which is showing up, and authenticity, which is what lets you keep showing up.
- He says people who struggle to post are usually making content that is not natural to them.
- He says he began a blog in 2005, started on Quora and LinkedIn in 2012, and started YouTube and Instagram in 2017, so what people see in 2025 was 10 to 20 years in the making.
- He says answering questions on Quora about pay and hiring at Nearbuy brought nearly 11,000 job applications in two years from people who cited his answers.
- He says he makes social media content only to help people and build trust, and never sells in it.
- He says India is a low trust society, and tells of Ola's call centre of about 480 people around 2014, where he says people called because they did not believe the cab was coming.
- He says selling is done by a separate, aggressive ads engine on separate channels, and none of his 16 million followers were paid for.
- He says his landing page converts 3 to 4% of first time visitors, and 31% of visitors who searched his name before arriving.
- He gives his ad spend as 50 to 60 lakh rupees, about 40 on Meta and 20 on Google.
- He says people follow people, not brands, so a company that wants to sell through content needs a person as its face: the founder, a team member, an AI avatar or a small creator it backs.
- He describes a pattern where a creator builds trust with useful content and only ever mentions one brand, subtly, in which they have a stake.
- He says a founder who says "buy my product" in content loses the trust the content built.
- He says a company that depends on one creator can spread the risk across several faces on the same channel.
- He says that earlier only creators who had been consistent for 10 or 20 years could win, and now the algorithm promotes good content whoever made it, so newcomers can grow in their first month.
- He says one reel reaching a million views does not mean the algorithm will favour you afterwards.
- He says X is not yet a place where people consume much video.

### Following versus community

- Warikoo says he has a following of 16 million, not a community. A following is one to many and a community is many to many.
- He says in a community members answer each other and the founder is no longer needed.
- He says his working thesis is that the best community for students is a jobs community that connects members who have work to give with members who want work.
- He says he has not found a product that runs a community of more than 50,000 well, and that he will not build on a young tool that may not last a decade.

### Investing and success

- Warikoo says he no longer invests in startups directly, because he falls for the founder more than he should. He puts money into funds as a limited partner.
- He says his picture of success came from a film scene of a grandmother at peace in her own house among family, and that his business results are not his measure.
- He says what impresses him in a person is that they have worked to know themselves, and that people avoid it because they are afraid of what they will find.

## Examples and cases used

- Simon Sinek's Golden Circle and Apple: people would buy a car or a school from Apple because they believe in its why.
- Harley-Davidson and Nike: nobody checks a Harley's mileage, they buy the feeling of freedom. Nike is bought for the belief that everybody with a body is an athlete.
- Amazon's weekly business review: the model for his single outcome review.
- A used car listing product from his first startup: built without talking to dealers.
- Ola's call centre: his illustration of how little Indian customers trust what a company tells them.
- Nikhil Kamath: named as a founder who is also a personal brand.
