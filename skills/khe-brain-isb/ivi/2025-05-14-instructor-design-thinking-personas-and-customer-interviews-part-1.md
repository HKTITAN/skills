# Design Thinking part 1, personas and customer interviews

This is part 1 of 3 of a design thinking class. The instructor is a former Microsoft product lead who refers to himself only by first name, so he is not identified here. He spent the first half on why founders must not design for themselves and on how personas differ from segments. After a break he ran a persona exercise and a live mock customer interview, then taught interview technique and the biases that distort it.

## What was said

### You are not the customer

- The instructor says whether the founder likes the product does not matter. What matters is whether the customer likes it and is ready to pay for it.
- He calls the trap "self-referential design": building what would make you happy and assuming you are the normal user.
- He says founders can share some traits with their customers, such as background, demography or industry, but they are not the same as their users.
- He says facing the problem yourself can be the beginning of a company but cannot be the end. The founder's job is to validate what other people care about.
- He says you do not need to have faced a problem to be a founder. You can have seen other people face it. The problem is the constant, not who has it.
- He names three things a venture needs, all independent of whether the founder has the problem: a clear customer value proposition with a large customer base ready to pay, a launch strategy that reaches the right customer, and a profit formula.
- He says the gap between the solution provider and what the user wants keeps growing, and a persona is a tool to bridge that gap by understanding the user deeply and having empathy.

### Segments and personas

- He says a group such as "people aged 30 and above with an eyesight problem" is a segment, not a persona.
- He says empathy is hard with a broad segment and becomes possible only with granular information about the user.
- He says a persona needs basic humanising traits such as a name, a face, a description and demography, and it describes a group of users, not one individual.
- He says the real difference from a segment is that a persona captures attitudes, mental models and behaviours.
- He says the same human being behaves as different personas depending on the situation and motivation, which is why segments alone mislead.
- He says people with the same role, segment, spending ability and goals can still behave differently, and then they must be treated as separate personas. The core product can stay the same while the experience differs.
- He summarises personas as a model, not real users: a type of group of users with similar goals, motivations, pain points and behaviours.
- He says personas are not built in a conference room. They are built through extensive field study, using both primary and secondary research. Without that you are making things up.

### Buyers and users in B2B

- He says B2C is simpler because the user is also the buyer, while B2B needs the founder to separate the buyer from the user.
- He says a startup with few resources cannot solve for everyone at once. It should first solve for the people who give the most return, to earn runway, and then use that runway to solve for the others.
- He says in SaaS renewals the buyer looks at usage metrics, meaning how many people use the product meaningfully, so solving only for the buyer is not enough.

### Knowing what not to do

- He says understanding a persona helps decide what not to build as well as what to build.
- He says knowing a problem exists does not mean you must solve it. If there is no commercial value, leave it.

### Customers, problems and solutions

- On the Steve Jobs line that customers do not know what they want, he says customers do not know the solution they want, but the problem has always existed.
- He says asking people what they want is the cardinal sin. "You never ask people what they want." Instead you work out the hindrances they face.
- He says the founder's skill is extracting the insight about the problem. The user is not expected to know the solution.
- He says no great product is a one-person show. Jobs decided, but many people gave him input, and his genius was extracting the insight from that input and turning it into a differentiated solution.
- He describes Google as engineer-centric, Microsoft as product-centric and Apple as experience-centric.
- When a user does not realise they have a problem, he says the job is not to argue with them. Validate the problem across multiple users, then build a solution whose value they can see.
- He says product management began in marketing, not technology, and that titles do not matter. His examples are Microsoft calling everyone a program manager until last year and Zomato using the title generalist.
- He says founders who spend most of their time on the problem will differentiate themselves from everyone else.
- He gives the order of work as problem first, then solution, then value proposition, then launch, marketing and pricing.
- He says he has seen hundreds of people do the research and come back saying the problem did not exist. He says most founders who start with an idea do not end with that idea.

### Using AI tools in persona work

- He says not to rely on ChatGPT to build personas. Use it for direction, not as your data.
- He says preparing what to ask in an interview used to take him two to three hours and a GPT tool now does it in seconds. That is the kind of task to use it for.

### How personas come out of field work

- He says you start with a segment and a hypothesis about a problem. The personas emerge once you start talking to users.
- He says field study reveals the persona and the problem together, not as separate steps.
- He says the hypothesis statements are tested by asking: do users have this problem, who has it, what characterises them, and who does not care.
- He says one interview gives data about one user, not a persona. Trends appear when you look across all interviews, using affinity analysis.
- He tells a founder who planned to describe the solution to interviewees and ask questions around it not to do that yet. Understand the problem first.

### Goals and activities

- He says a persona sheet should record the user's goals in the scenario, the activities they do to reach those goals, the challenges in those activities, their attitude while doing them, and their environment.
- He lists the environment as social, physical and technological.
- He says nobody's goal is to hammer a nail into a wall. The goal is to hang a picture, and hammering is an activity that serves it.
- He says you should optimise for the user's goal, not for the activities.
- He defines behaviour as how the person acts in the moment of pursuing the goal. His example is a shopper in a new country who is either watchful because he fears being fleeced or carefree because he enjoys haggling.
- He says a persona quote is something a user actually said in an interview that defines who they are.
- He says filling in the template from what you already know exposes the gaps in what you have collected about your user.

### Qualitative and quantitative validation

- He says qualitative work such as interviews gives the big picture of which areas to go after, and should be done before building a solution.
- He says quantitative work tells you how big and how important the problem is. He says the best way to do it is to put an MVP in the market and collect feedback from its use.

### How to run a customer interview

- He says an interview is not a question and answer session. He reads the word as "inter view": my view and your view, a conversation between two sets of views.
- He says a job interview works the same way. The candidate is interviewing the company as much as the company is interviewing the candidate.
- He says observation is as powerful as asking questions. His examples are daily wage workers who struggle to articulate answers and a three-year-old child.
- He attributes the interview structure he teaches to a professor at Stanford's Hasso Plattner Institute of Design: introduce yourself, introduce the project, build rapport, evoke stories and explore emotions.
- He says to seek stories, not speculation. "What will you do if this happens" is the worst question because people speculate about things they will never do.
- He says to ask about the past: what did you do when this happened, and has it happened another time. Past experience shows real behaviour patterns.
- He says rapport needs research on the person before you walk in, and simple connections such as school or college open the conversation. Some customers will still refuse, and then you leave.
- He says never suggest answers in a discovery interview. A leading question pushes the person towards the answer you want.
- He says never ask long, complex or multi-part questions, because the respondent cannot remember them.
- He describes three kinds of listening: hearing while ignoring, as with ambient noise, listening in order to respond, and listening to understand. Only the third is active listening.
- He says listening to respond makes you interrupt, because you are already working out your next question.
- He says the person asking questions should not take notes. Record the interview with permission or have a third person take notes.
- He says follow-up questions should be open-ended: why, what, how.
- He says setting the context at the start lets you bring a wandering conversation back to the topic politely.
- He says to listen intently, look for inconsistencies and watch non-verbal cues.
- He says he spent about two hours preparing before every interview he did.
- He says preparation before an interview is really required.

### Biases that distort interviews

- He ran an imagined story of a flight to an investor meeting and then asked which gender the class had pictured for the pilot, the air crew, the nurse and the head of technology. He used the result to show that everyone carries unconscious bias.
- He says bias affects both how you phrase questions and how you interpret the answers, so knowing your biases lets you compensate for them.
- He defines confirmation bias as asking questions that confirm your own hypothesis, which shows up as leading questions.
- He says anchoring bias feeds confirmation bias. If the first customer calls the idea useless, you go to every later customer looking to confirm that.
- He defines authority bias as giving greater accuracy to the opinion of an authority figure, and says authority can come from position or from knowledge.
- He says to take an expert's view as data and give it weight, but not to assume it is right. Validate it against other data points and interview multiple people.

## Examples and cases used

- Microsoft Surface RT: he says the team built it for a persona of high-income big-tech workers who like good-looking devices and carry them for pride. Those people did not buy it. Windows buyers care about productivity and being able to install any software, and the ARM version of Windows could not run existing apps.
- Google Glass: he says nobody, including its makers, could articulate who it was for or what problem it solved. It was repositioned from the general public to high fashion to safety industries such as construction and mining, and in the end it was built for the engineers themselves.
- MakeMyTrip: he says a business traveller wants to get in and out and does not care much about price, while a leisure traveller enjoys the process of exploring options and offers. The goal of booking a flight is the same but the attitude differs. He adds that during COVID, emergency travellers were ordinary consumers who needed the fast flow, and that corporate logins get an uncluttered experience with in-policy and out-of-policy flags.
- Online learning during COVID: he says research treated the parent as the persona and then found that "parent is a role". One persona trusts the child with an open internet and cares only about learning outcomes. The other wants to monitor and control every activity.
- A commercial truck maker that came to ISB: a heat sink near the pedals warmed the foot compartment for the first five minutes of driving. This mattered only in regions where drivers drive barefoot. The company calculated that redesign would cost more than expected sales there and chose not to fix it and to leave that market.
- Darwinbox: he says it first solved for the HR buyer, who cares about cost, compliance, security, privacy and integrations, and it targeted smaller companies. Once inside, it worked with employees and shipped small features so that usage and sentiment supported renewal. He says HR systems are hard to leave because migrating data takes months.
- Sony Walkman and Apple: he says the problem of carrying your music existed before Apple, and Walkman had already solved it. Apple's contribution was integrating several devices into one fluid touch product.
- BlackBerry and iPhone: he says users were happy with BlackBerry and did not know it could be improved, but they saw the value once someone improved it.
- Swiggy: he says the company began in logistics label printing, moved to hyperlocal last-mile delivery and then to food delivery. He uses it to show that the starting idea rarely sticks.
- 3M: he says that for walls where a hammer and nail work badly, 3M offered double-sided tape. That is optimising for the goal of hanging a picture.
- A study of very high earners: he says they were asked what they would do with the year's money, all said they would give to charity, and their statements at year end showed none had.
- TVS and Ather: he says TVS has been in the automobile industry for more than 100 years and Ather for less than 8, and that TVS did not predict electric vehicles. He uses it to show that an authority's view is not automatically how the market will move.

## Numbers given

- 900 million dollars: the write-off he says Microsoft took on Surface RT in 2013, after launching it in 2012.
- 1.8 billion: the number of Windows licences he cites, not counting unlicensed copies.
- 10 to 12 minutes: the time he says a leisure travel booking site is engineered to keep a user on it.
- About 3 minutes: the time he says a leisure traveller spends choosing an offer on the payment page.
- Volume and income: he says leisure is the larger share of bookings by volume and business by income, at MakeMyTrip.
- 2 to 3 hours: the time he says he used to spend deciding what to ask before an interview.
- 1.5 hours of theory and 1.5 hours of practice: how he split the class.
