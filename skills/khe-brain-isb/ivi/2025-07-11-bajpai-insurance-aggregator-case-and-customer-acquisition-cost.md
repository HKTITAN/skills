# Insurance aggregator failure case and customer acquisition cost, Amardeep Bajpai

Amardeep Bajpai opened the growth hacker bootcamp with a morning session. After founders in the room named their growth challenges, he told the story of an insurance comparison company he worked at as head of marketing, which failed in about a year and a half, and used it to teach customer acquisition cost, lifetime value and the role of branding. The session was a lecture with many questions from the room.

## What was said

### The case: an insurance comparison company in 12 European countries

- Bajpai says the company sold insurance products online in 12 European countries and had raised north of 150 million dollars as its starting money.
- He says the company faced three early questions: enter more markets or focus on one, go solo or grow through partners, and grow through acquisition marketing or through brand.
- He says the choice of many markets was driven by the vision sold to investors, which was to become the "Facebook of insurance".
- He says growth came through country managers who were partners with sizeable equity in each country, not ordinary employees.
- He says the company chose acquisition-led marketing over brand-led marketing and spent a few hundred thousand dollars a week on acquisition.
- He says the company was not really selling insurance. It was a proxy that had to build an engine to calculate other insurers' premiums and show quotes online, at a time when most insurers had no APIs for real-time prices.
- He says not every insurer shared its underwriting values, so fewer quotes were shown, and quotes were not always accurate in countries where a quote once given must be honoured.
- He says the country managers were asked to fix these local problems and deliver growth at the same time, which put them under pressure to chase acquisitions.
- He says the company ran out of runway when the 2008 financial crisis made it impossible to keep raising money, and that investors who discussed buying the whole company for one dollar declined even that.
- He says the signs of the crisis were visible about six months earlier, but the company was too committed across its markets to pull back.

### Lessons he draws from the case

- Bajpai says the question of more markets or one market should be driven less by ambition and more by the product: a product with fewer complexities can be scaled quickly, and founders often wrongly assume theirs has few dependencies.
- He says partnerships bring early growth and going solo is almost always slower, but partners must be chosen carefully and the company should have added them slowly, learning first what makes a good country manager.
- He says the company spent nothing on top-of-funnel awareness and put all its money into acquisition, and in hindsight that was the mistake.
- He says the founder later started Policybazaar with the lessons applied: go solo, focus on one market and build the biggest brand in insurance. He says the first money for it came from Naukri.com's Sanjeev Bikhchandani.
- He says he was told that a leader's key task is to make a few right decisions in a short time.

### Customer acquisition cost

- Bajpai defines customer acquisition cost as every cost spent on acquiring customers, divided by the number of incremental customers acquired, and says to repeat the same exercise per channel for a channel-level figure.
- He says the count is of customers who actually bought and paid, not leads and not people who saw the ad.
- He says distribution margins, discounts, and the salaries and bonuses of the marketing and sales teams all belong in acquisition cost. The cost of building the product does not.
- He says margins paid to wholesalers, distributors and resellers, which he puts at 5 to 15% at different points, are acquisition cost, and so is the margin a multi-level marketing company such as Amway pays to maintain its line.
- He says how aggressively to include overheads is a choice, but leaving acquisition costs out is a mistake that a venture-funded company can pay for dearly.
- He says acquisition cost is not an assumption because it can be calculated in real time, while customer lifetime value is typically an assumption.
- He says acquisition cost on digital channels moves from day to day even with the same daily budget.
- He says a business is always in motion: when it looks for growth it accepts a higher acquisition cost for future profit, and when it consolidates it spends less.

### Lifetime value and unit economics

- Bajpai says unit economics must include the cost of selling, not only the cost of goods sold.
- He says insurance brokers earn a yearly brokerage on each policy, so the industry works on customer lifetime value, and the company justified a high acquisition cost by counting many years of future brokerage.
- He says the problem with that logic is timing: the money comes in pieces year by year, so each customer leaves a hole in cash flow in the first year and the company must keep raising money.
- He contrasts this with what he calls the Marwari trader's rule of making money on every sale today and letting the customer go otherwise, and says that rule has worked for generations.
- He says that when a founder puts a higher value on a customer because of expected repeat purchases, the founder is in "the game of prediction".
- He says insurance renewals are close to guaranteed, but in other businesses people often do not come back to buy the same product, and he names Byju's as a company where this went wrong.
- He says repeat purchase rate is one of the key metrics large companies track to test the lifetime value assumption.

### Branding lowers acquisition cost

- Bajpai says that without brand support the cost of each additional customer from Google or Meta rises as volume rises, so the marginal value of the channel falls.
- He says acquisition cost falls only when two ratios improve, click-through rate and conversion rate, and both depend on how many people already know the product.
- He says branding has two parts: reach, meaning how many people in the audience get the message, and frequency, meaning how many times.
- He says a higher gross rating point count means a higher chance that the target audience received the message, which raises purchase intent.
- He says the difference between digital marketing and brand advertising is mainly that digital lets you measure acquisition cost narrowly, down to the campaign or keyword.
- He says TV, print and influencer marketing work the same way at the root, since a person reacts to a communication, and measuring them just takes more work.
- He says branding helps in the long run and can help in the short run, and acquisition marketing and brand marketing should run in tandem.

### Growth needs many parts working together

- Bajpai says growth is not only acquisition, branding, distribution or a product feature. He calls it a symphony of all of them.
- He says many e-commerce companies overestimate repeat purchase without spending on loyalty, underestimate mass outreach, and stop listening to customer feedback as the market changes.
- He says that without product-market fit, no amount of branding or acquisition spend will make acquisition profitable, and people will not remember the product for long.
- He says Meta ads can be one component of a growth story but not the whole story.
- He says founders who focus only on product features miss that Apple got both the product and the brand right.
- He closes the session with the line that "almost all marketing is measurable", but that does not make it replicable or scalable.

## Examples and cases used

- Insurance comparison company in Europe (his former employer): too many markets, too many partners added too fast, and all spend on acquisition with none on brand.
- Policybazaar: the same founder's second attempt, built solo in one market with heavy brand building. He cites its campaigns with Akshay Kumar.
- Confused.com: a large insurance aggregator that already existed in the West, his evidence that the failed company was not ahead of its time.
- Byju's: his example of lifetime value assumptions failing because customers did not keep buying.
- Amway: its channel margin is its customer acquisition cost.
- ibibo: heavy advertising made the name well known, but people did not know what it was for.
- Apple: the most sustainable product growth story because product and brand were both right.

## Numbers given

- North of 150 million dollars: the starting capital of the insurance comparison company, in Bajpai's telling.
- About one and a half years: how long the company ran before it failed.
- 12: the number of European countries it operated in.
- 5 to 15%: the margins he says are paid at different points of a wholesaler, distributor and reseller chain, all of which count as acquisition cost.
- A daily Meta budget of 5,000 rupees that brings five paying customers: his worked example of calculating acquisition cost.
