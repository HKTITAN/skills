# Pricing, sales and business models

This file answers three questions a founder brings together: what to charge, how to sell to businesses while the founders are still the sales team, and which business model the company is. The speakers are Kevin Hale, Dalton Caldwell, Tom Blomfield, Pete Koomen and Aaron Epstein, with Paul Graham's 2013 essay and a post signed Y Combinator for sales material, and Ryan Junee, who is a founder writing in the YC library and not a YC partner. The talks run from 2019 to 2025, so every money figure and rule of thumb carries its year, and where two years give different numbers both are kept.

## What should I charge?

- Hale, in a talk the library dates to 2019, frames every price with three points, cost, price and value: the gap between cost and price is the margin and the seller's incentive to sell, and the gap between price and value is the customer's incentive to buy. (Startup pricing 101, https://www.ycombinator.com/library/6h-startup-pricing-101)
- Hale says a price can be worked out from cost, which is cost-plus, or from value, and that startups should aim for value-based pricing because it lets them charge far more. (Startup pricing 101, https://www.ycombinator.com/library/6h-startup-pricing-101)
- Blomfield, in a talk the library dates to 2024 on B2B pricing, says never to start from cost, because cost plus a margin always ends in underpriced software: "Cost should only ever be a floor." (How To Price For B2B, https://www.ycombinator.com/library/LI-how-to-price-for-b2b)
- Hale's rule of thumb, in a talk the library dates to 2019, is that the value should be ten times the price and easy for the customer to see as ten times, so a $10 product should feel worth $100 to them. (Startup pricing 101, https://www.ycombinator.com/library/6h-startup-pricing-101)
- Blomfield, in a talk the library dates to 2024, charges B2B customers between 25 and 50% of the value the product delivers, so the customer keeps roughly two-thirds and the startup roughly a third. (How To Price For B2B, https://www.ycombinator.com/library/LI-how-to-price-for-b2b)
- Blomfield's worked example, in a talk the library dates to 2024, is a support team of a hundred agents at a fully loaded $100,000 each, where a tool that removes 20% of the work saves $2 million and might be priced at about $700,000. (How To Price For B2B, https://www.ycombinator.com/library/LI-how-to-price-for-b2b)
- Blomfield's example in a talk the library dates to 2025 is a customer service AI that saves a customer perhaps a million dollars in salaries a year, for which the startup might charge $200,000. (The Sales Playbook For Founders, https://www.ycombinator.com/library/Mo-the-sales-playbook-for-founders)
- Hale says the customers a startup first sells to are early adopters, the first 2% to 5% of a market, and that they care more about benefits than about price. (Startup pricing 101, https://www.ycombinator.com/library/6h-startup-pricing-101)
- Hale, in a talk the library dates to 2019, cites a survey of over 500 SaaS companies in which 1% more effort on acquisition returned about 3.32%, on retention about 6.7%, and on pricing the most, and he calls pricing the most neglected of the three. (Startup pricing 101, https://www.ycombinator.com/library/6h-startup-pricing-101)
- Epstein, in a talk the library dates to 2022, tells founders who are starting out to find only the right order of magnitude: ten dollars against a customer's hundred needs changing, ten against fifteen or twenty does not. (YC guide to business models, https://www.ycombinator.com/library/Gh-yc-guide-to-business-models)
- Koomen, in a talk the library dates to 2024 on enterprise sales, advises treating each pricing conversation as an experiment that tests one price point and learns from how the prospect reacts. (Enterprise Sales for Founders, https://www.ycombinator.com/library/LF-enterprise-sales-for-founders)
- Koomen tells founders making their first few sales to pick a number that makes them a little uncomfortable and to accept being negotiated down, because at that point they are optimising for learning and not unit economics. (Enterprise Sales for Founders, https://www.ycombinator.com/library/LF-enterprise-sales-for-founders)
- Epstein's fifth pricing insight is to keep it simple: he contrasts a Quicken pricing page with five buy buttons against GitLab's three clear plans, and says complexity adds friction that stops customers paying. (YC guide to business models, https://www.ycombinator.com/library/Gh-yc-guide-to-business-models)

## Am I charging too little, and how do I raise prices?

- Caldwell, in a talk the library dates to 2019, says most startups charge far too little, and that he has seen prices at a tenth or a hundredth of the right level. (Most startups are undercharging, https://www.ycombinator.com/library/7M-most-startups-are-undercharging)
- Caldwell warns that winning by being cheaper is dangerous because the founder cannot tell whether the product is good or solves a real problem, and may only be collecting buyers who want the cheapest option. (Most startups are undercharging, https://www.ycombinator.com/library/7M-most-startups-are-undercharging)
- Caldwell says a product that becomes successful usually charges a premium over competitors, and that a market willing to pay a premium is a sign of something people want. (Most startups are undercharging, https://www.ycombinator.com/library/7M-most-startups-are-undercharging)
- Koomen says the most frequent pricing mistake among founders is a price that is too low, or giving the product away in exchange for feedback, out of worry that a price will scare customers off. (Enterprise Sales for Founders, https://www.ycombinator.com/library/LF-enterprise-sales-for-founders)
- Epstein's first pricing insight is to charge at all: he calls not charging the most common mistake, made because founders fear customers will say no, walk away or go to a competitor. (YC guide to business models, https://www.ycombinator.com/library/Gh-yc-guide-to-business-models)
- Hale, in a talk the library dates to 2019, tells founders who have any price, especially in B2B or enterprise sales, to practise raising it by 5% at a time and to keep going until they lose 20% of their deals. (Startup pricing 101, https://www.ycombinator.com/library/6h-startup-pricing-101)
- Blomfield, in a talk the library dates to 2024, tells B2B founders who cannot work out a price to pick a number near what the customer pays for other software, then raise it by 50% with each new customer pitched, and to treat losing more than 25% of potential deals on price alone as the sign the number is about right. (How To Price For B2B, https://www.ycombinator.com/library/LI-how-to-price-for-b2b)
- Epstein, in a talk the library dates to 2022, says to keep raising prices in small steps until users push back, and that the ideal price is the one where customers complain and still pay, while an immediate yes probably means money left on the table. (YC guide to business models, https://www.ycombinator.com/library/Gh-yc-guide-to-business-models)
- Epstein says raising prices is the easiest way to grow revenue: a company with a thousand customers can double revenue by doubling the price, if the product supports it, instead of finding a thousand more customers. (YC guide to business models, https://www.ycombinator.com/library/Gh-yc-guide-to-business-models)
- Epstein allows a lower price in four cases: for the very first user, for a customer with a recognisable logo, when the product builds lock-in, and when the price can be raised at renewal after the first year. (YC guide to business models, https://www.ycombinator.com/library/Gh-yc-guide-to-business-models)
- Epstein says pricing is not permanent, and gives two ways to raise it: let existing customers keep their price and raise it only for new ones, or give advance notice of the increase. (YC guide to business models, https://www.ycombinator.com/library/Gh-yc-guide-to-business-models)
- Epstein, in a talk the library dates to 2022, says Stripe set its early price at five percent per transaction when most competitors charged around three, to test how much value customers saw in fast sign-up and detailed developer documentation. (YC guide to business models, https://www.ycombinator.com/library/Gh-yc-guide-to-business-models)
- Koomen says the Collison brothers charged more for Stripe than their competition at the start, and that selling anyway was evidence they were onto something: "High prices make customers more serious." (Enterprise Sales for Founders, https://www.ycombinator.com/library/LF-enterprise-sales-for-founders)
- Koomen, in a talk the library dates to 2024, recalls his Optimizely co-founder quoting a prospect $10,000 a month and being talked down to $2,000 a month before the prospect bought, his example that a customer who really wants the product is hard to scare away with a high quote. (Enterprise Sales for Founders, https://www.ycombinator.com/library/LF-enterprise-sales-for-founders)
- Epstein, in a talk the library dates to 2022, tells how Segment, which had just begun charging ten dollars a month, was pushed by a sales advisor to quote a hundred and twenty thousand dollars a year: the customer countered with twelve thousand and they settled on eighteen thousand, 150 times the old price. (YC guide to business models, https://www.ycombinator.com/library/Gh-yc-guide-to-business-models)
- In an undated post signed Y Combinator, the authors quote Paul Buchheit's line that taking 80 cents from a customer and handing back a dollar never makes sense, and say growing an unprofitable product only drains cash. (YC's essential startup advice, https://www.ycombinator.com/library/4D-yc-s-essential-startup-advice)

## How do I price a B2B or enterprise contract?

- Blomfield, in a talk the library dates to 2024, says founders who have never worked at a big company are badly calibrated on what companies pay for software, think of their own subscriptions, and quote $19 or $49 a month. (How To Price For B2B, https://www.ycombinator.com/library/LI-how-to-price-for-b2b)
- Blomfield's main B2B pricing tool is what he calls the value equation: write down with the champion, step by step, the cost saving, time saving or revenue increase the product will deliver, and have them challenge it, because they will take it to their boss or CFO to justify the purchase. (How To Price For B2B, https://www.ycombinator.com/library/LI-how-to-price-for-b2b)
- Blomfield, in a talk the library dates to 2024, tells B2B software startups to aim for gross margins of 80 or 90%. (How To Price For B2B, https://www.ycombinator.com/library/LI-how-to-price-for-b2b)
- Blomfield says that when an equivalent competitor undercuts by half, matching them starts a race to the bottom, so the answer is to differentiate on functionality or value, for instance by picking a niche, certain integrations or certain industries. (How To Price For B2B, https://www.ycombinator.com/library/LI-how-to-price-for-b2b)
- Blomfield advises asking the champion how they pay for similar software, whether a flat monthly fee, per seat, usage bands or credits, and mirroring the structure that industry is used to. (How To Price For B2B, https://www.ycombinator.com/library/LI-how-to-price-for-b2b)
- Blomfield, in a talk the library dates to 2024, prefers committed monthly or annual recurring revenue to usage-based pricing, because committed revenue is protected in a downturn until renewal, while usage revenue can collapse in one bad month, which worries investors. (How To Price For B2B, https://www.ycombinator.com/library/LI-how-to-price-for-b2b)
- Blomfield, in a talk the library dates to 2024, suggests asking the champion what amount they can sign off alone, giving $15,000 as an example of such a limit, and pricing the pilot under it so no CFO or legal approval slows the deal. (How To Price For B2B, https://www.ycombinator.com/library/LI-how-to-price-for-b2b)
- Blomfield, in a talk the library dates to 2025, gives the same tactic with a $10,000 or $20,000 charge on the champion's corporate card as the example, and says he would often take less money to skip a long approval process. (The Sales Playbook For Founders, https://www.ycombinator.com/library/Mo-the-sales-playbook-for-founders)
- Blomfield says an enterprise price published on a website leaves money on the table, because the value differs for each enterprise customer: one number is too high for some and far too low for others. (How To Price For B2B, https://www.ycombinator.com/library/LI-how-to-price-for-b2b)
- Blomfield, in a talk the library dates to 2024, describes the usual structure as one or two cheaper plans plus an enterprise plan that gates things such as single sign-on, audit logs, compliance reports and data kept in certain geographies, sometimes at up to 10 times the price. (How To Price For B2B, https://www.ycombinator.com/library/LI-how-to-price-for-b2b)
- Koomen says early Optimizely published self-serve pricing but kept the Enterprise plan unpublished and sold through sales, which let the founders try a different price each time. (Enterprise Sales for Founders, https://www.ycombinator.com/library/LF-enterprise-sales-for-founders)
- Koomen says the most important pricing conversations happen without the founder in the room, so the prospect should be given slides or a one-page PDF that explains the pricing and the product's benefits. (Enterprise Sales for Founders, https://www.ycombinator.com/library/LF-enterprise-sales-for-founders)

## How does my price decide how I can sell?

- Hale, in a talk the library dates to 2019, says a self-serve product priced at $2,000 or less can afford almost only inbound marketing, minimal support and no sales team, though conversions can happen the same day. (Startup pricing 101, https://www.ycombinator.com/library/6h-startup-pricing-101)
- Hale, in a talk the library dates to 2019, says a price between $2,000 and $10,000 pays for qualified-lead marketing, onboarding help and perhaps an inside sales rep, though not a dedicated salesperson, with a sales cycle no longer than one to three months. (Startup pricing 101, https://www.ycombinator.com/library/6h-startup-pricing-101)
- Hale, in a talk the library dates to 2019, says a price over $25,000 is enterprise, which supports brand marketing, high-touch support, sales managers, territories and sales engineers, with a sales cycle of about six to 12 months. (Startup pricing 101, https://www.ycombinator.com/library/6h-startup-pricing-101)
- Hale says a company that takes months to close each customer without the revenue to cover it has acquisition costs too high to sustain, and must raise the price or cut the cost of acquisition. (Startup pricing 101, https://www.ycombinator.com/library/6h-startup-pricing-101)
- Blomfield, in a talk the library dates to 2024, gives a rule of thumb of about 5 to 1 between the new annual recurring revenue a salesperson signs and their total pay including commission, so $100,000 of pay should bring in $500,000 of new ARR a year. (How To Price For B2B, https://www.ycombinator.com/library/LI-how-to-price-for-b2b)
- Blomfield, in a talk the library dates to 2024, works that target through: at $25,000 a contract it is about two deals a month, which he calls doable, but at $1,000 a contract a rep would need 42 deals a month, which is at best an inside-sales call centre and not an outbound sales team. (How To Price For B2B, https://www.ycombinator.com/library/LI-how-to-price-for-b2b)

## Do I have to do the selling myself?

- Koomen, in a talk the library dates to 2024, says the founder of an early-stage startup selling to businesses is capable of selling the product and is probably the only person who can, since a founder who cannot sell it at first is unlikely to be able to hire someone to do it. (Enterprise Sales for Founders, https://www.ycombinator.com/library/LF-enterprise-sales-for-founders)
- Koomen says sales before product-market fit is a founder's job because it takes vision, credibility with customers, constant experimenting and close contact with whoever builds the product. (Enterprise Sales for Founders, https://www.ycombinator.com/library/LF-enterprise-sales-for-founders)
- Graham's 2013 essay says in a note that founders cannot avoid sales by hiring someone for it: they do it themselves at first and can hire a real salesperson to replace them later. (Do Things that Don't Scale, https://paulgraham.com/ds.html)
- Graham's 2013 essay says a startup succeeds only if one founder or more, usually the CEO, puts a lot of time into sales and marketing. (Do Things that Don't Scale, https://paulgraham.com/ds.html)
- Koomen says technical founders probably do not need a business co-founder to sell, because they bring expertise in the problem and the product and sincere conviction, which he finds surprisingly important in sales. (Enterprise Sales for Founders, https://www.ycombinator.com/library/LF-enterprise-sales-for-founders)
- Graham's 2013 essay says the most common unscalable thing founders must do at the start is recruit users by hand, and that nearly all startups have to. (Do Things that Don't Scale, https://paulgraham.com/ds.html)
- Junee, a founder writing in the YC library, argues that hackers can sell to enterprises because a large company is a network of people and processes that can be understood and influenced the way a computer system can. (Enterprise sales for hackers, https://www.ycombinator.com/library/4m-enterprise-sales-for-hackers)
- Junee addresses early stage companies with no repeatable sales process yet, and assumes they are at or near product-market fit. (Enterprise sales for hackers, https://www.ycombinator.com/library/4m-enterprise-sales-for-hackers)
- Junee says he hires salespeople for skills and raw characteristics over experience, and warns early companies against the "coin operated" salesperson from a large company when they need someone comfortable with uncertainty who learns quickly. (Enterprise sales for hackers, https://www.ycombinator.com/library/4m-enterprise-sales-for-hackers)

## How do I run the funnel from prospect to signed deal?

- Koomen, in a talk the library dates to 2024, walks the enterprise sales funnel as six steps: prospecting, outreach, qualification, pricing, closing and implementation. (Enterprise Sales for Founders, https://www.ycombinator.com/library/LF-enterprise-sales-for-founders)
- Koomen says prospecting starts from a sales hypothesis, a statement that a named kind of customer has a named problem the product will solve, which makes clear who to talk to. (Enterprise Sales for Founders, https://www.ycombinator.com/library/LF-enterprise-sales-for-founders)
- Koomen says the easiest way to get a meeting is to have the prospect reach out, so even a sales-led startup should launch early and often, publish technical content, build self-serve demos and answer questions in the forums its customers use. (Enterprise Sales for Founders, https://www.ycombinator.com/library/LF-enterprise-sales-for-founders)
- Koomen says to look for a warm introduction first, and that cold email is usually the least efficient way to get a prospect's attention but can work when each one is written by hand, short, clear in its ask and specific about why this recipient. (Enterprise Sales for Founders, https://www.ycombinator.com/library/LF-enterprise-sales-for-founders)
- Koomen says that when a startup's customers gather at industry conferences the founders should be there too, with the attendee list in hand and meetings set up in advance. (Enterprise Sales for Founders, https://www.ycombinator.com/library/LF-enterprise-sales-for-founders)
- The Y Combinator post's summary list says to stay away from conferences except where they are the best route to customers. (YC's essential startup advice, https://www.ycombinator.com/library/4D-yc-s-essential-startup-advice)
- Koomen warns against talking to anyone who will take the call, because that selects for people who are easy to talk to instead of good customers and gives an illusion of progress. (Enterprise Sales for Founders, https://www.ycombinator.com/library/LF-enterprise-sales-for-founders)
- Koomen says one form of that mistake is going bottom-up with a product that must be adopted top-down: for software a large hospital has to coordinate across teams, an individual doctor is no use and the founder needs a senior leader such as a CFO or CIO. (Enterprise Sales for Founders, https://www.ycombinator.com/library/LF-enterprise-sales-for-founders)
- Koomen calls the idea of selling to companies that buy quickly even when they are poor customers a meme attributed to YC and a misconception: look for fast buyers, he says, but only ones with the problem, the budget and the authority to decide. (Enterprise Sales for Founders, https://www.ycombinator.com/library/LF-enterprise-sales-for-founders)
- Graham's 2013 essay says in a note that when the choice is between users who will sign up quickest and those who will pay the most, it is usually best to pick the quickest, since they are probably the early adopters and take less sales effort. (Do Things that Don't Scale, https://paulgraham.com/ds.html)
- Koomen says the first call is not for selling: it is for qualifying the prospect on problem, budget and decision-making authority, and for booking a follow-up demo. (Enterprise Sales for Founders, https://www.ycombinator.com/library/LF-enterprise-sales-for-founders)
- Koomen says one of the biggest founder sales mistakes is diving into the pitch without asking enough questions, and that great salespeople mostly listen. (Enterprise Sales for Founders, https://www.ycombinator.com/library/LF-enterprise-sales-for-founders)
- Koomen says the job of a demo is to convince the audience their problem can be solved, so it should tell the story of one user solving that problem and not be a tour of features. (Enterprise Sales for Founders, https://www.ycombinator.com/library/LF-enterprise-sales-for-founders)
- Koomen says the biggest mistake at closing is being surprised that a done deal is not done, and that the cure is asking the prospect upfront how they buy software and who has to sign off. (Enterprise Sales for Founders, https://www.ycombinator.com/library/LF-enterprise-sales-for-founders)
- Koomen says to speed procurement by starting steps like a security questionnaire early and in parallel, and by keeping legal documents simple, for which he recommends the open-source templates from Common Paper. (Enterprise Sales for Founders, https://www.ycombinator.com/library/LF-enterprise-sales-for-founders)
- Koomen says the single biggest mistake founders make is treating implementation as the customer's job: Optimizely closed six-figure deals and found at renewal a year later that some customers had not run one test. (Enterprise Sales for Founders, https://www.ycombinator.com/library/LF-enterprise-sales-for-founders)
- Koomen says Optimizely learned to build implementation plans with the customer before signing, to refuse to sign without one, and to project-manage each rollout with a shared roadmap, an owner for every task and regular check-ins. (Enterprise Sales for Founders, https://www.ycombinator.com/library/LF-enterprise-sales-for-founders)
- Blomfield, in a talk the library dates to 2025, says that after signing a founder may need as much or more effort on onboarding, and tells of a company that had signed $4 million of contracts and implemented less than $2 million of them for lack of a customer success function. (The Sales Playbook For Founders, https://www.ycombinator.com/library/Mo-the-sales-playbook-for-founders)

## How do I run pilots and get an enterprise deal through?

- Blomfield, in a talk the library dates to 2025, describes a progression for first B2B contracts from the long unpaid design partnership, through free and then paid pilots, to recurring contracts with an opt-out period, and says founders get stuck in the early stages 90% of the time. (The Sales Playbook For Founders, https://www.ycombinator.com/library/Mo-the-sales-playbook-for-founders)
- Blomfield, in a talk the library dates to 2025, says design partnerships are often far too long, three or six months, poorly scoped, and short of engagement because the customer is not paying for the founders' time. (The Sales Playbook For Founders, https://www.ycombinator.com/library/Mo-the-sales-playbook-for-founders)
- Blomfield says most design partnerships, though not all, are a waste of time, and that the alternative is to pick a narrow wedge product, sell it hard for a couple of weeks, and pick a different wedge if it does not sell. (The Sales Playbook For Founders, https://www.ycombinator.com/library/Mo-the-sales-playbook-for-founders)
- Graham's 2013 essay says he sometimes advises founders of B2B startups to choose one user and behave like consultants building for that user alone, tweaking until the product fits them perfectly. (Do Things that Don't Scale, https://paulgraham.com/ds.html)
- Graham's 2013 essay says this is safe only while the startup is not paid for the attentiveness, because a customer who starts paying by the hour expects everything to be done. (Do Things that Don't Scale, https://paulgraham.com/ds.html)
- Blomfield, in a talk the library dates to 2025, says free trials, pilots and proofs of concept share the design partnership's low-commitment problem when they run two or three months with no target, so each needs success metrics agreed with the customer in advance. (The Sales Playbook For Founders, https://www.ycombinator.com/library/Mo-the-sales-playbook-for-founders)
- Blomfield, in a talk the library dates to 2024, calls a long free trial or pilot counterproductive and says to keep one to a couple of weeks, maybe four, with clear success criteria taken from the value equation. (How To Price For B2B, https://www.ycombinator.com/library/LI-how-to-price-for-b2b)
- Blomfield, in a talk the library dates to 2025, says a paid pilot should be only long enough for the customer to feel the full benefit, maybe seven or 14 days when the product is well tuned. (The Sales Playbook For Founders, https://www.ycombinator.com/library/Mo-the-sales-playbook-for-founders)
- Blomfield says founders fear the willingness-to-pay conversation, that raising a dollar amount almost never scares the customer off, and that the founder needs to disqualify customers who are not ready, able or willing to buy. (The Sales Playbook For Founders, https://www.ycombinator.com/library/Mo-the-sales-playbook-for-founders)
- Blomfield says getting paid for the pilot upfront makes the customer take it much more seriously, since they are now paying for the founders' time. (The Sales Playbook For Founders, https://www.ycombinator.com/library/Mo-the-sales-playbook-for-founders)
- Blomfield, in a talk the library dates to 2025, says the move past paid pilots is a monthly or annual recurring contract that opens with a money-back or opt-out period of 30 or 60 days, which becomes a full contract by default with no second sales process. (The Sales Playbook For Founders, https://www.ycombinator.com/library/Mo-the-sales-playbook-for-founders)
- Blomfield, in a talk the library dates to 2024, said of the same opt-out contract that a founder can count it as recurring revenue pretty much straight away. (How To Price For B2B, https://www.ycombinator.com/library/LI-how-to-price-for-b2b)
- Blomfield, in a talk the library dates to 2025, adds a caution to be careful what is reported to investors as MRR or ARR while customers are still inside the opt-out period, and to say clearly where those customers sit. (The Sales Playbook For Founders, https://www.ycombinator.com/library/Mo-the-sales-playbook-for-founders)
- Blomfield, in a talk the library dates to 2025, says to start SOC 2 and any other security certification as soon as possible, because they can delay a deal by months. (The Sales Playbook For Founders, https://www.ycombinator.com/library/Mo-the-sales-playbook-for-founders)
- Blomfield says to treat the internal champion almost like a co-founder inside the customer, and to set a closing date with them, which he says they will miss almost every time but which still creates urgency. (The Sales Playbook For Founders, https://www.ycombinator.com/library/Mo-the-sales-playbook-for-founders)
- Blomfield says early founders should sign more or less whatever contract terms a customer wants unless a clause is company-ending, such as unlimited liability or a transfer of all the product's IP. (The Sales Playbook For Founders, https://www.ycombinator.com/library/Mo-the-sales-playbook-for-founders)
- Junee says legal teams wear a seller down with redlines on clauses that matter little, so the founder should decide up front what they care about and give way on the rest. (Enterprise sales for hackers, https://www.ycombinator.com/library/4m-enterprise-sales-for-hackers)
- Junee says a champion is the seller's ally inside the target company, that their influence depends on seniority, and that a founder needs champions at director level and above as well as on the line. (Enterprise sales for hackers, https://www.ycombinator.com/library/4m-enterprise-sales-for-hackers)
- Junee advises avoiding the IT department until security reviews require it, unless the product's users are in IT, and says he has been burned building features IT asked for that the real users did not want. (Enterprise sales for hackers, https://www.ycombinator.com/library/4m-enterprise-sales-for-hackers)
- Junee, in an undated post, says procurement is usually incentivised to get the lowest price, so the seller should arrive ready to give something, which he offers as one reason enterprise software carries a high list price discounted by 80% or more. (Enterprise sales for hackers, https://www.ycombinator.com/library/4m-enterprise-sales-for-hackers)
- The Y Combinator post says founders are tempted to chase big deals with large companies, and that such deals rarely end well for a tiny startup: they run long, cost a lot and often fail outright. (YC's essential startup advice, https://www.ycombinator.com/library/4D-yc-s-essential-startup-advice)

## Which business model am I, and what do I measure?

- Epstein, in a talk the library dates to 2022, says nearly every billion dollar company uses one of nine business models, and that a founder should innovate on the product and copy the business model from a proven one. (YC guide to business models, https://www.ycombinator.com/library/Gh-yc-guide-to-business-models)
- Epstein, in a talk the library dates to 2022, sorted the top 100 YC companies by primary model and found SaaS at 31, transactional at 22 and marketplaces at 14, together 67 percent of the list. (YC guide to business models, https://www.ycombinator.com/library/Gh-yc-guide-to-business-models)
- Epstein, in a talk the library dates to 2022, says marketplaces are 14 percent of that top 100 but 30 percent of its value, because so many of them are in the top ten, and that although they are hard to start, needing supply and demand at once, they become dominant winners once network effects arrive. (YC guide to business models, https://www.ycombinator.com/library/Gh-yc-guide-to-business-models)
- Epstein, in a talk the library dates to 2022, says transactional businesses are 22 percent of the top 100 and 29 percent of its value, because they sit directly in the flow of funds and can easily take a cut. (YC guide to business models, https://www.ycombinator.com/library/Gh-yc-guide-to-business-models)
- Epstein, in a talk the library dates to 2022, says only three percent of the top 100 make their money mainly from advertising, and that ads should not be the primary model unless the founder expects a top 10 site on the internet. (YC guide to business models, https://www.ycombinator.com/library/Gh-yc-guide-to-business-models)
- Epstein says no services or consulting business is in the top 100, since their revenue does not recur, they scale with people and their margins are low, though he says consulting for customers can be a good way to learn what to build. (YC guide to business models, https://www.ycombinator.com/library/Gh-yc-guide-to-business-models)
- Epstein says recurring revenue works only with strong retention: at 95 percent monthly retention, 100 customers at the start of a year become 54 by the end. (YC guide to business models, https://www.ycombinator.com/library/Gh-yc-guide-to-business-models)
- Epstein says that at 90 percent monthly retention the same 100 customers become 28, a gap he says can decide whether a startup lives. (YC guide to business models, https://www.ycombinator.com/library/Gh-yc-guide-to-business-models)
- Epstein's written guide, which the library dates to 2022, gives the main SaaS metrics as monthly or annual recurring revenue, growth rate, net revenue retention and customer acquisition cost, and says SaaS is usually sold to businesses, ideally on annual contracts. (YC guide to business models, https://www.ycombinator.com/library/Gh-yc-guide-to-business-models)
- Epstein's written guide, which the library dates to 2022, says transactional businesses are measured on gross transaction value and net revenue, and often run high volume on a low fee, with 1 to 3% common. (YC guide to business models, https://www.ycombinator.com/library/Gh-yc-guide-to-business-models)
- Epstein's written guide gives marketplaces gross merchandise value and net revenue as metrics, and says supply and demand must be scaled in sync. (YC guide to business models, https://www.ycombinator.com/library/Gh-yc-guide-to-business-models)
- Epstein's written guide, which the library dates to 2022, defines the enterprise model as large fixed-term contracts with companies of 5k or more employees, very few customers, deals of $100k or more a year, and a frequent start in paid pilots or letters of intent. (YC guide to business models, https://www.ycombinator.com/library/Gh-yc-guide-to-business-models)
- Epstein's written guide warns against confusing usage-based revenue with recurring revenue, and has usage-based companies track monthly revenue, revenue retention and gross margin. (YC guide to business models, https://www.ycombinator.com/library/Gh-yc-guide-to-business-models)
