# Mental fitness and the power of pause, Swati Desai

The speaker, who coordinates the campus counselling team, ran her second workshop with the cohort on handling stress as a founder. The recording does not name her. She is identified from the programme's speaker list. The session was a series of guided exercises with short explanations between them: a breathing pause, a body scan, recalling something loved, mindful sipping of a drink, and writing down an intention.

## What was said

### The pause

- Swati Desai calls the pause the foundational skill for mental health, and says a person cannot follow any other mental health advice until they learn it.
- She separates a pause from a break. A break is going away to watch a film or scroll reels. A pause is stopping everything for a short time and paying attention to breath and body.
- Her pause to take in energy is a deep breath in and out with attention on the breath and body.
- Her pause to relax the body is a short deep inhale followed by a very long exhale, which can be a sigh.
- She says the pause comes first whenever a person wants to change a habit, because the change needs awareness.
- She says relaxing the body takes practice, and at first it can take a long time for the body to settle.

### What founders face

- She says stress, anxiety and pressure are part of a founder's life, and that anxiety has become a new normal that people are not used to handling.
- Her list of what founders experience includes feeling overwhelmed, not being able to switch off or sleep, panic, feeling unmotivated, guilt about family, self-medication through partying and over-drinking, and impulsive behaviour.
- Since her first workshop she added four items to the list: FOMO, loneliness, communication with family and tough feedback.
- She describes burnout as fatigue where a person is too overwhelmed to think, wants only to sleep, and cannot focus when they start working.
- She says detachment can be needed for a short time, but if it continues it is not the good kind.

### The amygdala

- She says the amygdala is the emotional centre of the brain, and a very small part of the brain that ends up controlling a person.
- She says the amygdala is activated when it senses a threat, and it only sends the body the message to fight, flee or freeze. It does not think about long term harm or about solving the problem creatively.
- She adds a fourth response, fawn, where a person says nice things to get into someone's good books.
- She defines anxiety as fear of something bad that is anticipated, and stress as something happening right now that one must get ready for.
- She says stress becomes anxiety because people create scenarios in their heads.
- She says whenever there is stress or anxiety the first job is to calm the emotions.

### Calming down and feeding the good wolf

- She asks the group to notice where stress sits in the body. The room names shoulders, chest and stomach.
- She says shaking the hands or jumping around when the body is uncomfortable is a way of taking care of the body and not a destructive impulse.
- One exercise is to think of a person, a place or an activity that gives joy and love. She says the aim is for oxytocin to replace cortisol.
- She defines compassion as feeling love, and not judgment, on seeing someone in pain.
- She says whatever a person feeds in the moment gets stronger, and puts it as "neurons that fire together, wire together".
- She says the mind dwells on what could go wrong because it wants to be prepared, and the counter is to also pay attention to the positive things that happen every day.
- She suggests pausing to notice pleasant surroundings as an easy way to practise this.

### Two daily practices

- The first practice is mindful drinking. Take the first two sips of tea or water slowly, notice the sensations and what comes to mind, and do nothing about it. She calls this a two minute meditation.
- She says if the tea is not warm enough, do not go to the microwave. Tolerating the small discomfort is the practice.
- The second practice is the telephone challenge. Take one deep breath before picking up the phone. She asks for it at least three times, not every time.
- She says these practices need daily repetition to change a habit.
- She says a person who cannot find even two minutes for this is not prioritising their mental fitness.
- She says two or three minutes at a time is enough, and waiting or travel time can be used for it.

### Intention, inspiration and integrity

- She gives three next steps after the pause: intention, inspiration and integrity.
- She uses the marshmallow experiment, where some children ate the one marshmallow and others waited for two, to illustrate holding back an impulse.
- She defines integrity as stopping one's impulses, and links it to not reheating the tea.
- She asks each person to write down their intention privately, including the weakness they want to overcome, and to read it regularly.
- She says reading and visualising an intention puts it in the subconscious, so the brain keeps working on it and starts noticing relevant opportunities. Her comparison is buying a Honda and then noticing Hondas everywhere.
- She tells the group to change the story they tell themselves under distress. In place of thinking they cannot take it, they can tell themselves they have handled it before and can handle it again.
- She says in distress a person needs to trust that they can handle it, and that it feels like a life and death issue but is not.
- She notes that intoxication is a common way groups handle stress, and asks the group to be aware of it without telling them not to drink.

### Putting it together and getting help

- Her sequence under distress is to pause, notice the body and name what one is feeling, calm the amygdala, and then do creative problem solving.
- She says professional counsellors are available on campus, that visits are confidential within the counselling team, and that going to a therapist should be treated as a normal part of life.
- She says it is a good idea to see a therapist when something cannot be resolved by talking to friends and family and cannot be set aside.
- She says therapy is not for everybody, and a person who can work through things without it does not need to go.
- She says any habit can be changed, but a person should change one because it is not helping them, not because it is supposed to be different.
- She says addictions are cultivated and can take over the good parts of life, and should be worked on one step at a time.

## Examples and cases used

- The marshmallow experiment: children who waited for a second marshmallow, used to show tolerating discomfort.
- The two wolves story: the wolf a person feeds is the one that grows.
