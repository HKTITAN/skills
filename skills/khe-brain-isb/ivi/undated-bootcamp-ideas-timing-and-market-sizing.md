# Finding an idea, timing and market sizing

Speaker and date are withheld. This note keeps general teaching points only and leaves out the speaker's own frameworks, examples and materials. It covers where ideas come from, being first against being much better, timing, learning from earlier attempts, sizing a market, validating with customers and the questions a founder should keep asking.

## What was said

### Where ideas come from

- The instructor names signals from global markets, emerging trends in India and white spaces as places to look for an idea.
- The instructor describes a white space as an area where a lot still needs to be done.
- The instructor says a founder should think early about whether they are building a first-to-market solution or a solution ten times better than what exists.

### Timing and being first

- The instructor says some ideas were tried in the past and failed because it was not the right time, and that the landscape sometimes changes drastically.
- The instructor says a founder has to work out what has changed in the last three to five years and why that makes this the right thing to do now.
- The instructor says being first to market has merits and demerits: sometimes the first company is creating the market and does not end up as the biggest player.
- The instructor says a founder who is first to market should be ready for the journey to be more brutal.

### Learning from earlier attempts

- The instructor says founder communities and LinkedIn are resources for finding out what others have already done.
- The instructor says a founder can reach out to founders who tried the same thing and failed, to understand what they learned and what to do differently.
- The instructor says a simple insight is sometimes what separates an attempt that works from an earlier one that failed.
- The instructor says that for a founder in a category with many players, such as a direct-to-consumer skincare brand, justifying the difference from others is very important and has to be proven hard.

### Sizing the market

- The instructor describes the total market as the total number of people who are interested in the product and might pay for it, including people the founder cannot reach directly right now.
- The instructor says taking the total population of India and applying a percentage cut is not good enough as a market size.
- The instructor warns that a weak answer on market size gets a founder written off on that slide.

### Validating with customers

- The instructor says validation has to happen before the brand is launched, because validating after launch wastes money.
- The instructor says data answers doubts about whether a founder is the right person for the business: if there is no revenue yet, the data is market insight.
- The instructor says having talked to 500 customers is itself an answer, because it shows the founder has taken the pain of reaching out.
- The instructor tells founders to record customer conversations with a note-taking tool and document them very deeply.
- The instructor says that at a trade show, where a founder talks to hundreds of people in two or three hours, a team member should write the conversations down as bullet points so nothing is missed later.
- The instructor says meeting notes should carry action items.
- The instructor advises founders who sell to hotels, restaurants and caterers to meet potential customers early and keep going back to them.

### What an investor wants to hear

- The instructor says an investor wants to hear something they do not already know, not what they already know.
- The instructor says that for a new category the founder has to bring primary data on customer behaviour.
- The instructor says a founder should acknowledge what they do not know yet and say which metrics they are tracking, because the choice of metric is itself an insight for the investor.

### Questions for the founder

- The instructor advises founders to question themselves every day on two things: internally why you, and externally why now.
- The instructor says a founder should list the four to six pillars the company needs to succeed, expect to be good at one or two of them, and work out how to cover the ones they are bad at.
- The instructor says experience builds only with age and a young founder cannot help that.
- The instructor says not to worry too much about competition in the early days.
- The instructor says every company goes through at least two to three near-death experiences, so founders should not worry too much about them.

## Numbers given

- Three to five years: the instructor's window for asking what has changed in a market.
- 500 customers: the instructor's example of an amount of customer conversation that answers doubts about the founder.
- Two to three: the instructor's count of near-death experiences every company goes through, as a minimum.
