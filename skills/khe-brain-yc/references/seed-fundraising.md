# Seed fundraising

This file answers when to raise a seed round, how much to raise and at what valuation, who to raise from, how to reach and pitch investors, how to build the deck, and what founders say went wrong. Geoff Ralston, Paul Graham, Brad Flora, Carolynn Levy, Michael Seibel, Dalton Caldwell, Aaron Harris and Kevin Hale speak in it, along with two posts signed Y Combinator. Money figures differ by person and by year, so every round size, valuation and dilution figure carries its speaker and its date wording, and differing figures sit side by side without being merged.

## When should I raise?

- Ralston says founders can raise once they can tell the story investors pay for: a compelling idea, a team that can carry it out, and an opportunity that is real and large enough. (A guide to seed fundraising, https://www.ycombinator.com/library/4A-a-guide-to-seed-fundraising)
- Ralston says a product investors can see or use is usually not enough, because they want to know there is product market fit and actual growth. (A guide to seed fundraising, https://www.ycombinator.com/library/4A-a-guide-to-seed-fundraising)
- Ralston calls growth of 10% per week for several weeks impressive, while saying the rate that counts as interesting depends. (A guide to seed fundraising, https://www.ycombinator.com/library/4A-a-guide-to-seed-fundraising)
- Graham's 2013 essay says the time to raise is when you can convince investors, and not when you need the money or when a deadline such as a Demo Day arrives. (How to Convince Investors, https://paulgraham.com/convince.html)
- Graham's 2013 essay tells founders to judge honestly whether the startup is worth investing in before raising, and not to try to raise if it is not. (How to Convince Investors, https://paulgraham.com/convince.html)
- Graham's 2013 essay says a three month old company at Demo Day only has to be a promising experiment, while a two year old company raising a series A has to show the experiment worked. (How to Convince Investors, https://paulgraham.com/convince.html)
- Flora says the best founders build a first version, even a toy-like one, get some users, and only then start thinking about raising. (How startup fundraising works, https://www.ycombinator.com/library/Il-how-startup-fundraising-works)
- Flora, in a talk the library dates to 2023, gives Solugen as the example: the founders built a reactor that fit on a desk, then one large enough to sell ten thousand dollars a month of hydrogen peroxide to hot tub supply stores, and raised four million dollars on that progress. (How startup fundraising works, https://www.ycombinator.com/library/Il-how-startup-fundraising-works)
- Seibel, in a talk the library dates to 2021, says that about 97 percent of the time a demo, a product, or an MVP with customers gives a founder far more leverage when fundraising. (Dalton & Michael: YC founders made these fundraising mistakes, https://www.ycombinator.com/library/DY-dalton-michael-yc-founders-made-these-fundraising-mistakes)
- Seibel says raising before you have tried to sell the product is often a fear-based decision by a founder who believes the product will fail and wants the money before investors can see that nobody wants it, and his verdict is "Not the best play." (Dalton & Michael: YC founders made these fundraising mistakes, https://www.ycombinator.com/library/DY-dalton-michael-yc-founders-made-these-fundraising-mistakes)
- Seibel reads a YC founder's note, in a talk the library dates to 2021, saying that with a startup that was not growing the founder spoke to 140 investors and got two angel checks, and with a growing one the seed round took one week. (Dalton & Michael: YC founders made these fundraising mistakes, https://www.ycombinator.com/library/DY-dalton-michael-yc-founders-made-these-fundraising-mistakes)

## How much should I raise, and at what valuation?

- Ralston says the ideal is to raise enough to reach profitability, so that you never have to raise again. (A guide to seed fundraising, https://www.ycombinator.com/library/4A-a-guide-to-seed-fundraising)
- Ralston says startups that will need a follow-on round, such as hardware companies, should raise enough to reach their next fundable milestone, usually 12 to 18 months later. (A guide to seed fundraising, https://www.ycombinator.com/library/4A-a-guide-to-seed-fundraising)
- Ralston, in an undated post, says giving up as little as 10% in a seed round is wonderful, most rounds require up to 20% dilution, and founders should try to avoid more than 25%. (A guide to seed fundraising, https://www.ycombinator.com/library/4A-a-guide-to-seed-fundraising)
- Flora, in a talk the library dates to 2023, says founders raising millions of dollars on SAFEs are selling only ten to twenty percent of the company. (How startup fundraising works, https://www.ycombinator.com/library/Il-how-startup-fundraising-works)
- Levy, in a talk the library dates to 2019, says ownership sold through convertible securities is hard to see because the holders are not yet stockholders, so founders must track it and have no excuse for being surprised that they sold 30% of the company to angels. (Modern startup funding, https://www.ycombinator.com/library/8F-modern-startup-funding)
- Ralston, in an undated post, gives a rule of thumb that an engineer costs about $15k per month all-in, so 18 months with an average of five engineers needs about $1.35mm. (A guide to seed fundraising, https://www.ycombinator.com/library/4A-a-guide-to-seed-fundraising)
- Ralston, in an undated post, says to answer the question of how much you are raising with a number of months, usually 12 to 18, and the amount that follows, usually between $500k and $1.5 million. (A guide to seed fundraising, https://www.ycombinator.com/library/4A-a-guide-to-seed-fundraising)
- Ralston, in an undated post, says early raises usually run from a few hundred thousand dollars up to two million, with most first rounds seeming to cluster around six hundred thousand and sizes rising over the last several years. (A guide to seed fundraising, https://www.ycombinator.com/library/4A-a-guide-to-seed-fundraising)
- Flora, in a talk the library dates to 2023, says a typical seed round is five hundred thousand to maybe a couple of million dollars, closes in weeks if not days, and carries no legal fees. (How startup fundraising works, https://www.ycombinator.com/library/Il-how-startup-fundraising-works)
- Levy, in a talk the library dates to 2019, describes the old way: a first round was a Series A preferred stock financing of about 1.5 to 2 million dollars that took months and could cost twenty-five to one hundred thousand dollars in legal fees. (Modern startup funding, https://www.ycombinator.com/library/8F-modern-startup-funding)
- Ralston says the amount asked for must be tied to a believable plan, and that it is usually a good idea to prepare several plans at different amounts and say the company succeeds either way, with speed of growth as the difference. (A guide to seed fundraising, https://www.ycombinator.com/library/4A-a-guide-to-seed-fundraising)
- Seibel reads a YC founder's note that says "Raise what you need and nothing more." because a company finds a way to spend whatever is in the bank. (Dalton & Michael: YC founders made these fundraising mistakes, https://www.ycombinator.com/library/DY-dalton-michael-yc-founders-made-these-fundraising-mistakes)
- Caldwell says companies pulled forward by customer revenue tend to own more of themselves at IPO or exit, and that having less money forces them to innovate. (Dalton & Michael: YC founders made these fundraising mistakes, https://www.ycombinator.com/library/DY-dalton-michael-yc-founders-made-these-fundraising-mistakes)
- Seibel says the biggest difference in how much of a company founders end up owning is how desperate they ever were to raise, and whether they raised with leverage. (Dalton & Michael: YC founders made these fundraising mistakes, https://www.ycombinator.com/library/DY-dalton-michael-yc-founders-made-these-fundraising-mistakes)
- Ralston says no formula gives a company's value, since companies are worth what investors were convinced they are worth, so it is best to let the market set the price and find an investor to set the price or cap. (A guide to seed fundraising, https://www.ycombinator.com/library/4A-a-guide-to-seed-fundraising)
- Ralston, in an undated post, says seed valuations tend to range from $2mm to $10mm, and that a high valuation does not raise the likelihood of success. (A guide to seed fundraising, https://www.ycombinator.com/library/4A-a-guide-to-seed-fundraising)
- The Y Combinator post says valuation does not equal success or even its probability, and names Airbnb, Dropbox and Twitch as companies that raised on tiny initial valuations. (YC's essential startup advice, https://www.ycombinator.com/library/4D-yc-s-essential-startup-advice)

## Who are the investors?

- Ralston says angels invest their own money on their own terms and VCs invest other people's, and that angels usually decide faster, alone, and with more emotion. (A guide to seed fundraising, https://www.ycombinator.com/library/4A-a-guide-to-seed-fundraising)
- Ralston says VCs usually take more time and more meetings, involve several partners in the final decision, and invest in very few of the many deals they see. (A guide to seed fundraising, https://www.ycombinator.com/library/4A-a-guide-to-seed-fundraising)
- Ralston, in an undated post, says a large number of independent angels will put anywhere from $25k to $100k or more into a single company. (A guide to seed fundraising, https://www.ycombinator.com/library/4A-a-guide-to-seed-fundraising)
- Levy, in a talk the library dates to 2019, says convertible rounds can end with 25 or 35 angels where six to ten investors were once usual, which she says is not bad but makes collecting stockholder consents hard after a priced round. (Modern startup funding, https://www.ycombinator.com/library/8F-modern-startup-funding)
- Levy says investors in convertible rounds tend to write smaller checks and care less because they are not yet stockholders, which she calls a double-edged sword. (Modern startup funding, https://www.ycombinator.com/library/8F-modern-startup-funding)
- Levy, answering a question in a talk the library dates to 2019, says VCs do not view small angel checks negatively when a founder later comes to them for a priced round. (Modern startup funding, https://www.ycombinator.com/library/8F-modern-startup-funding)
- Graham's 2013 essay says the best investors rarely care who else is investing while mediocre ones almost all do, so the question works as a test of investor quality. (How to Convince Investors, https://paulgraham.com/convince.html)
- Graham's 2013 essay says it is not uncommon for a startup to be rejected by every VC except the best ones, and gives Dropbox, which Boston investors saw first without closing and which raised a series A from Sequoia a couple of weeks later. (How to Convince Investors, https://paulgraham.com/convince.html)
- Flora says investors who see that you are making something people want do not care about your school, employer, friends or age, and calls them "coin-operated lizard people" who care more about making money. (How startup fundraising works, https://www.ycombinator.com/library/Il-how-startup-fundraising-works)
- Flora says to turn down anyone who offers to raise money on your behalf and ask them for an introduction, because founders should own the investor relationship themselves. (How startup fundraising works, https://www.ycombinator.com/library/Il-how-startup-fundraising-works)

## How do I reach investors and get a meeting?

- Ralston says that apart from a demo day, the best way to meet a VC or an angel is a warm introduction. (A guide to seed fundraising, https://www.ycombinator.com/library/4A-a-guide-to-seed-fundraising)
- Seibel says a cold email to an investor should take 60 seconds or less to read, because a longer one gets saved for later with no clear time when he returns to it. (How to cold email investors, https://www.ycombinator.com/library/65-how-to-cold-email-investors)
- Seibel says the email should state the problem, the solution, whether you have launched or have any growth, how big the market could be, whether you have co-founders and can write code, and anything you know that others would dispute. (How to cold email investors, https://www.ycombinator.com/library/65-how-to-cold-email-investors)
- Seibel says to drop jargon, because unlike a customer an investor may not know your industry's terms, and to use language any friend would follow. (How to cold email investors, https://www.ycombinator.com/library/65-how-to-cold-email-investors)
- Seibel says to send from a company address that carries your name, and not from an info address or an oddly formatted personal one, partly because investors use tools that look up the sender. (How to cold email investors, https://www.ycombinator.com/library/65-how-to-cold-email-investors)
- Seibel says attaching a deck is fine and not required, and that an attached deck should follow the format Silicon Valley investors are used to, which he says a search for Airbnb's fundraising deck will show. (How to cold email investors, https://www.ycombinator.com/library/65-how-to-cold-email-investors)
- Seibel says the first email should aim for a reply and a back-and-forth, and that founders reach for too much when it asks for a meeting, a call or an investment. (How to cold email investors, https://www.ycombinator.com/library/65-how-to-cold-email-investors)
- Seibel says not to ask for an in-person meeting straight away, because an investor who feels pushed into a meeting of unknown quality becomes uncomfortable. (How to cold email investors, https://www.ycombinator.com/library/65-how-to-cold-email-investors)
- Seibel says not to send several follow-ups quickly, since an investor who has opened a short email has already chosen when to reply. (How to cold email investors, https://www.ycombinator.com/library/65-how-to-cold-email-investors)
- Seibel says the most important rule is to say what the company does in the email, because offering to explain it only over an hour in person raises the bar for getting the meeting. (How to cold email investors, https://www.ycombinator.com/library/65-how-to-cold-email-investors)

## What do I say in the pitch?

- Graham's 2013 essay says inexperienced founders try to convince with the pitch, when most would do better to understand why the startup is worth investing in and explain that well. (How to Convince Investors, https://paulgraham.com/convince.html)
- Graham's 2013 essay says seeming like a future big success takes formidable founders, a promising market and, usually, some evidence of success so far. (How to Convince Investors, https://paulgraham.com/convince.html)
- Graham's 2013 essay says most investors decide in the first few minutes whether founders seem like winners, and that the opinion is hard to change once set. (How to Convince Investors, https://paulgraham.com/convince.html)
- Graham's 2013 essay warns first-time founders against imitating the swagger of experienced ones, because investors are good at judging confidence. (How to Convince Investors, https://paulgraham.com/convince.html)
- Graham's 2013 essay says an inexperienced founder seems most formidable by sticking to the truth, which means convincing yourself first. (How to Convince Investors, https://paulgraham.com/convince.html)
- Graham's 2013 essay says investors can tell fairly quickly whether you are a domain expert from how well you answer their questions. (How to Convince Investors, https://paulgraham.com/convince.html)
- Graham's 2013 essay says a convincing case names a specific trend, usually found by asking "why now?", with the ideal answer being that something changed recently and nobody else has noticed. (How to Convince Investors, https://paulgraham.com/convince.html)
- Flora says founders need to convince investors and not impress them, and that investors get bored when founders try to win them over. (How startup fundraising works, https://www.ycombinator.com/library/Il-how-startup-fundraising-works)
- Flora says convincing means making a product, getting it to users, and explaining in plain language how there is even a one percent chance it becomes huge. (How startup fundraising works, https://www.ycombinator.com/library/Il-how-startup-fundraising-works)
- Flora says that when investors are not investing, the cause is that the startup is not good enough yet, and the fix is to make the startup better. (How startup fundraising works, https://www.ycombinator.com/library/Il-how-startup-fundraising-works)
- Flora says Retool's founder had no deck, opened his laptop, built a small internal tool in minutes and talked about why early customers liked it, and that this convinced Flora to write a check. (How startup fundraising works, https://www.ycombinator.com/library/Il-how-startup-fundraising-works)
- Ralston says to cut the pitch to why the product is great, why you are the right team to build it, and why it could become a gigantic company. (A guide to seed fundraising, https://www.ycombinator.com/library/4A-a-guide-to-seed-fundraising)
- Ralston says the chance of a deal rises sharply if you can get the investor to talk more than you do. (A guide to seed fundraising, https://www.ycombinator.com/library/4A-a-guide-to-seed-fundraising)
- Ralston says investors seldom commit on the first day they hear a pitch, so the goal at an investor day is the next meeting. (A guide to seed fundraising, https://www.ycombinator.com/library/4A-a-guide-to-seed-fundraising)
- Ralston says never to leave an investor meeting without an attempted close or, at the least, clear next steps. (A guide to seed fundraising, https://www.ycombinator.com/library/4A-a-guide-to-seed-fundraising)

## What goes in the seed deck, and how do I design it?

- Harris, writing a week before the Winter 2018 Demo Day, says a seed deck should aim for clarity and concision, because most seed stage companies have little meaningful detail and pretending otherwise muddles the story. (How to build your seed round pitch deck, https://www.ycombinator.com/library/2u-how-to-build-your-seed-round-pitch-deck)
- Harris's template opens with a title slide carrying the company name and a one line description, and then runs through problem, solution, traction, what makes you special, business model, market, team, and the money you need. (How to build your seed round pitch deck, https://www.ycombinator.com/library/2u-how-to-build-your-seed-round-pitch-deck)
- Harris says every slide after the title is the first of a possible set, that one slide per set is ideal, and advises against any set longer than three in a seed deck. (How to build your seed round pitch deck, https://www.ycombinator.com/library/2u-how-to-build-your-seed-round-pitch-deck)
- Harris says a traction slide should make the numbers clear and meaningful, and that revenue would be the better figure on a second metrics slide, though another metric is acceptable. (How to build your seed round pitch deck, https://www.ycombinator.com/library/2u-how-to-build-your-seed-round-pitch-deck)
- Harris says the team slide matters a great deal at seed and should be about the founders and why they suit the problem, adding "Nobody cares about your advisors." (How to build your seed round pitch deck, https://www.ycombinator.com/library/2u-how-to-build-your-seed-round-pitch-deck)
- Harris says the last slide states how much money you need and what it buys, and that showing where you will be within a year, at a point that makes you Series A ready, is powerful. (How to build your seed round pitch deck, https://www.ycombinator.com/library/2u-how-to-build-your-seed-round-pitch-deck)
- Ralston says a deck has no fixed format or order, and lists the parts usually present: company and tag line, vision, problem, customer, solution, market, landscape, traction, business model, team, summary and fundraising. (A guide to seed fundraising, https://www.ycombinator.com/library/4A-a-guide-to-seed-fundraising)
- Ralston says the deck should work as a leave-behind, and that graphics, charts and screenshots are more powerful than many words. (A guide to seed fundraising, https://www.ycombinator.com/library/4A-a-guide-to-seed-fundraising)
- Hale, writing about Demo Day slides, says he usually hates screenshots because they are almost always illegible, complex and non-obvious, and prefers a bulleted list of steps to show what a product does. (How to design a better pitch deck, https://www.ycombinator.com/library/4T-how-to-design-a-better-pitch-deck)
- Hale says he works with each company to pick the 5 to 7 ideas investors should remember, and that among 100 other presenting companies a founder is lucky if investors recall 1 or 2. (How to design a better pitch deck, https://www.ycombinator.com/library/4T-how-to-design-a-better-pitch-deck)
- Hale's rule for Demo Day slides is that they must be legible, simple and obvious, because nobody can remember what they did not understand. (How to design a better pitch deck, https://www.ycombinator.com/library/4T-how-to-design-a-better-pitch-deck)
- Hale says legible means large, bold type in a simple font with good contrast, placed at the top of the slide where the back of the room can read it. (How to design a better pitch deck, https://www.ycombinator.com/library/4T-how-to-design-a-better-pitch-deck)
- Hale says a simple slide expresses one idea, so a Demo Day deck ideally has only about 5 to 7 slides. (How to design a better pitch deck, https://www.ycombinator.com/library/4T-how-to-design-a-better-pitch-deck)
- Hale's test for an obvious slide is to show it to a stranger and see whether they state your idea immediately. (How to design a better pitch deck, https://www.ycombinator.com/library/4T-how-to-design-a-better-pitch-deck)
- Hale compares diagrams to mazes that usually make the path to the idea too long, and lists animations, transitions, memes and too much text among the distractions to remove. (How to design a better pitch deck, https://www.ycombinator.com/library/4T-how-to-design-a-better-pitch-deck)
- Hale says investors back teams and not slides, so slides should not pull attention from what the founder is saying. (How to design a better pitch deck, https://www.ycombinator.com/library/4T-how-to-design-a-better-pitch-deck)

## How do I run the process and close?

- Flora says real fundraising is a grind of one-on-one coffee chats and Zoom meetings repeated many times, and that pitch competitions and Shark Tank are for show. (How startup fundraising works, https://www.ycombinator.com/library/Il-how-startup-fundraising-works)
- Flora, in a talk the library dates to 2023, reports that Freshpaint met 160 investors, got a yes from 39, took checks from five thousand to 200 thousand dollars, and needed four months and 18 days to raise 1.6 million dollars. (How startup fundraising works, https://www.ycombinator.com/library/Il-how-startup-fundraising-works)
- Ralston says to meet as many investors as possible while focusing on the ones most likely to close, and to optimize for getting money soonest. (A guide to seed fundraising, https://www.ycombinator.com/library/4A-a-guide-to-seed-fundraising)
- Ralston says investors are usually the more experienced negotiators, so a founder should take requests away and get help from advisors or counsel. (A guide to seed fundraising, https://www.ycombinator.com/library/4A-a-guide-to-seed-fundraising)
- Ralston says not to delay after a yes: get the documents signed and the money into the bank as soon as possible. (A guide to seed fundraising, https://www.ycombinator.com/library/4A-a-guide-to-seed-fundraising)
- The Y Combinator post says to raise as quickly as possible and get back to work, noting that a flattening growth curve often shows when a company is fundraising. (YC's essential startup advice, https://www.ycombinator.com/library/4D-yc-s-essential-startup-advice)
- The Y Combinator post says the money raised is not the founder's money, and that founders have a fiduciary and ethical duty to spend it only on improving the company's prospects. (YC's essential startup advice, https://www.ycombinator.com/library/4D-yc-s-essential-startup-advice)
- Levy says the point of modern early financing is focus: with little to negotiate, the money arrives fast and the founder returns to building the company. (Modern startup funding, https://www.ycombinator.com/library/8F-modern-startup-funding)
- Ralston says investors have many ways of saying no, and that a founder should recognise a refusal and part on good terms because the same investor may say yes another time. (A guide to seed fundraising, https://www.ycombinator.com/library/4A-a-guide-to-seed-fundraising)
- Graham's 2013 essay says the most common investor question is "who else is investing?", and that implying other investors are about to commit is a tactic he does not recommend to most founders because they could not carry it off. (How to Convince Investors, https://paulgraham.com/convince.html)
- Graham's 2013 essay says the best response to earlier rejections is to explain openly why investors turned you down and why they are mistaken. (How to Convince Investors, https://paulgraham.com/convince.html)
- Flora says investors will reject a startup however good the product and traction are, and that a founder needs only a few of them to believe. (How startup fundraising works, https://www.ycombinator.com/library/Il-how-startup-fundraising-works)
- Flora, in a talk the library dates to 2023, says the founder of Envision was rejected more than fifty times before a first check of twenty-five thousand dollars, in a first round of five hundred thousand dollars. (How startup fundraising works, https://www.ycombinator.com/library/Il-how-startup-fundraising-works)

## What mistakes do founders report?

- Seibel says a common failure path is putting the investor at the center in place of the customer, as if the investor were a teacher and the money were the grade. (Dalton & Michael: YC founders made these fundraising mistakes, https://www.ycombinator.com/library/DY-dalton-michael-yc-founders-made-these-fundraising-mistakes)
- Seibel suggests early-stage founders audit last week's hours: 80 to 90 percent spent talking to customers and building product is probably right, and something near 20 percent probably means something is very wrong. (Dalton & Michael: YC founders made these fundraising mistakes, https://www.ycombinator.com/library/DY-dalton-michael-yc-founders-made-these-fundraising-mistakes)
- Ralston's list of things not to do with investors includes creating detailed financials and using market size numbers without clear justification. (A guide to seed fundraising, https://www.ycombinator.com/library/4A-a-guide-to-seed-fundraising)
- Ralston's list says not to try playing investors off against each other unless you are highly skilled at fundraising. (A guide to seed fundraising, https://www.ycombinator.com/library/4A-a-guide-to-seed-fundraising)
- Ralston's list says not to over-optimize valuation or worry too much about dilution. (A guide to seed fundraising, https://www.ycombinator.com/library/4A-a-guide-to-seed-fundraising)
- Graham's 2013 essay says founders who are not convinced themselves will not convince investors, unless they are good con artists, because investors detect bullshit better than founders produce it. (How to Convince Investors, https://paulgraham.com/convince.html)
- Graham's 2013 essay says vague, grandiose marketing language does not work on investors and reads as a mark of incompetence. (How to Convince Investors, https://paulgraham.com/convince.html)
- Hale says a very common mistake is packing every nuance of the business into one slide, which leaves a slide nobody can easily remember. (How to design a better pitch deck, https://www.ycombinator.com/library/4T-how-to-design-a-better-pitch-deck)

## Which documents and what data room do I need?

- Ralston says not to spend much time on diligence documents for a seed round, and that an investor asking for too much diligence or financials is almost certainly one to avoid. (A guide to seed fundraising, https://www.ycombinator.com/library/4A-a-guide-to-seed-fundraising)
- Ralston says the executive summary should be one or two pages, one being better, covering vision, product, team, traction, market size and minimum financials. (A guide to seed fundraising, https://www.ycombinator.com/library/4A-a-guide-to-seed-fundraising)
- The Y Combinator post defines a data room as a collection of documents that is easy to share with investors, and says it is commonly used in Series A and later rounds. (Data rooms and additional documents to prepare for seed fundraising, https://www.ycombinator.com/library/Da-data-rooms-and-additional-documents-to-prepare-for-seed-fundraising)
- The Y Combinator post says most companies do not need a data room to raise a seed round. (Data rooms and additional documents to prepare for seed fundraising, https://www.ycombinator.com/library/Da-data-rooms-and-additional-documents-to-prepare-for-seed-fundraising)
- The Y Combinator post names two cases where one can make sense at seed: a hard-tech, biotech or life science company, or a company with a lot of traction raising a large round from large investors. (Data rooms and additional documents to prepare for seed fundraising, https://www.ycombinator.com/library/Da-data-rooms-and-additional-documents-to-prepare-for-seed-fundraising)
- The Y Combinator post says a data room at the seed or Series A stage is typically a Google Drive or Dropbox folder, with a separate folder per investor to track what was sent to whom. (Data rooms and additional documents to prepare for seed fundraising, https://www.ycombinator.com/library/Da-data-rooms-and-additional-documents-to-prepare-for-seed-fundraising)
- The Y Combinator post says that when an investor asks for a data room it is fine to ask which documents they want, and to send only what fits the stage of the conversation. (Data rooms and additional documents to prepare for seed fundraising, https://www.ycombinator.com/library/Da-data-rooms-and-additional-documents-to-prepare-for-seed-fundraising)
- The Y Combinator post lists business materials to consider: signed enterprise deals, monthly historical financial statements, financial projections, and metrics more detailed than the deck, which can also sit in a deck appendix. (Data rooms and additional documents to prepare for seed fundraising, https://www.ycombinator.com/library/Da-data-rooms-and-additional-documents-to-prepare-for-seed-fundraising)
- The Y Combinator post lists legal materials such as the certificate of incorporation and bylaws, the cap table, employee agreements and signed SAFEs, and says it is often best to wait until someone asks for them. (Data rooms and additional documents to prepare for seed fundraising, https://www.ycombinator.com/library/Da-data-rooms-and-additional-documents-to-prepare-for-seed-fundraising)
