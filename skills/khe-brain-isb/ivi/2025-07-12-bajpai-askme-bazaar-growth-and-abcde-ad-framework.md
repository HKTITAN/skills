# Askme Bazaar growth and the ABCDE ad framework, Amardeep Bajpai

This is the second part of day two of Amardeep Bajpai's growth hacker bootcamp. He finished the Askme Bazaar case with its acquisition channels, weekly shopping festivals, results and collapse, and drew a lesson about taking almost all funding from one large investor. He then taught ABCDE, his five-part test for an advertisement, and the room played ads and judged them against it.

## What was said

### Askme Bazaar: channels and weekly festivals

- Bajpai says the team spread acquisition marketing across Google, Facebook, Twitter and other platforms and did not rely on one.
- He says a large part of Google's revenue comes from ads shown on other websites that carry its AdSense code, and that sites outside that network are reached through supply-side and demand-side platforms, which his team was among the first in India to use.
- He says the point of many platforms is wider reach, and that spend must be measured wherever it goes to see which platform and which kind of ad performs better.
- He says the team analysed everything every Saturday and made changes on Monday.
- He says Indians shop most around festivals, with peaks before Diwali and before Valentine's Day.
- He says the team copied that pattern by creating a shopping festival on the site every week, with its own banner, items and discounts.
- He says acquisition advertising was built around each festival and sent buyers to it on the site, which made a loop that fuelled a large part of the growth.
- He says a Valentine's campaign that rewarded top shoppers ran for 30 days and brought in more revenue than the company had made since it was founded.
- He says small tweaks in the funnel from constant analysis also played a large role.

### Askme Bazaar: results and what went wrong

- Bajpai says the site reached conversion rates above 7%, meaning at least seven of every hundred visitors placed an order.
- He says the company closed the year at a gross merchandise value of about 4,800 crore rupees, against about 9,500 crore rupees that he says Flipkart filed the same year.
- He says gross merchandise value is not revenue, and that revenue is about 10 to 20% of it.
- He says the company grew faster than it could service, and management should have put a brake on growth.
- He says the company could not manage refunds well when many buyers disliked the products.
- He says the investors then put a lot of pressure on the founders, and there was a clash between two ideologies.
- He says the investor chose to write off about 400 million dollars.

### One large investor against a group of venture funds

- Bajpai says almost all of the roughly 400 million dollars came from a single wealthy group, and that this was a big problem for the company.
- He says a group such as Tata is not an investor looking for an exit after ten years. It deploys its own capital, builds a business and takes it to an IPO.
- He says large venture funds typically hold from 500 million dollars to a few billion and have to deploy all of it.
- He says venture funds are too small to join a company that a very wealthy group funds almost entirely, so other investors stayed away.
- He says such groups are instead the final buyers that venture funds sell to, as when Flipkart was sold to Walmart.
- He says one investor taking a very big position in a company is not a good idea, especially in the early days.
- He says raising from a group of venture funds leaves the promoter in charge because the investors hold diverse views, and good funds advise having more investors.

### The ABCDE test for an ad

- Bajpai says ABCDE stands for attention, brand element, comprehension, duration or dimension, and emotion.
- He says to think in roughly that order, except that emotion ranks ahead of duration and was placed last only to fit the letters.
- He says the brand should be present throughout the message, and that about 70% of the ads he sees on YouTube never show the brand on screen until the end.
- He says viewers tune out or press skip before the end, so a brand shown only at the end is lost.
- He agrees with the view that an ad should not look like an ad in the first five seconds, and says to win attention first and then let subconscious branding, such as the brand's colours, work.
- He says an ad has lost the plot when viewers remember it but cannot say which brand made it.
- He says comprehension must be easy. The viewer should not have to work out the message, unless a brain teaser is itself the attention device.
- He says an ad that does not tell a good end-to-end story must be short, under 20 seconds.
- He says that in small formats the main message must stand out clearly, because people cannot absorb a lot of information at once.
- He says every ad should evoke an emotion, such as fun, laughter, fear or sadness, because people remember feelings more than facts.
- He says longer video ads cost more to show, so a short version or a GIF of the same message lowers the cost per thousand impressions.
- He says most consumers lack the energy or inclination to work at discovering a company.

### Judging ads in the room

- Bajpai says an ad with a very large view count and very few likes was mostly boosted, and that most viewers did not watch it to the end.
- He says an ad becomes a good one when the brand is named in the first couple of seconds, and that almost every modern ad reveals the brand early.
- He says anyone who claims an early brand reveal hurts the story is not being careful enough, because the reveal and the story can be woven together.
- He says the CRED ads work because they use a proven celebrity and show that celebrity in contrast to their image.
- He says an ad that becomes a talking point can be paired with PR before and at launch, and he puts the gain at 20 to 30 times the ad's own impact.
- He says any ad that creates a controversy is good for the brand, because an apology then brings both attention and sympathy, but it works only a couple of times before credibility suffers.
- He says the Coca-Cola campaign with Aamir Khan correlated the brand with the category, and that once the price was comparable people started asking for Coca-Cola at tea and paan shops.
- He says that campaign brought a new regional version every three to six months, and that Pepsi had been the dominant brand before it.
- He says older ads were made for a captive TV audience and should be judged with that in mind, while the same ad made now should show the brand on screen early.
- He says an ad must be judged by whether its target audience finds it interesting, not by whether the reviewer does.
- He calls a used-car ad featuring Sachin Tendulkar a poor ad: comprehension fails, the setting is foreign to Indian buyers, and a one-of-a-kind celebrity does not fit mass second-hand cars.
- He says recruitment ads aimed at potential employees also do a fairly good job with potential customers.

## Examples and cases used

- Askme Bazaar: weekly on-site shopping festivals tied to acquisition ads, very fast growth, then collapse over servicing, refunds and an investor dispute.
- Flipkart and Walmart: his example of a venture-backed company sold to a very large group.
- Tata: his example of a group that builds and lists businesses and does not seek an investor's exit.
- Zepto ad: short, clear, entertaining, and it ends on a simple screen.
- CRED ads: a celebrity shown against type holds attention.
- Coca-Cola's campaign with Aamir Khan: brand correlated with the category, at a 5 rupee price point, in regional versions.
- Mokobara ad with Diljit Dosanjh: he says it conveys a clear product benefit and gained from controversy, and he asks if the ambassador fits the audience.
- Spinny ads with Sachin Tendulkar: his example of a poor ad.
- Airtel ad: the brand appears early on a phone screen within the story.
- Liquid Death: played as the closing example.

## Numbers given

- Above 7%: Askme Bazaar's conversion rate, in Bajpai's telling.
- About 4,800 crore rupees: Askme Bazaar's gross merchandise value for the year, against about 9,500 crore rupees he attributes to Flipkart for the same year.
- 10 to 20% of gross merchandise value: his rule for an e-commerce company's revenue.
- About 400 million dollars: the funding he says came almost entirely from one investor and was written off.
- 500 million to a few billion dollars: his range for the size of large venture funds.
- About 70%: the share of YouTube ads he says do not show the brand on screen until the end.
- Under 20 seconds: his limit for an ad that does not tell a story.
- 20 to 30 times: the lift he says PR can add to an ad that becomes a talking point.
