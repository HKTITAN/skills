# Pitch deck bootcamp, the gap between founder and investor, Mohit Bansal

The opening stretch of a full day pitch deck bootcamp led by Mohit Bansal of DeckRooster. He first collects the cohort's difficulties with pitching, then argues that a pitch is an argument aimed at what investors think of the business, and sets a worksheet exercise in which founders mark where they stand and where investors stand on five questions. This recording covers the first part of the day, up to the start of the exercise.

## What was said

### What a pitch deck is

- Mohit Bansal has the room define the term and settles on this: the deck is a communication tool, and what it communicates is the pitch.
- He describes the pitch as a cohesive argument for why someone should invest in your business.
- He says most founders start from a template, defining the problem, then the solution, then market size, then go to market, without first thinking about what investors currently think of their business.
- He says the things founders blame, such as another deck having better metrics, a photo or a story, are not the real reasons a pitch is held back.
- He says his firm has no templates and has never used any.
- On crediting sources for borrowed videos or content, he says investor decks are private presentations that are not put out publicly, so founders need not worry much about it.
- When a founder describes showing conviction while privately treating the numbers as assumptions, he replies that conviction is something you feel deep down, and if you do not feel it, it is not conviction.

### What investors are assessing

- He says a founder is selling something to an investor, so the founder has to know what drives the investor and line the pitch up with that.
- He says what follows is his firm's own way of seeing it and not a universal rule.
- He reduces what investors look for to two broad things: how much potential the opportunity has, and how capable the business is of extracting that potential.
- He places the founders under capability, not under potential.
- He says the earlier the stage, the more weight falls on showing the potential of the opportunity, and he keeps the day's focus there.
- He says scalability does not ensure the return an investor wants, but without it that return is harder to reach.
- He says an investor who does not understand your industry cannot judge your potential however good the pitch, so helping them understand the industry is part of the founder's job.
- He adds that an investor's own abilities can count on the capability side, since founder and investor could extract the potential together.

### The gap the pitch has to close

- He says a pitch sometimes fails because the investor places the opportunity's potential, or the business's capability, at a different level from the founder.
- Closing that gap is what the pitch is for. Once a founder has worked out how to close it, the deck communicates the answer.
- If founder and investor already agree, he says, there is no work to do.
- If both see low potential, the only question left is whether that potential is enough for a venture fundable startup.

### Three questions and the worksheet

- He brings investor questions down to three: why invest in this kind of company, why invest right now, and why invest in my company.
- He says the same three apply to selling a product: why buy this kind of thing, why buy now, and why buy mine.
- He says "why now" is very important, and that many B2B sales do not happen because there is no urgency.
- His worksheet asks about confidence in what the business does today, confidence in future product market fit, whether it can be massively profitable in the long term, and whether it will be among the winners in the short term and in the long term.
- He stresses "among the winners". He is not asking founders to show they will be the single winner.
- He says he is not looking for profitability in the short term, only for whether the business can be massively profitable in the long term, and that unit economics belongs under that question.
- Each question has a five step scale from strongly believing it will not happen, through neutral, to strongly believing it will.
- Founders mark where they think investors stand and where they themselves stand. He says he is mainly interested in why the gap exists.
- To a founder who has not pitched enough to know what investors think, he says finding out is part of the job, and until then to assume the worst.
- Founders fill the sheet for their own startup first, then exchange in groups so that others can say whether the self assessment is right.

## Examples and cases used

- A bet on a car race: he asks the room why they would back one participant over another, to draw out how an investor judges capability. The recording loses most of the exchange.

## Not covered

- Slide by slide structure, design, and how many slides a deck should have. He declines to give a template.
- How much to ask for, financial projections by stage, and investor update emails. Founders raised these and he parked them for later in the day.
- The results of the worksheet exercise. The recording ends as it begins.
