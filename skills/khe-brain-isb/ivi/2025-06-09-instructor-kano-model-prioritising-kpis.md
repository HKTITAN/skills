# Kano model for prioritising KPIs

The first part of an operations management class, about 70 minutes, taught by the course instructor, who is not named in the recording. He recaps the CTQ tree from the previous session, then teaches the Kano model through three stories and takes questions. The class then breaks before a session on process improvement.

## What was said

### Choosing the right KPIs

- The instructor says the voice of the customer is vague. People say they want great donuts, great mileage, great safety or a low price, and none of that can be measured as stated.
- He says the CTQ tree from the previous session is the tool that removes this vagueness and turns the voice of the customer into key performance indicators.
- A good KPI is easy and clear for a front-line employee to understand. His example is "make 50 donuts at 9 am, 50 at 12 noon, 50 at 4 pm". It also has to be comparable.
- He says organisations fall into the trap of reporting red, amber and green dashboards to top management without asking whether they measure the right KPIs.
- With inappropriate KPIs, he says, the dashboard looks green most of the time while the company loses customers and never learns why.
- After the CTQ tree a manager holds a laundry list of 40 to 50 KPIs, and common sense says improve whatever is amber or red.
- He says that asking customers what to improve does not help, because a customer given a choice asks for everything. His example is a Swiggy customer who wants variety, delivery in five minutes, great taste and all of it free.
- A firm has limited people, limited time and limited money, so the question a manager must answer is which KPIs to improve first.

### The three kinds of characteristic

- The instructor says products and services have characteristics, just as people do, and that these fall into three types.
- A dissatisfier is a characteristic that adds no new satisfaction when present but causes dissatisfaction when absent. His example is an ATM. A customer who gets cash feels only neutral, and a customer with a valid card, PIN and balance who gets no cash panics.
- A satisfier has a linear relation to satisfaction. The more the characteristic performs, the happier the customer, and the less it performs, the less happy.
- His satisfier example is a diagnostic lab that promises blood test reports by evening. Reports that arrive in 15 minutes delight the customer, reports after two hours still please, and reports after two days and two calls to customer care leave the customer dissatisfied.
- A delighter makes the customer very happy when given, because it comes as a pleasant surprise, and changes nothing in the customer's impression when it is not given.
- His delighter example is Citibank calling a credit card holder on her birthday to give five times the reward points on every swipe that day.
- He says delighters are the exact opposite of dissatisfiers. One creates no new happiness when present, the other creates no new dissatisfaction when absent.
- He says dissatisfiers are also called must-be quality.
- He names the framework as the Kano model.

### Using the three baskets

- Sorting KPIs by these definitions replaces a list of 50 or 60 KPIs with three baskets.
- The instructor's priority order for improvement is dissatisfiers first, satisfiers second, delighters third.
- He advises not listing all KPIs together on a dashboard. Classify them as dissatisfiers, satisfiers and delighters and look at the dissatisfiers first.
- If any dissatisfier KPI shows red or amber, he says the firm should start a process improvement project at once, because the customer's basic needs are not met and the firm has a survival problem.
- He says all dissatisfiers green means only that the firm can survive in the market.
- Dissatisfiers and satisfiers all green means competitive parity, in his reading.
- All three baskets green is when a firm gets competitive advantage.
- He draws the conclusion that process improvement is not only an internal operations matter. It can produce competitive advantage, and he calls connecting operations to strategy operational excellence.

### Three implications of the model

- The model is not static. He uses mobile phones to show it. A phone you could carry while walking was once a wow, then a music player, then a camera, and today nobody buying a phone asks whether it has a camera or Wi-Fi.
- He says a delighter does not stay a delighter. Over time it becomes a satisfier and eventually a dissatisfier. A customer who got the birthday call last year will be upset if it does not come this year.
- His recommendation, speaking as a consultant, is to redo the classification every half year, because the KPIs in each basket change over time.
- Giving a delighter sets an expectation. He says a firm that cannot repeat it next year can lose a customer it would have kept had it never given the delighter.
- Before giving a delighter, he says, assess whether you can sustain it for at least a few iterations. Creating a habit in the customer's mind is "a double edged sword".
- The third implication is that a delighter disrupts the market. The delighted customer tells her friends, a friend who holds a card from HDFC asks why her own bank gives nothing like it, and she thinks about switching.
- He says the real target of a delighter is not the loyal customer who receives it but the people she talks to. In his words the firm has "hijacked" the competitor's customers.
- He adds that in the digital world the cost of switching provider is almost zero.

### Questions from the class

- Asked whether a delighter must be a surprise, he says no. It must meet a hidden need, something the customer likes but would never have asked for.
- He says a firm need not balance its KPI performance. Which basket a KPI falls in depends on the company's value indicator, so time can be a dissatisfier for one company and a delighter for another.
- His examples are Domino's, where speed is what matters, and a Maruti Suzuki buyer, who has already made a trade-off in their mind at a price.
- He says customer satisfaction is a proxy for revenue, either from that customer or from the customers acquired through them.
- Once a delighter is out in the market, he says, it stops being a delighter for competitors and becomes an obligation, because they must give it to protect their own customers.
- He calls this a strategy. By launching a delighter a firm makes its competitor spend money in a direction the firm has chosen.
- He says the timing of launching an innovation matters. The market entry of an innovation is a strategy as important as the market entry of the product or the firm.
- On customers or employees who want to leave, his rule is never to make a stakeholder feel uncomfortable in any encounter with you, including the exit.
- He says making an exit difficult is a bad sign, because unhappy customers also talk to people. A comfortable exit leaves a seed of the brand in the person's mind.
- He applies the same rule inside the firm. A manager should not hold a gun to an employee's head with policy, because how an employee leaves is employer branding.
- Asked for a framework to create such experiences, he says management is both a science and an art, and there is no framework for the art. A book by an Olympic gold medallist does not teach you to swim. Being pushed into the pool does.

## Examples and cases used

- ATM cash withdrawal: a dissatisfier, neutral when it works and panic when it fails.
- Diagnostic lab report time: a satisfier, where satisfaction moves in line with speed.
- Citibank birthday call with five times reward points: a delighter, and later the example of a delighter turning into an expectation.
- HDFC credit card: the competitor whose customer hears about the delighter and considers switching.
- Nokia 1100 and later phones: features that were once delighters are now taken for granted.
- Swiggy: a customer asked what to improve asks for everything at once.
- Domino's and Maruti Suzuki: firms and buyers trade off some KPIs for others, so KPIs need not be balanced.

## Numbers given

- 40 to 50 KPIs: the instructor's figure for the laundry list a manager holds after a CTQ tree exercise.
- Every half year: how often he recommends redoing the Kano classification.
- Five times reward points: the birthday offer in his Citibank example.

## Not covered

- How to survey customers to place a characteristic in a Kano category. The instructor says design thinking has frameworks for finding hidden needs but he does not cover them.
- The CTQ tree itself, which was taught in the previous session.
- Process improvement, which starts after the break.
