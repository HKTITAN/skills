# Negotiation class on context, agents and lying

The negotiation professor, who is not named in the recording, ran the second to last session of a six-session course. She debriefed a property-sale role play that the transcript renders as Boulard or Bullard Houses, taught how to work with agents using a three-part training video by Professor Susskind of MIT, and then taught lying and lie detection. The class ended with students starting a dispute resolution case.

## What was said

### Analyse the context first

- The instructor says each negotiation is independent: the frameworks are the same but the context is different.
- She says the previous negotiation tends to shape how people approach the next one, and that this is a mistake.
- She says context comes first and BATNA, reservation price and target price follow from it.
- She says part of the context is who you are in the negotiation. An agent has to ask what decision-making authority they hold.
- She says people focus too much on numbers because numbers are easier to process, and in many business negotiations the other issues matter more.
- She says information that is easy to process is not necessarily the most important information.
- She says to plan many hypothetical scenarios before a negotiation.
- She gives five times as a guide for preparation: a negotiation expected to last a month may need five months of preparation.
- She says a negotiation is full of questions, so preparation includes deciding how you will answer questions about your alternatives, your competitor offers and your future plans.
- She says negotiating means asking a lot of questions and listening.

### The property case: when no deal is the right outcome

- In the property case she says the price ranges overlapped, but the interests did not: the seller would not allow the houses to be torn down for commercial use and the buyer wanted a hotel.
- She says a deal in that case only happened when one side misrepresented its plans.
- She says the aim is not to make a deal but to make a deal that satisfies your interests.
- Her advice is "Don't be afraid to walk away", especially when your BATNA is better.
- She says once you find that your interests are not being met you should walk away, even though walking away feels like a loss.
- She says an agent has to think about the client's interests first.
- She says a buyer's representative in that case should have prepared answers to questions about confidentiality and intended use, and the seller's representative should have prepared to ask them.
- She says an agent can answer a confidentiality question by saying they are not authorised to reveal it, and that this is one reason people hire agents.
- She says when a counterpart agrees to every term without asking a question, that is a signal to ask more.

### Limited-time offers

- She calls a now-or-never offer the scarcity principle: people assign more value to something available for a limited time.
- She says to ask yourself whether you are taking the offer because it is limited or because it is good for you.
- She says to decide before the negotiation what package you would accept on the spot.
- She says if you ask for more time you should know what you will do with it, such as getting more data.

### First offers, stories and framing

- She says when the other side makes the first offer she treats it as an opportunity to be prepared for, because everyone is affected by an anchor.
- She says an aggressive first offer has to come with a justification, because people need a reason for a number.
- She describes an experiment in which a person asking to jump a queue for a printer was allowed ahead far more often when the request included the word because.
- She says an aggressive offer is acceptable but a ridiculous offer makes it likely that no deal happens.
- She cites a recent paper in the Quarterly Journal of Economics, which she had read only in summary, as finding that people remembered stories long after they had forgotten numbers.
- She tells students to give a story before quoting a number, including when asked for their last drawn salary.
- She says people find information more believable when it is vivid and coherent, and they remember negative information more.
- She explains prospect theory as losses looming larger than gains.
- She says people are more risk seeking when information is framed as a loss and more risk averse when it is framed as a gain, so a risky proposal should be framed as a loss the other side avoids.
- Her example of vividness is a household electricity bill that changed behaviour only after consumption above the average was colour coded.

### Working with agents

- She lists the advantages of agents: they are emotionally neutral, they have more expertise, and they have more data because they operate in that market.
- She lists the disadvantages: information is lost at each layer, the agent's goals can differ from the principal's, and the agent's fee makes the zone of possible agreement smaller.
- She says an agent who is paid only when a deal closes will close the deal, even when it harms the principal.
- Her example is a rental agent paid one month's rent, who gains from a higher rent. She says to tie the fee to your own interests, such as the size, budget and timeline you want.
- She suggests a tiered bonus for a sales agent: a set amount for hitting the target and more for going beyond it.
- She says the first negotiation is with your own agent, before the agent meets the other side.
- She says to make the agent's authority clear: what they can decide, where they must check back, and what your interests and priorities are.
- She says grey areas in an agent's mandate are normal and acceptable, because professional agents are experts and should not be over-constrained.
- She says an agent's loyalty lies with the principal.
- She says employees are agents too: once a startup grows, employees negotiate on the founder's behalf.
- She says trust is a very important currency in the market and agents should be transparent about what they keep.

### Lessons from the principal and agent video

- She points out that the principal in the video states the priority five times, and says a principal has to communicate priorities that carefully.
- She points out that the agent is assertive but never aggressive, and tells his boss that the two goals pull against each other.
- She notes that the agent does not promise a result. He says he will test the waters and check back.
- She says the boss in the video refuses to think about alternatives, and that it is not a good idea to have no BATNA.
- She says companies bring in a third party such as a procurement consultant when the usual contact has become a friend of the vendor and can no longer negotiate hard.
- She explains the solution in the video: work out why the consultant is there and what his incentive is, satisfy that interest, and get him out of the picture.
- In the video the agent meets the consultant's margin target by reducing scope and removing free extras, sets a fixed rate for extra work, and plans to renegotiate with the client's vice president afterwards.
- She says business involves trading off risk and reward, and sometimes a deal is taken at a lower price in return for a better one later.
- She recommends Susskind's book Entrepreneurial Negotiation and the book Negotiation Genius.

### Lying by the other side

- She says lying, bluffing and misrepresentation are very common in negotiation, and cites a survey in which 81% of respondents admitted to lying.
- She describes her own experiment in which students over-reported how many maths problems they solved when the only reward was being named in the top three.
- She says that when everyone assumes the other side lies, even a truthful person is treated as a liar.
- She says to reduce the other side's temptation to lie by not asking direct questions about their alternatives or their best price.
- She says to look prepared and have data, because people lie more easily to someone who has not done their homework.
- She says once a person tells one lie they are likely to tell another.
- She says to get everything in writing, because people get away with lies that are not written down.
- She says telling the other side that you trust them because of their record reduces their temptation to lie.
- She says the signs of lying are speech hesitation, avoiding eye contact, inconsistencies, vagueness and answering a different question from the one asked.
- She says to ask follow-up questions and to ask the same question again, because an unprepared liar contradicts themselves.
- She says to propose a contingent contract when a claim is about the future and you cannot verify it.
- She says offering packages and asking why one is preferred shows whether the other side lied about its priorities.
- She says to test your own assumptions and data before confronting someone, because a wrong accusation spoils the relationship.
- She says asking someone whether they are lying only produces another lie.
- She says that once you are sure the other side is lying you can walk away, or you can use the knowledge to your advantage without showing it.
- She says that if you lose a deal you should go back and ask why.

### Your own temptation to lie

- She says all lies are costly: a lie is harder to keep track of than a fact, it damages the relationship, and lying about a rival offer can lead to legal trouble.
- She says to decide in advance whether you will lie and to prepare for the questions you do not want to answer.
- She says most unplanned lies happen because people are not prepared.
- She says moving down from a first offer is a concession and not a lie.
- She says you do not have to reveal your cost or margin, and saying that you cannot reveal it is an alternative to lying.
- She says you should never ask for the other side's reservation price or margin, because it closes the room for negotiation.
- She says she does not have an answer for buyers who insist on knowing your cost, which students told her is common in Indian markets.
- She distinguishes exaggeration in marketing from lying, which she defines as knowing the facts and not giving them when asked.
- She says to know what kind of reputation you want and to consult lawyers so you never get into legal trouble.

### Power, disputes and AI

- Asked about founders facing investors, she says power is determined by alternatives, so a founder should have more investors to go to.
- She says an investor's job is to find valuable startups, so a founder with value is not without power.
- She says in a dispute the alternative to a negotiated settlement is a worst alternative, so disputes are approached differently.
- She says she does not expect AI to take over negotiation in the near future, because negotiation is about people and not only numbers.
- She says very few negotiation cases are written in the Indian context and little research has been done there.

## Examples and cases used

- The property-sale role play: prices overlapped but interests did not, so no deal was the honest outcome.
- The printer queue experiment: giving a reason with the word because changed how people responded.
- A Singapore rental agent paid one month's rent: an incentive that works against the tenant.
- Professor Susskind's principal and agent video: briefing an agent, handling a procurement consultant, and reporting back to a boss who grades the result.
- Colour-coded electricity bills: vivid presentation changed consumption when plain numbers had not.
- Recruiters who describe an employer only as a big player in an industry: agents keeping a principal confidential.

## Numbers given

- 81%: the share of respondents who admitted to lying in the survey she cites.
- Five times: her guide for preparation time relative to negotiation time.
