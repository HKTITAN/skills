# Lying in negotiation and how to detect it

The second half of the fifth session of the six-part negotiation course, taught by the course professor, who is not named in this recording. She runs it as a discussion on four questions: how to stop the other side lying, how to spot a lie, what to do when you catch one, and whether to lie yourself. The class then starts a dispute resolution exercise, which is not part of this note.

## What was said

### People do lie

- The instructor cites a large survey in which respondents admitted to lying in negotiations.
- She says she ran an experiment in her own ethics teaching where students lied with no money at stake, only a ranking in the top three.
- Her first advice is to be prepared to test what you are told: check the facts and do your homework.

### Reducing the other side's temptation to lie

- She says that once a person has told one lie, the chance that they lie again goes up, so the temptation should be reduced at the very start.
- She says not to ask the questions that invite a lie.
- She says to look prepared and carry data, so that the other side does not try to lie to you.
- She accepts a student's point that ground rules can be set at the start, with some behaviour named as unacceptable and written into clauses.
- She says telling the other side that they have always been a good and reliable counterpart reduces their temptation to lie, because people act in line with being seen as trustworthy.
- She says she told the class in the first session never to ask for the other side's reservation price, and calls it a bad strategy.

### Detecting a lie

- She says most people show speech hesitation and avoid eye contact when they lie.
- She says to look for inconsistencies in what is being said.
- Her test for a claimed competing offer is to ask for details of what it contains. A made-up offer has little coherence under such questions.
- She says a fact does not have to be remembered, while a liar has to invent and keep track of many scenarios, so something is usually missing from the story.
- Her summary is to watch body language, ask one good question and then ask follow-up questions.
- She says proposing contingencies and offering several packages with why and why not questions shows whether the other side lied about its priorities.

### When you catch a lie

- She says whether to confront the other side depends on the power balance and the relationship.
- She says this should be planned before the negotiation, since the goal is still to get a good deal.
- She says a reputation, once built, makes later negotiations much easier because people know what kind of person you are.

### After a lost deal

- She advises not closing the conversation when you lose a project. Go back and ask why.
- She retells an example from the book Negotiation Genius in which a seller who lost an order asked the buyer to explain what had happened and so learned why.

### Should you lie yourself

- She says all lies are costly and some are more costly than others, and that for most people there is also a personal cost.
- She says giving a concession is not lying. Moving down from your first price is how negotiation works.
- She separates the offer from its justification: the offer is not a lie, but the reasons given for it can be.
- She says you do not have to reveal your margin in a negotiation.
- She suggests turning the talk to the other side's numbers and interests so that you are not pushed to reveal your own.
- She says that if you have no competing offer you should never invent a competitor's name, and should prepare what you will say if asked.
- She says a person who does decide to lie must at least be able to defend it, because being caught damages reputation.
- She says a negotiator should decide where they stand on lying before the meeting, not inside it.
- She lists three alternatives to lying about your alternatives: improve them, do not discuss specifics, and highlight your strengths.
- She says founders should consult their legal department when unsure.

### Power with investors

- Asked about facing investors who have many options, she questions the assumption that the investor has more power.
- She says power depends on alternatives and on how much the other side values what you offer, so a founder should also have other investors to approach.

## Examples and cases used

- A survey on lying and her own classroom experiment: used to show that people lie even without a money incentive.
- A coffee seller who justifies a price with cost claims and then drops the price: used to show that the justification, not the concession, is where the lie sits.
- An example from Negotiation Genius of a seller who asked why an order was lost: used to show that a failed negotiation is not the end.
