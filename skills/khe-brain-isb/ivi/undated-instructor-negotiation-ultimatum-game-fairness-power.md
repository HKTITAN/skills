# Negotiation, the ultimatum game, fairness and power

This is the opening class of a negotiation course taught by a faculty member who is not named in the recording. She set out how the course works, ran two rounds of the ultimatum game with the room split into offerors and respondents, and used the results to teach fairness, likeability and power. The file also carries a classmate's written notes, used below only where marked.

## What was said

### How she frames negotiation

- The instructor splits negotiation into issues, which she calls the science, and people, which she calls the art.
- She says every negotiation has an opening, an exchange and a closing, and that people forget to learn techniques for closing the deal.
- She says there is always a tension between the short term and the long term in a negotiation.
- She says most business negotiations involve money and other things, and she calls it a good thing that other things are involved.
- She separates integrative issues from distributive issues, where the parties divide something such as money.
- She says negotiation is a skill and the only way to get better is to practise, analyse, take feedback and practise again while changing something.
- She says the classroom is a low risk place to try strategies, and that the course runs four or five simulations, most of them based on real cases.
- She says that when people say they dislike negotiating, the reason is mostly about people issues.

### The ultimatum game

- She explains the rules: an offeror holds 100 dollars and offers any share to a respondent, who can only accept or reject. If the respondent rejects, both get zero.
- She says the game allows no communication and no negotiation, and that the class has to stick to the rules of a simulation.
- She says that in the game generally anything above 30 is accepted, and that 30 to 70 is the most accepted outcome.
- She says the game has been played with real money in different cultures and with rich and poor players, with almost similar outcomes.
- She points out that rational decision making gives no reason to reject a positive amount in a one shot game, yet people reject.
- Her example is a respondent who rejected an offer because the same offeror had offered a friend nothing in the other round. People care about how others are treated and not only about their own payoff.

### Fairness

- She says perception of fairness differs between people. Ten dollars can be fair for one person and fifty not fair for another.
- She says perception of fairness also differs for the same person in different roles. The person who offers 50 can think 50 is not good when responding.
- She tells students never to say "this is a very fair offer for you" to the other party.
- She says that because fairness is subjective and resources are limited, the task is to change the perception of fairness and not the outcome.
- She says one way to do that is to show the trade offs, for example by taking on the complex or operational part of the work as the reason for a larger share.
- She says you help the other party see why the offer is a good one, and that there are many ways to do this.
- She says we forget the other party is a different person, so issues that matter to them and not to us get left out of our planning document.

### Concessions

- She says every concession sends a signal, so a negotiator should be careful about each one.
- She says a very large concession makes the other party believe you have room to give more, so you never make large concessions.
- She says concessions can be made on many things besides money, and that a negotiator needs a plan for how many concessions to make.

### Liking and trust

- She says people accept an offer from someone they like and refuse the same offer from someone they do not like.
- She says we tend to trust the people we like, and we accept a deal that does not look good from a person we trust.
- Her list of what makes people likeable includes attractiveness and dress, making others feel comfortable, and listening.
- She says it is very important that the other party wants to talk to you.
- She says people get emotional because they are attached to their own outcome.
- Her own technique, from her time in HR tech sales before her PhD, is to negotiate as if on behalf of a friend. She thinks about what outcome the friend would want and so distances herself from the result.

### Power

- She says power is asymmetrical in many negotiations.
- The class split on whether the offeror or the respondent holds more power. The offeror holds the money and judges what will be accepted. The respondent has the final say and can destroy the deal.
- She says perception of power is subjective in the same way as fairness. You can think you have power when the other party does not.
- She says a negotiation is derailed when you start from a position of power that the other party does not recognise.
- She says you cannot easily change the other party's perception because it comes from their experiences and biases.
- She says that in most situations, even when we have power, we do not use it unless we need it.
- She says alternatives are the biggest source of power, so you list your own alternatives and the other person's.
- She says that when you do not know how the other side sees your power, you do your homework and ask questions during the negotiation, knowing which questions you can and cannot ask.
- She says signalling power works only if the other party has not done its homework, and can backfire if it has.
- She says the sources of power can differ while both parties hold equal power.
- She says the course uses science based techniques to raise the probability of success so that nothing is left to chance.

- The notes name the third pillar alongside fairness and power as trust.
- The notes record BATNA, the best alternative to a negotiated agreement, as the term for alternatives.
- The notes record that people with no power have nothing to lose, and that this becomes their power.
- The notes record the advice not to showcase your sources of power.
- The notes record the advice to step back when you feel angry.
- The notes list the books named as Negotiation Genius, Getting to Yes and Getting Past No.

## Examples and cases used

- The ultimatum game: played twice in class with a notional 100 dollars, to show that people reject offers they see as unfair and that fairness and power are both matters of perception.

## Numbers given

- 30: the share of 100 above which, the instructor says, offers in the ultimatum game are generally accepted.
