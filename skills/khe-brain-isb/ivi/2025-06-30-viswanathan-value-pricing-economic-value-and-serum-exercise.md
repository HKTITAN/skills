# Value pricing part 2, economic value and a serum pricing exercise, Madhu Viswanathan

Madhu Viswanathan taught the second half of the value pricing class, after the break. The speaker is not named in this recording and is identified by matching it to another recording of the same class. He finished a worked economic value example, explained total cost of ownership, showed a short Bain video on the B2B elements of value, and ran a 20 minute group exercise pricing a made up skincare serum. The file also carries a classmate's written notes, used below only where marked.

## What was said

### Economic value example

- Madhu Viswanathan works an example where the reference value is 75,000, the negative differentiation is 12,500 and the positive differentiation is 19,000, giving a total of 81,500.
- He says 81,500 is not the price. It gives a range from 75,000 to 81,500 and you can price anywhere in it.
- He says choosing where to price inside the range is where pricing strategy comes in.

### Total cost of ownership

- He uses two light bulbs to explain total cost of ownership, comparing expected life and price per bulb.
- He describes total cost of ownership for an electric vehicle as a one time setup cost, a recurring charging cost and a maintenance cost.
- He says sellers have to do this total cost calculation, and can be clever about what they include in it.

### Closing the value gap

- He says closing the gap between the value you offer and the value the customer sees depends largely on communication.
- His example is a printer advertisement with the line that the printer is so smart it pays for itself, backed by cost per page arithmetic.

### B2B elements of value

- He plays a Bain video which says B2B decisions are not purely rational and involve a lot of emotion, including anxiety.
- The video lists table stakes such as being ethical, functional value tied to economic benefit, ease of doing business such as vendor responsiveness, individual value such as the decision maker's career and learning, and aspirational value such as making the company or the world better.
- He says an acceptable price depends on your value, and is anchored to a reference price, meaning what the industry charges or what the alternatives cost.
- He tells founders who sell to businesses to look at the elements of value because it will widen their view of what they offer.
- He describes functional value as benefits from features and capabilities that go beyond direct economic cost, such as time savings.
- He describes social value as benefits from how others perceive the purchase, and says it is the hardest to pin down.
- He groups the sources of value into three views: the product view of features, benefits and experience, the customer view of needs, wants and pains, and the alternatives, which act as reference points.

### The serum exercise

- He sets a made up skincare serum and asks groups to calculate a maximum willingness to pay, propose a launch price and pitch an Instagram ad.
- He gives his own figures, a reference price of 799 and a maximum willingness to pay of 1,049, as anchors only, and says groups are free to choose their own.
- He restates the formula as reference price plus positive differentiation minus negative differentiation.
- He says that when no reference price is given, you find it from the market by asking what people would replace you with.
- He says a group that counts the whole saving on salon visits as differentiation value is being super aggressive, and must be able to convince the buyer of it.
- He says "If you have data to back up, go all in", and that this is what marketing research helps with.
- He says people will not believe preposterous assumptions.
- He says conjoint analysis is the tool for putting numbers on such differences.
- He says negative differentiation is estimated by looking at the feature differences against an established brand and asking how much they are worth, which needs knowledge of the industry and product expertise.

### Debrief

- He says pricing above the maximum willingness to pay does not mean zero sales. Demand still exists but is a lot smaller, and the product is then positioned as super premium.
- He says there is no real answer to the exercise because the product does not exist.
- He says 999, the price many groups chose, is a good strategy but one can do better.
- He says the better answer is to introduce two products at 999 and 1,199, so that the 999 product, which has many of the features of the dearer one, becomes the attractive choice.

## Examples and cases used

- LED and incandescent bulbs: total cost of ownership over the life of the product.
- A Samsung printer advertisement: value communicated as a saving per page that pays for the printer.
- Bain's B2B elements of value: a video and an interactive graphic he recommends to founders who sell to businesses.
- A made up skincare serum: the class exercise for reference price, differentiation value and launch price.

## Numbers given

- 75,000, 12,500, 19,000 and 81,500: reference value, negative differentiation, positive differentiation and total in his worked example.
- 799 and 1,049: his own reference price and maximum willingness to pay for the made up serum, in rupees.
- 999 and 1,199: the two price points he suggests launching together.
