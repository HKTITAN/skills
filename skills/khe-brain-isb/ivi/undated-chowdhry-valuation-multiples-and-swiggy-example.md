# Valuation multiples and the Swiggy example, Prof Bhagwan Chowdhry

A finance class in two halves. Prof Bhagwan Chowdhry derived the growing perpetuity formula on the board and used it to explain where valuation multiples come from. A co-instructor, addressed as Prachi in the recording, then valued Swiggy as a private company using Zomato's listed multiple, and covered enterprise value, free cash flow and market sizing along the way. Much of the board arithmetic did not reach the transcript.

## What was said

### The growing perpetuity formula

- Prof Bhagwan Chowdhry says a cash flow C that grows at rate g for ever and is discounted at rate i is worth C divided by i minus g today.
- He calls this formula "the true Mona Lisa" of finance and says every multiple rests on it.
- He says it is something a founder should carry in their head without Excel or ChatGPT.
- He says the discount rate i is made of the real interest rate, inflation and a risk premium.
- He says if the discount rate includes inflation the growth rate must include it too, so inflation cancels out of i minus g.
- He says i cannot equal g for ever. A firm can grow faster than the economy for two or three years, but if growth stayed that high interest rates would rise.
- He says the infinite formula does not fit an asset with a fixed life. His example is a ship that will be scrapped in 25 years, where you stop the sum at 25.
- He says value comes from cash flow, and if you cannot see cash flow at some point in the future there is no value.

### Where multiples come from

- He says if earnings are roughly equal to cash flows, the price to earnings ratio is an estimate of 1 divided by i minus g.
- His example uses a 2% risk-free rate, a 5% risk premium and 2% growth, which gives a price to earnings multiple of 20.
- He says the same firm with no real growth has a multiple of 1 divided by 0.07, about 14, and with 4% growth it is 1 divided by 0.03, about 33.
- He says the price to earnings ratio therefore depends on two things, the discount rate and the growth rate.
- He says the formula holds only when growth has stabilised. Early firms have tiny earnings that grow very fast, so their price to earnings ratio tells you nothing.
- He gives Amazon's early price to earnings ratio as around 700 to 800 to show this.
- He says cash flows are discounted and not profits because profit is in the book and not in the hand, and what matters is cash you can take out and use elsewhere.
- He says EBITDA multiples and revenue multiples come from the same formula, with an assumed steady relation between that measure and earnings.
- He says firms with no revenue get valued on multiples such as eyeballs, and the present value formula still sits underneath.
- He calls the multiple approach the "Instagram version" of valuation: cheap, simple and easy to remember, with details still to fill in.
- He says the details are the risk premium for the firm's industry and whether the firm is early or late in its life.
- He says a quick first answer to what a firm is worth is steady-state earnings times the industry's typical multiple. That is a benchmark to start from and not the value.

### From exit value to today's valuation

- The co-instructor says customer lifetime value comes from the same formula, with the churn rate acting as the opposite of a growth rate.
- She says the discount rate is the cost of capital for the business, which is the opportunity cost for its investors and carries more risk than a fixed deposit.
- She says the venture approach is to estimate the exit value, when and how much, and then discount it back to get today's post-money valuation.
- She says the exit can be an IPO or an acquisition.
- She says the post-money valuation and the term sheet together decide how the value is split.

### Enterprise value and equity value

- She says enterprise value is the value of the entire business and belongs to equity holders and debt holders together.
- She says equity value is enterprise value less debt, plus cash and plus non-operating assets.
- She says non-operating assets are things like money parked in mutual funds or small strategic stakes in other startups that do not produce operating revenue.
- She says that to get a clean multiple from a listed firm you take its equity value, add debt and remove the cash pile and non-operating assets, so that only the core business is compared.
- She says the valued firm's own cash and non-operating assets are added back at the end, because investors get a share of equity value and not enterprise value.

### The Swiggy example

- She sets the example at a time when Zomato had listed and Swiggy was still raising from venture investors, and uses Zomato as the benchmark.
- She says a revenue multiple was used because Zomato was not profitable at its IPO.
- She gives Zomato's enterprise value at IPO as 27.6 times its revenue.
- She says the multiple is applied to Swiggy's projected revenue at the expected exit, which the example puts two years out.
- She says the two firms must record revenue the same way. In her example Swiggy reported gross merchandise value while Zomato reported its net take, so one has to be adjusted.
- She says the projected growth rate is negotiated between startup and investors, and a very early venture has less bargaining power.
- She says the discount rate has an industry risk premium plus an extra premium for the chance that the firm never reaches the exit.
- She gives 30 to 50% as the discount rate applied in venture capital.
- She says negotiations tend to focus on the best case, such as an IPO in two years, so the chance of failure gets loaded into the discount rate.
- She says the Zomato numbers are concrete but how far they apply to Swiggy is where the disagreement happens. The technique is mathematical and the application is judgment.

### Adjusting a borrowed multiple

- She says a multiple transfers cleanly only if the two firms match on three things: discount rate, expected growth and the share of EBITDA that becomes free cash flow.
- She says two firms in the same country and industry share most of the discount rate, and differ mainly on failure risk.
- She says a firm expected to grow faster after its IPO deserves a higher multiple.
- She says Zomato listed in 2021 just after the pandemic with a recovery still ahead, so its expected growth was higher than Swiggy's would be at a later IPO, and investors would mark the multiple down.
- She says a perfect comparable is almost impossible to find. These factors tell you the direction of the adjustment and not the size.
- She says the method also assumes the comparable is itself valued correctly, and a multiple from a frothy period will be adjusted if the exit is expected in leaner times.
- She says it is always a good idea to run a sensitivity analysis on the multiple, the growth rate and the discount rate.
- She says the rate in a multiple valuation must come from another firm or an industry average, and she has almost never seen a multiple taken from a different industry.
- She says a discounted cash flow valuation is the better approach when it is possible because it fits the specific business, like tailored clothes against borrowed ones.
- She says a global peer can serve as the comparable.

### Free cash flow and working capital

- She says earnings and free cash flow are close only at steady state. For a growing firm the gap is what must be reinvested in the business.
- She says free means the cash can be taken out of the business. Cash the business still needs is not free.
- She defines net working capital as accounts receivable plus inventory minus accounts payable.
- She says a growing business holds more inventory to support higher sales, and that increase in working capital is subtracted when moving from profit to free cash flow.
- She says if working capital stays constant there is no further cash outflow after the initial investment.
- She says a good rule of thumb for projections is working capital as a fraction of sales, taken from your unit economics and ratio analysis, or from similar businesses if you are new.

### Market sizing

- She says the total addressable market is what you would sell if you captured the entire market for what you sell.
- She says the serviceable market narrows that by your current focus. Her example is skincare narrowed to the premium segment and then to urban buyers.
- She says the obtainable market is the share you can realistically achieve given the competition, over five or ten years and not necessarily today.

## Examples and cases used

- Zomato and Swiggy: a listed firm's revenue multiple used to value an unlisted rival, with the adjustments that requires.
- Amazon: an early price to earnings ratio in the hundreds, to show that multiples mislead before steady state.
- A ship with a 25-year life: an asset where the perpetuity formula does not apply.
- A marriage proposal: you start from a benchmark such as the person's profession and then look at the details, as with an industry multiple.
- Premium urban skincare: narrowing a total market to a serviceable one.

## Numbers given

- 20: the price to earnings multiple in Prof Chowdhry's example with a 2% risk-free rate, 5% risk premium and 2% growth.
- 700 to 800: his figure for Amazon's early price to earnings ratio.
- 27.6: Zomato's enterprise value to revenue multiple at IPO, as used in the class example.
- 30 to 50%: the discount rate the co-instructor says venture investors apply.
- 2 years: the assumed time to Swiggy's exit in the example.
