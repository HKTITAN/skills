# Habits, team and content, Ankur Warikoo

Ankur Warikoo, an ISB alumnus and founder of Webveda, spent close to four hours with the cohort. He told his own story for the first stretch, then asked the room for topics and worked through them: habits, time management, how he runs and pays his team, how he hires, personal branding, content against marketing, and community. He showed his own calendar, task tracker, salary sheet and hiring questions on screen.

## What was said

### His journey

- Warikoo says he planned from school to do a PhD in the US, work at NASA and go to Mars, and he reached Michigan State University for a PhD in astrophysics on a full scholarship.
- He says he was good at physics but it did not make him happy, so he dropped out at 24 and came back to India in 2004.
- He joined ISB with nine months of work experience in a batch of 330 where the average was five years, and says having nothing to contribute freed him to absorb everything.
- After ISB he worked in consulting at A.T. Kearney, and spent a year and a half on a classmate's remarriage matrimonial site at night before joining it full time.
- He says he applied for the Groupon India role without researching Rocket Internet, which managed Groupon's international expansion, and was later told he was the only applicant from the alumni groups that got the email.
- He started Groupon India in 2011. After Groupon's share price fell from 26 dollars at listing to about 2 dollars, he stayed, and was handed Asia Pacific, going from a team of 200 to 1,500.
- He says he was a micromanaging first-time manager who made his team miserable, and that his team's direct feedback between 2013 and 2015 trained him as a manager.
- In August 2015 he led a management buyout of Groupon India with Sequoia Capital. He gives the split as Groupon 30%, Sequoia 40% and the founders 30%, with a first cheque of 109 crore rupees. The company was renamed Nearbuy.
- He says Nearbuy went downhill through his own mistakes as a first-time funded founder: burning money to grow because it looked like the thing to do, then hitting a funding winter and laying people off.
- Paytm took Nearbuy over in 2017. He says it became profitable for the first time in 2019 at a scale of about 600 crore rupees, and he stepped down as CEO at the end of 2019.
- He says that in March 2020 he had five months of money left, because he had taken below-market salary and put his savings back into Nearbuy.

### Living without goals

- Warikoo says he has not had a personal goal or target for 20 years. He sets habits and lets them compound.
- He describes himself this way: "I am a plastic bag in the storm."
- He says the biggest trap a founder sets is deciding where they want to be before they start, because a founder controls the input and has no control over the outcome.
- He says a company, startup or team needs a goal or target, but a person should not attach themselves to it. Missing it makes you blame yourself and hitting it makes you applaud yourself more than you should.
- He says being contrarian does not lead to success. It leads to a different result from the average, and that result can be worse.
- He calls his own story survivorship bias: many people left consulting, did buyouts or started making content, and the room only hears from him because he survived.
- He compares his career to sitting on many chairs to find the comfortable one, and says the current one is comfortable for now and will change.
- He says he does things only while he loves doing them, and that loving something is different from wanting it to be easy.
- He separates being burnt out from being checked out. He says he left Nearbuy because he was no longer interested, not because he was exhausted.

### Habits and discipline

- Warikoo says discipline is not forcing yourself awake at 6 am for the gym. He calls that torture.
- He defines discipline as finding the form that works for you to get what you want, then doing that every day.
- He says to pick a habit you want to do, not one you think you should do.
- His own habits as he lists them: he reads every day, writes every day including by hand, meets one new person every week, meditates 30 minutes a day and blocks time with no agenda.
- He has kept a blog since 2005 with nearly 3,000 posts and says he does not care how few people read it.
- He reads all of his 300 to 350 emails a day, and says he has hired his entire team through cold emails.
- He says "last man standing" is a strategy. During Covid everyone made content, and what mattered was who was still making it when that ended.

### Finding your why

- Warikoo tells every entrepreneur to watch Simon Sinek's Golden Circle talk first.
- He says most people know what they do, fewer know how, and very few know why. The common mistake is to settle the what and the how and then invent a why to justify them.
- His method is to start with the most basic reason for what you do and ask why at least five times until it cannot be broken down further.
- His test for the answer is that it must explain everything you have done in life so far. If it does not, you are at the wrong spot.
- He states his own why as helping people make choices from awareness and not ignorance. Books, courses and reels are only forms of that.
- He says his audience stays because they sense he is trying to make them aware and not sell to them, and they would leave if he turned salesy or prescriptive.

### Time management

- Warikoo says he logged every waking hour of his day for 13 years, from age 13 to 26.
- He says a person's calendar shows where they will be in a year, and that having a grip on your time is different from setting a goal.
- At Nearbuy his team told him he carried the hangover of one meeting into the next, so he stopped trying to win the day and started trying to win the week.
- Winning the week means bucketing the week's work into blocks of at least four hours on one thing, and accepting that not everything gets done every day.
- He shoots all of his content on Mondays: four 30-minute YouTube videos, seven shorts for each of two channels and seven Instagram reels.
- He keeps Tuesday mostly free, writes his newsletter on Wednesday, reviews content on Thursday and keeps Friday and the weekend clear.

### How the team runs

- Warikoo says his company is fully remote and asynchronous, with no working hours, no managers and no regular meetings.
- The team meets in person once a quarter at a five-day off-site.
- Work runs on Asana. Each person opens it and sees their tasks for the day, which he says is what removes the need for a manager.
- He splits every workflow into repeatable items, which get a fixed cadence in the tool, and ad hoc items, which get a checklist that switches on when triggered.
- He says the system gives people two things: what to do and when to do it. Quality cannot be put in as a measure and shows up later through a review or through performance.
- A weekly email lists who delayed which tasks. He shows a week with 8 delays out of 239 tasks and a quarter with 51 delays out of 3,532.
- He audits the process every six months and reviews each person monthly, starting with their own self-assessment.
- He says he does not care how long people work or when, only that the work is done, so early finishers and last-minute workers both fit a two-week content cycle.
- He encourages team members to freelance for other companies, with no restriction on competitors, because he says that is how young people learn.
- He says checks and reprimands lower the incentive to be accurate, because people rely on the checker. Knowing your work goes straight to a large audience raises it.
- He credits his first manager in consulting with the line he runs his company on: "If I can't trust you, it doesn't matter how smart you are."
- The one meeting of the whole team is a weekly business review on Monday evening, a practice he says came from Amazon through Groupon executives. He gives it 10 minutes on the metrics and 10 minutes on what everyone is doing.
- He keeps the business review separate from weekly team check-ins, which exist to clear roadblocks and not to review the business.

### Pay and transparency

- Everyone's salary at his company is on a public sheet. He says he hates companies winning over employees through information the employee does not have.
- Each year the company benchmarks every role and level against published salary surveys and pays at the 50th percentile.
- A new hire's level is set by the company from the task they complete, not from their stated experience or past salary.
- There are no ESOPs, because he would be the only buyer of the stock. 10% of profit is shared with the team every quarter.
- 40% of each person's share of the profit pool depends on whether they met their timelines.
- Interns get 10,000 rupees a month, the same as in March 2020. He says anyone who wants more can become full time with the responsibilities that brings.
- He says his fixed pay is not life-changing and that people stay for an environment that money cannot buy.
- He says he fires fast, usually within the first three months.

### Hiring

- Warikoo says he never looks at resumes, because a resume biases him.
- Everyone who writes to him gets the same 20 personal questions, for every role, whether or not a role is open. None is about work.
- He reads the answers for one thing: whether this is a person he would like to spend time with and whose values match his.
- Candidates also take two psychometric tests, one for emotional regulation and one for attachment style. He works only with people who score as emotionally regulated and securely attached, and says this is his personal choice.
- He then meets the person without a role in mind, asks his team whether a role exists, and only then gives a task assignment built for that role.
- He says he adopted this 13 years ago after conventional hiring by pedigree failed him.
- He says the first five people decide whether a startup survives, so he is willing to hire slowly.
- At Nearbuy he could not match big employers on campus, so he offered graduates work that would build their profile for ISB. He says nine people out of about 200 went to ISB between 2015 and 2019.

### How Webveda was built

- In April 2020 he announced paid webinars on LinkedIn on a pay-what-you-want basis, using a Razorpay payment page with a one-rupee minimum.
- For the first set, half the people paid one rupee and the average came to 193 rupees a person.
- He took three things from the webinars: he could teach online, people pay for content they find useful, and people trusted him enough to pay in advance.
- In September 2020 he put 5 lakh rupees into recording two courses, one on time management and one a startup guide, and delivered them as password-protected Vimeo videos.
- He says he was happy for the password to be shared, because it built trust.
- Webveda courses are fully recorded, cost between 399 and about 3,000 rupees and carry a 100% refund.
- He says the refund policy works because serving one more student of a recorded course costs close to nothing.
- He says he welcomes piracy of his books and recorded courses because he wants the knowledge to spread, and that this is different for live teaching, where his time is the cost.
- He declines to tie his earnings to students' outcomes, because those depend on each student's motivation and context, which he does not control.
- Each course is first taught live to a founding batch on full scholarship. The recordings of that cohort become the raw material for the recorded course.
- He says the recorded course then feels like being part of a cohort, and gives that as the reason his completion rate is high.
- A course starts with a curriculum outline benchmarked against what already exists, then detailed modules, then a text-only draft with no visuals so the flow can be checked before delivery.

### Personal brand, content and marketing

- Warikoo says personal branding comes down to two things, consistency and authenticity, and that authenticity is what makes consistency possible.
- He says his reach took 10 to 20 years: a blog from 2005, LinkedIn and Quora from 2012, YouTube and Instagram from 2017, with seven years of videos before anyone noticed.
- He says answering questions on Quora brought Nearbuy about 11,000 job applications in two years.
- He says content can sell or it can build trust, and the two almost never overlap. He uses content only for trust and never mentions Webveda on his social channels.
- Selling is done by a separate paid marketing engine run from a different channel.
- He says people who see an ad then look him up, find years of content that sells nothing, and convert at a much higher rate.
- He says people follow people, not brands, so a startup that wants to sell through content should build a personal brand around a person.
- That person can be the founder, a team member, an AI avatar or a small creator the company backs.
- His suggested method is to have that person build a trusted voice in the category and mention only the company's brand, quietly and never any other brand.
- To reduce dependence on one creator he suggests several creators, AI avatars, or several faces on one channel.
- He says a founder's struggle story cannot be the sales engine, because nobody cares about it.
- He says platform algorithms no longer favour old or large accounts. A new creator can reach a million views on day one, and each new piece starts from scratch.
- He says YouTube still leads, WhatsApp channels are interesting, and LinkedIn is his favourite as the lowest effort for the highest return. He says video on X has not worked yet.
- For a product used by school students, his advice is to market to the parents, who are the buyers, on whichever platform the parents use.

### The market is the market

- At a used-car marketplace he co-founded in 2009, the three founders did not speak to a single used-car dealer for the first three months.
- He says used-car demand then far exceeded supply, so sellers and buyers were served instantly by dealers and nobody needed an online marketplace.
- He says a marketplace only works when demand and supply are in balance.
- He says nobody buys a product because of the founder's college, funding or sacrifices. They buy only because the product works for them.
- He calls entrepreneurship the most brutal form of self-discovery, because past credentials stop counting and only the product does.

### Community

- Warikoo says he has a following, not a community. A following is one to many and a community is many to many.
- His working thesis is that the best community for his students is a jobs community that connects people who have work to give with people who want it.
- He says he has not found a product that handles a community of 450,000 people well.
- He says he cannot build on a tool from a new startup, because he needs something he is sure will last a decade.
- He says he does not want to be a community leader. He wants to light the spark and leave.

### Success and self-knowledge

- He defines success through a scene in the film Dilwale Dulhania Le Jayenge: to be old, at home, among people he loves, and able to say he lived a good life.
- He says what impresses him is a person who already knows where they stand emotionally, and that people avoid finding out because they are scared of the answer.
- His advice from his year at ISB is to spend time with people unlike yourself and have lunch with a new person every day.
- He says that at 19 the world will demand a plan from you, so give people one and do not change your own course because of it.
- He says he no longer invests in startups directly, because he falls for the founder and ignores the business. He invests through funds as a limited partner.

## Examples and cases used

- Rocket Internet and Groupon: he took a job others researched and rejected, and says not overthinking sometimes works out.
- Flipkart: his example of early Indian startups building logistics and cash on delivery themselves because nothing existed.
- Simon Sinek's Golden Circle, with Apple, Harley-Davidson and Nike: people buy why you do something, not what you do.
- Elon Musk's Dark Knight analogy: the same script filmed by every cinema would give different films, which is how education is delivered today. Warikoo wants the best single version of each subject delivered online.
- Ali Abdaal: a conversation with him is where Warikoo realised that last man standing is a strategy.
- Ola: its founder told him the top reason people rang Ola's call centre was to ask where the car was, though the app showed it. Warikoo uses this to show that India is a low-trust market.
- Amazon: source of the weekly business review, built around one business outcome, which he says for Amazon is free cash flow.
- Kunal Shah: his example of a founder whose audience responds to his posts on life and goes quiet when he posts about his company.
- YMCA: the community he says he would most like to replicate.
- Penguin: he says he told his publisher he was glad his books were pirated.

## Numbers given

- Webveda: about 454,000 paying students, 6,000 to 8,000 new students a month, about 7% from outside India, as he states them.
- Webveda: refund rate 6% and course completion rate 72%, his figures.
- Company revenue of 20 to 25 crore rupees a year and profit of 5 to 6 crore, his figures.
- Content side earns 5 to 8 crore rupees a year from speaking and brand collaborations. He charges 10 lakh rupees for an hour of speaking and runs no ads on YouTube.
- 16 million followers and 63 pieces of content a day, his figures.
- Team: about 10 people, five of them full time.
- Reach: his content reaches about 33 million people a month and his ads about 47 million.
- Landing page conversion of 3 to 4% on a first visit, against 31% when the visitor searched his name first, his figures.
- Ad spend of 50 to 60 lakh rupees a month, about 40 on Meta and 20 on Google.
- Team benefits: 72,000 rupees a year for wellness and 90% reimbursement of any online course.
- Nearbuy: voluntary attrition of 1.2%, his figure.
- Two people have left his current company in five years.
- His ISB batch of 330: about 28 people work in companies started by batchmates.

## Not covered

- The detail of his mistakes at Nearbuy beyond burning money for growth.
- How to build a community product. He says he has no answer yet.
- Cross-cultural relationships, which the room asked for and he did not get to.
