# Building Pickrr and advice on B2B and D2C, Gaurav Mangla and Ankit Kaushik

Two co-founders of the logistics company Pickrr told the cohort how they started, then spent most of the session answering founders' questions on B2B sales, D2C operations, co-founder conflict and fundraising. The transcript has many gaps and does not mark who is speaking, so the claims below are attributed to the Pickrr founders together.

## What was said

### How Pickrr started

- The Pickrr founders say their first venture began in college, working nights, with a small Python script they wrote after learning from YouTube lectures.
- They say they sold that first company because they could not see it reaching large monthly revenue, and that it did get there six to eight years later.
- They say the next idea was to deliver things within two to three hours. They called their third co-founder, who joined the next day.
- They say they started with savings of 50,000 to 60,000 rupees, two or three delivery boys and friends from school and college.
- They say the hyperlocal delivery model of 2015 did not have workable unit economics, so they scaled up abruptly and then had to stop it.
- They say that in 2017 there was a gap in serving D2C brands, and that became the sweet spot for the company.
- They say that of 24 companies in their space, only about 10 remain.

### Selling B2B software in India

- They say that for small customers the service part can be handled by automation and AI.
- They say an early B2B startup should aim for one big customer and two or three running customers, which gives a roadmap to the next 20 to 30 customers.
- They say B2B SaaS in India has to be software plus a service and automation layer plus intelligence or consulting that helps the customer grow.
- They say that once a large customer has validated the product, the same service can be sold to smaller customers at a premium. Their example is 10 rupees for small customers against 2 rupees for an enterprise customer.
- Asked how to land a first enterprise customer, they say to find a friend inside, learn that person's priorities, and look for an easy starting point.
- Their example of a starting point is giving the company a report so that it gets to know you, then implementing one actionable part of it, then extending from there.
- They say compliance is usually not the bottleneck in enterprise sales. The main challenge is how easily the customer's data gets into your platform with minimal effort on their side.
- They say if a buyer is in the market for a tool and you are not on their list, it is very hard to get in the next time, so founders should stay connected with people in the target industry.
- Asked about customer requests that fall outside the plan, they say to explore them in the early stage and to build what the first 20 customers ask for.

### Reducing returns for online brands

- They list what an online buyer is unsure of: whether the parcel will come, whether the price is right, whether they will receive what they saw, how long the product will last, and the brand's online presence.
- They say addressing those doubts in what is displayed to the buyer helps significantly.
- They say they use consumer cohort data, such as how a buyer's acceptance of parcels changes with the discount level, to reduce returns.
- They advise showing an estimated delivery date on the product page and having the founder track it as a key metric.

### Co-founders

- They say they never brought in a third party to settle a conflict between the three co-founders.
- They say an unresolved disagreement was given a set time frame, and if results started to show in that time it resolved itself.
- Their example: after losing the money they had raised in operations, one co-founder wanted to continue only if VCs funded them. Within three to four weeks the others showed that the same volumes could be sustained without raised money.
- They say three people working together for ten years will disagree every month and even every day.

### Fundraising and what investors look for

- They say fundraising is cyclical, like real estate.
- They say every VC firm has a thesis, so at the earliest stage a founder has to approach many firms, which they call carpet bombing.
- They say more people are willing to fund startups than ten years ago and early valuations have risen.
- They say their first institutional round had only one backer at the start and the other three came in organically.
- They say they now invest themselves and are open to any sector where they can see white space.
- Asked how they judge early founders, they say a founder has to show the power to convince people: investors, customers and a team.

### Inventory, manufacturers and channels

- They say whether to hold stock in one warehouse or many is a maths problem, and many large brands run from a single warehouse.
- They say spreading stock across India raises inventory, so a brand short of capital should keep central operations.
- They say a pattern they have seen work for larger brands is to place no more than 20% of inventory out in distributed locations.
- They advise a brand that depends heavily on its manufacturers early on to give them some equity, and mention a shoe brand that raised two crore rupees from its manufacturers.
- They say that for an online brand, offline retail is a different business altogether.
- They say the D2C channel has been stagnant for the past three years.
- They say that in their data, orders for many brands come from only four or five cities, and name Delhi, Bombay and Bangalore.
- They say that for a brand starting with dropshipping they give working capital at a 12% interest rate and put in equity financing if the business does well. They say their earlier equity financing of brands did not give much return.
- On discounting, they say whether a brand can sell without discounts depends on loyalty and brand trust.
- They say backing a new sales channel early is a risk and reward decision, because nobody knows whether the channel will work.

## Examples and cases used

- Meesho, Flipkart and Amazon: they say the large marketplaces depended on third party courier companies, and that with Valmo the courier companies took a hit.
- Amazon: they say it creates its own brands because it sees everything end to end.
- A shoe brand: it took two crore rupees from its manufacturers as investors.

## Numbers given

- 50,000 to 60,000 rupees: the savings the Pickrr founders say they started with.
- 24 companies in their space, of which about 10 remain, in their count.
- One big customer and two or three running customers: their target for an early B2B startup.
- 20 to 30: the next block of customers that the first few give a roadmap for.
- 20%: the share of inventory they say larger brands cap for distributed locations.
- 12%: the interest rate at which they say they give brands working capital.
