# Life journey and living without a plan, part 1, Ankur Warikoo

Ankur Warikoo, founder of Webveda and former chief executive of Nearbuy, spoke to the cohort for an afternoon. This first part is his own account of his career from a physics PhD to Groupon India, Nearbuy and his current education and content business, followed by his case for building habits instead of setting personal goals, and the start of an open question round. The second part of the same talk is a separate note.

## What was said

### From physics to an MBA

- Warikoo says he grew up in a middle class family in which some loan was always running, and nothing in his background suggested a remarkable outcome.
- He says his plan had three steps: go to the US for a PhD, specialise in space sciences, and work at NASA on Mars research.
- He says he got into Michigan State University's astrophysics PhD on a full scholarship, which was the only way his family could have afforded it.
- He says he scored well by memorising answers, and a professor's remark on one exam made him see that he was good at physics but it did not make him happy.
- He says he believed he could never be the best at something that did not make him happy, so at 24 he dropped out and came back to India.
- He says dropping out was not admired in 2004, and the conversation with his parents was very hard.
- He says he chose the one-year MBA at ISB because his classmates would have real work experience and he knew nothing about the working world.
- He says he joined with nine months of experience when the batch average was five years, and that having nothing to contribute freed him to simply absorb.
- He says he went to ISB not only for a job but to find out who he was and what he was good and not good at.

### Consulting and the first startup

- Warikoo says he picked consulting because projects changed every three to six months across industries, which let him sample everything before choosing a strength.
- He says that at 26 he was advising executives who had worked in their company longer than he had been alive, and it was the best launch his career could have had.
- He says he worked on a batchmate's matrimonial website at night for about a year and a half while keeping his consulting job, because he did not want to say no to an opportunity he could not evaluate.
- He says he then took an 80% pay cut to join the startup full time, reasoning that consulting would always be there but this opportunity might not come again.
- He says starting up in India in 2009 was not respectable and everything had to be built by brute force, and he is glad he learnt in that period.
- He says the three founders, all from the same ISB batch, sold the business around 2010 and went separate ways.

### Groupon India and Nearbuy

- Warikoo says he applied to Rocket Internet without any background check, and was told he was the only applicant from the top Indian business schools. His lesson is that sometimes not over-researching lets things work out.
- He says he started the Groupon India business in 2011, and that after Groupon's IPO the share fell from 26 dollars to about 2 dollars and most people left.
- He says he stayed because he loved the job, and as one of the few left he was handed Asia Pacific, going from a team of 200 to a team of 1,500.
- He says he was a micromanager who made his team miserable, and that from 2013 to 2015 his team trained him as a manager by telling him what he was doing wrong.
- He says that in August 2015 he completed a management buyout of Groupon's India business with Sequoia Capital as investor, after nine months of negotiation, and calls it India's first management buyout.
- He says the new company went downhill through his own mistakes: it burned money to grow because he thought that was the thing to do, then hit a funding winter and had to lay people off.
- He says the company merged with Paytm's deals business in 2017 and took in the cash that business had.
- He gives Nearbuy's later scale as about 600 crore rupees of GMV and 70 to 75 crore rupees of revenue, profitable and cash flow positive on a monthly basis.
- He says that on reaching profitability he felt done instead of renewed, and stepped down as chief executive at the end of 2019.

### Starting again in 2020

- Warikoo says that in March 2020, at 40, he had five months of money in the bank because he had drawn below-market salary and put all his savings back into Nearbuy.
- He says his advice on personal finance comes from his own mistakes, not from expertise.
- He says he and his wife agreed that if nothing worked within three months they would sell their house.
- He says that on 7 April 2020 he announced a series of five webinars and charged on a pay-what-you-want basis through a Razorpay payment page.
- He says he ran 17 pay-what-you-want webinars from April to July 2020 and about 4,000 people signed up, about half of whom paid one rupee.
- He says the webinars led him to ask whether he could record high quality courses for people without access to the institutions he had, sell them cheaply, and not teach each student live.
- He says that in September 2020 he spent 5 lakh rupees, which cut his runway by two months, to produce two courses: a one and a half hour time management course priced at 399 and an eight hour startup guide priced at 1,499, both with a full refund.
- He says he hosted the first courses on Vimeo behind a single shared password and was happy if the password was passed around, because high quality content was building trust.

### The business today

- Warikoo says his courses business has 450,000 paying students, a 100% refund policy, a refund rate of 6% and a completion rate of 72%, with fully recorded courses and one live question session a month.
- He gives his company's revenue as 20 to 25 crore rupees a year and profit as 5 to 6 crore rupees a year, and says profit fluctuates because much is reinvested.
- He says the company makes no money from social media and runs no ads on its YouTube channels by choice. It earns from the courses business and from his public speaking.
- He says he charges companies and colleges 10 lakh rupees for a one hour talk.
- He says about 80% of his time goes to the courses business and under 1% to his investments, which sit in mutual funds, portfolio management services and startup funds run by others.
- He says the core team is 10 people aged 20 to 26, fully remote, with no fixed hours, no meetings and no managers, and salaries that everyone can see.
- He says the team meets only at a five day offsite every quarter.
- He says he gives no ESOPs because he would be the only buyer of the stock. He shares 10% of profit with the team every quarter instead.
- He says team members are free to freelance for others and he pushes them to hold more than one job, because that is how they learn.
- He says his ambition is accessible, high quality education, and that the school he is building need not stay online only.

### Habits instead of goals

- Warikoo says he was told after dropping out that winning at life needs a plan, and that he could not make a new plan because he had destroyed a good one with his own hands.
- He says the question he asked instead was whether it is possible to live without a plan, and that his year at ISB was his experiment to answer it.
- He says he has not set a personal goal or target for 20 years. He sets habits and lets them compound.
- He describes himself as "a plastic bag in the wind", with no idea where he will land and a belief that another gust will come.
- He says the biggest trap a founder sets is deciding where they must be before they start, when they have no control over the outcome.
- He says a company or team needs a goal, but a founder should not attach their own worth to it, because missing it leads to self-blame.
- He says the way to choose a habit is to pick something you want to do, not something you think you should do or that would impress others.
- He says discipline is not forcing yourself awake for a gym session you hate. It is finding the form of an activity that works for you.
- He lists his own habits: reading every day, writing every day since his ISB blog, meeting one new person every week, a practice that helps him be aware of his thoughts, and a weekly block of time with no agenda.
- He says he reads all of his email and writes bold emails asking for help, and that many turns in his life came through email.
- He says everyone started creating content during Covid, and that being the last man standing is a strategy that only time and consistency can deliver.
- He says that at ISB he joined everything he could and gave up sleep and food to do it.

### Education as a script

- Warikoo retells an analogy he attributes to an Elon Musk interview: the same film script given to different directors makes different films, and education today is the same syllabus delivered at very different quality by each school.
- He says the fundamentals of each subject should be taught once by the best possible teacher and delivered to every learner electronically.

## Examples and cases used

- Rocket Internet and Groupon: he applied without researching the company's reputation, and stayed through the share price fall, which is how he came to run the region.
- "Two Bansals" selling books online: his picture of 2009, when founders had to build their own logistics and cash on delivery because nothing existed.
- Nearbuy and Paytm: the buyout, the burn, the funding winter and the 2017 merger, told as his own mistakes.
- Ali Abdaal: a lunch conversation in London about strategy, which Warikoo answers with consistency over time.
- His ISB batch of 330: he says about 28 batchmates now work in companies started by other batchmates, to make the point about the value of the community.

## Numbers given

- 1,500 dollars a month: his PhD stipend at Michigan State, which he says was more than his father earned.
- 26 dollars to about 2 dollars: Groupon's share price after listing, in his telling.
- 200 to 1,500: the team he managed before and after being given Asia Pacific at Groupon.
- About 600 crore rupees GMV and 70 to 75 crore rupees revenue: Nearbuy at the time he stepped down.
- 17 webinars and about 4,000 sign-ups: his pay-what-you-want webinars, April to July 2020.
- 450,000 paying students, 6% refund rate, 72% completion rate: his courses business.
- 20 to 25 crore rupees revenue and 5 to 6 crore rupees profit a year: his company.
- 10% of quarterly profit: the pool shared with his team.
