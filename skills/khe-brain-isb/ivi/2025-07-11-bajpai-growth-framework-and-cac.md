# Growth, CAC and branding, Amardeep Bajpai

Amardeep Bajpai taught day one of the two-day growth hacker bootcamp. He opened by asking founders for their growth challenges, then spent most of the day on one case from early in his own career: an online insurance aggregator where he led marketing and growth, which failed and was restarted as what became Policy Bazaar. From that case he built his view of customer acquisition cost, lifetime value, branding and how the pieces of a growth plan fit together. The founders' own introductions and the small-group work at the end are left out of this note.

## What was said

### The insurance aggregator that failed

- Amardeep Bajpai says the company sold car and home insurance as an aggregator in about 12 countries, mostly in Europe, and had raised more than 150 million dollars.
- He says the company faced three decisions: many markets or one, growing through partners or building its own operation, and acquisition-led or brand-led marketing.
- He says it chose many markets at once because of its vision of an insurance platform in every country, which was also the vision sold to investors.
- He says it chose partnerships, with country managers who held sizeable equity and ran each country.
- He says it chose acquisition-led growth, putting as much money as possible into online channels to bring in customers.
- He says the back end was a shared underwriting engine run from India, at a time when most insurers had no way to give premium prices online in real time.
- He says not every insurer would share its underwriting, so fewer quotes were shown and the quotes were not always accurate.
- He says country managers were responsible for fixing insurance practice and for growth at the same time. Where they chased acquisition they compromised on insurance best practice, and where they did not compromise they missed growth.
- He says the company ran for about a year and a half, and that after it could not be sold even for one dollar to investors linked to Admiral Group, it was shut down.
- He says the Lehman Brothers crisis was the trigger for the shutdown, and that how a company handles black swan events matters a great deal when it depends on continuous fundraising.
- He says that even with its mistakes the company had a good chance of succeeding if the crisis had not happened.

### Lessons he draws from the case

- On many markets or one, he says the question should be answered less by ambition and more by the product: a product with fewer complexities can be scaled across markets quickly.
- He says founders, in their ambition, assume a product will carry over to other markets when it may not.
- He says a more focused understanding of insurance as a product would have led to a better first decision.
- On partners, he says growth without partnerships is slower, partnerships are faster but bring their own problems, and partners must be chosen carefully and not too many in a short time.
- He says the company should have expanded more slowly and first learned what makes a good country manager.
- On marketing, he says the company spent almost nothing on branding in its first year and a half and put all its money into acquisition, which burned far more cash.
- He says product understanding is close to the most important input when planning growth.

### The restart

- He says the founder took six months to go over the mistakes and then raised close to 4 million dollars from new investors, including Sanjeev Bikhchandani.
- He says the restarted company made three changes: one country only until it was big there, going solo because partnerships were not its strength, and building the biggest brand in its category.
- He says the business became Policy Bazaar, with Paisa Bazaar as a second business, and that it expanded to one more country only after a long time.
- He credits its branding campaigns, naming the Ullu Mat Bano campaign and the Akshay Kumar Yamraj campaign.

### Unit economics and the CAC to lifetime value bet

- He defines unit economics as whether you make money on each unit after the cost of producing it and the cost of selling it.
- He says the cost of selling must always be included. His example is a price of 100 and a cost of goods of 80, which looks like 20 of profit until selling cost is added.
- He describes the conservative approach, which he associates with Marwari traders: treat lifetime value as equal to today's sale, and let the customer go if you do not make money on this sale.
- He says startups changed this equation by counting the return on one sale as a multiple of it, because the customer is expected to keep buying.
- He says that once you do that you are in the game of prediction: you assign a higher value to the customer and so justify more marketing spend.
- He says the first thing that goes wrong for many companies is spending heavily to acquire a customer on the assumption that lifetime value will pay for it, when the customer does not come back.
- He says insurance is different because premiums recur, so the company felt justified in spending 10 to 20 times the first payment from a customer on acquiring that customer.
- He says spending this way means a high burn rate and a constant need to raise money, and that a black swan event then cuts off the ability to raise.
- He says many e-commerce companies make two mistakes: they overestimate how many times a product will be bought, and they do not plan for why a customer would come back, such as spending on loyalty.
- He says the repeat purchase rate is the key metric that decides how far you can stretch the ratio of acquisition cost to lifetime value.
- He says that when founders present these numbers to investors they often use creative definitions, and names WeWork as a case of creative accounting.

### What goes into customer acquisition cost

- He defines customer acquisition cost as every cost spent on acquiring customers, as opposed to producing the goods, divided by the number of incremental paying customers.
- He says the customers counted are those who bought and paid, not leads and not people who saw the ad.
- He says acquisition cost includes the full cost of the marketing team and the sales team, including salaries and bonuses.
- He says margins paid to wholesalers, distributors and resellers are acquisition cost, and so are discounts.
- He says production staff and production cost belong to cost of goods sold, not to acquisition cost.
- He says that when you pay at two levels to win one customer, such as an employer and then the employee, both amounts are part of acquisition cost.
- He says offline spend is calculated over a period: the budget divided by the additional customers gained in that period.
- He says to calculate acquisition cost per channel, do the same exercise for each channel.
- He says acquisition cost is a real number you can calculate as you go, while lifetime value is typically an assumption.
- He says how much you include is a choice that makes the business look more or less profitable, and that a company seeking growth accepts a higher figure while a company consolidating spends less.
- He says leaving acquisition cost out of unit economics is a mistake that a venture-funded company pays for dearly.
- He frames the owner's question as: this money is spent on acquiring the customer, not on making the goods, so where will it come from.

### Why acquisition cost rises and how branding lowers it

- He says that without branding support the next customer from a channel such as Google or Meta typically costs more than the last, so the marginal usefulness of the channel falls.
- He says acquisition cost only falls when two ratios move in your favour: click-through rate and conversion rate.
- He says conversion rate reflects buying intent, buying intent depends on whether people know the product, and that depends on the branding work done.
- He says branding has two parts, reach and frequency: how many people in your audience you reach and how many times.
- He says reach multiplied by frequency gives gross rating points, and that higher rating points raise the chance that the audience receives the message and picks your product over a competitor's.
- He says branding reduces acquisition cost over time, and can help in the short run when acquisition marketing and brand marketing are run together.
- He says digital marketing differs from brand advertising mainly in letting you calculate acquisition cost narrowly, by channel, campaign or keyword.

### Measurable does not mean replicable or scalable

- He says: "almost all marketing is measurable", but that does not make it replicable or scalable.
- He says production and delivery can be predicted with great accuracy, while demand is extremely hard to predict.
- He says businesses go wrong when they assume today's acquisition cost will hold tomorrow.
- He says the cost stays stable only at very low volume. At even fairly high volume both acquisition cost and order numbers fluctuate heavily.
- He says the ratios along the buying journey change every day, so the final acquisition cost changes every day.
- He says people want predictable scale because it looks good in a spreadsheet, and that this does not happen in reality.
- He says a planned acquisition cost can go wrong by multiples, not by a few percentage points.
- He says heavily funded competitors will outspend a small founder because they are playing to create a monopoly.
- He says growth is non-linear and has many moving parts, including acquisition, branding, product and distribution, and that focusing on one of them does not give sustainable growth.
- He says a great product alone does not guarantee a great growth story.
- He says Meta ads can be a component of the growth story but cannot be the entire story.

### How search ads price a click

- He explains click-through rate as the share of people who saw the ad and clicked. It decides how many visitors reach the product page.
- He says Google charges by a second-price auction, a variant of the Vickrey auction, adjusted by a quality score.
- He says ads with a higher click-through rate pay a lower cost per click, which lowers acquisition cost.
- He explains conversion rate as the share of visitors who complete the purchase, and says it is the product of the ratios at each step of the funnel.
- He says a high bounce rate, where people leave the landing page quickly, is treated as a negative by Google and raises your cost.
- He says an acceptable bounce rate varies by industry and by keyword match type. An exact-match ad should have a lower bounce rate than a broad or phrase match ad.
- He says Google search is declining and AI search is rising, so this model will lose relevance, though it is still the dominant one.

### How to protect a growth plan

- His first protection is to diversify across channels and not depend on only Google or only Meta.
- He says to decide channels by looking at where your customer spends most of their time, not by what other people tell you.
- He compares digital acquisition marketing to day trading and branding to long-term fundamental investing, and says to start investing in brand early.
- He says replicability and scalability need people to fundamentally like the product.
- He says being present at the point of purchase, through distribution and shelf space, brings more stability to forecasts.
- He says that without product-market fit no amount of branding or acquisition spend works: acquisition stays loss-making and people do not remember the product.

### How brand campaigns behave

- He says a good branding campaign produces an S-curve: enquiries rise, then taper, then drift back down.
- He says companies space campaigns out, running one for a couple of months, letting its effect play for another two to three months, and launching the next before the numbers return to where they began.
- He says brand owners such as HUL and P&G keep measuring, and launch a new campaign when the curve starts to flatten.
- He says more spend does not keep raising enquiries, because people do not want to see an ad more times than it is educational or entertaining, and beyond that it creates dissatisfaction.
- He says a campaign should be a concentrated burst of a few weeks that reaches as much of the target audience as possible.
- He applies marginal utility: stop when the next unit of spend no longer brings a positive return.
- He says a product that is at the point of purchase with few alternatives may not see the tapering and may not need another brand campaign for a long time.
- He says that if you reach very high rating points and still see no growth, the product is not good enough and needs a second look, not more money.
- He says acquisition activity must run during a brand campaign and continue after it, and should never be switched off.
- He says a brand built only on acquisition through constant discounts loses its value perception.
- He says discounting does not work when exclusivity or rarity is the value being sold.
- He says a strong brand still needs a plan to convert awareness and visitors into customers.

### Staying focused with limited resources

- Asked how a small startup can manage all these elements, he says the key is to stay focused and to measure success and failure continuously.
- He describes a founder who decided in advance to give each business one and a half to two years, measure it week on week, and shut it down if it missed its milestone, without becoming attached to it.
- He says founder stories are useful only for takeaways you can apply, not for being awed by the person.
- He says thinking the problem statement through carefully and designing the campaign around it makes the task much easier.
- He recalls being told by his former chief that the job is to make a few right decisions in a month.

## Examples and cases used

- The insurance aggregator that became Policy Bazaar: the main case, used for all three decisions and for the cost of ignoring branding.
- Confused.com: named to show that large insurance aggregators already existed in the West, so the idea was not too early.
- WeWork: his example of creative definitions used in front of investors.
- An online soap seller: assumes a customer will buy nine times a year and sets marketing spend on that assumption.
- A brass cup priced at 1,000 rupees sold through a search ad on a buy-intent keyword: his walk through click-through, landing page, conversion and bounce.
- Mumbai's dabbawalas: used to show that operations can be predicted very accurately, unlike demand.
- ibibo: ran a great deal of advertising and became well known, but people did not know what to do there, so awareness did not convert. He says MakeMyTrip later bought it.
- HUL and P&G: time new campaigns from measurement and never stop running acquisition ads.
- Rolex: an exclusivity brand that should not discount.
- A clothing brand that sold only on heavy discounts: lost its value perception despite good products.
- A friend's FMCG business in Kerala: combined distribution, branding and shelf presence with digital spend.
- A former Coca-Cola leader: told him that when a campaign shows no traction the answer is to redefine the product, not to add money.
- HCL: a B2B company that ran TV brand advertising in India while its clients were in the US. He says the biggest reason was to win the war for talent.
- Junglee Games: the founder ran nine businesses before it. Its rummy campaigns were aimed at high rollers.

## Numbers given

- More than 150 million dollars: what he says the first insurance venture raised. About 12 countries, about a year and a half before it shut.
- Close to 4 million dollars: what he says the restart raised.
- 10,000 rupees of acquisition spend against 100 rupees of first-year brokerage: his illustration of the insurance bet, a first-year loss of 9,900 per customer.
- 200 rupees of brokerage against 1,000 rupees spent to acquire: a second illustration, leaving a gap of 800 per customer to fund.
- 4,000 rupees of Meta spend and five paying customers gives a CAC of 800: his worked example.
- 5 crore rupees of TV spend and one lakh additional customers gives 500 rupees per customer: his offline example.
- 5% to 15%: the margins he says are paid at each level of a distribution chain.
- 100 rupees for the 100th customer, up to 110 for the next and up to 120 by the 150th: his illustration of rising incremental cost.
- 50 rupees planned and 500 rupees actual: his illustration of how far acquisition cost can drift.
- Under 30%: the bounce rate he suggests aiming for, with the caveat that it varies by industry.
- 60 to 70%: the share of a real-money rummy platform's revenue he attributes to high rollers, and also the share of India's real-money rummy players he places in two markets, Hyderabad with Andhra and Telangana, and Chennai.
