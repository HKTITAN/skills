# Why Not, finding ideas and the Honest Tea story, Barry Nalebuff

Typed notes from a session with Prof Barry Nalebuff of the Yale School of Management, who teaches negotiation, innovation and entrepreneurship. He gave ways to find business ideas and tested them against his own company, Honest Tea.

## What was said

### Start from the customer's problem

- Barry Nalebuff's starting question is what the customer's problem is and how he can solve it.
- His example is a wart remover, where a small bottle lasts a lifetime. The real customer is children who walk barefoot, so parents buy it to protect the child.
- He uses the story of the princess and the pea to say founders should think about the annoyances they have learned to ignore, which he calls your peas.
- He says that once you find the pea, the next question is whether you can make a business out of it.
- He asks the class for the peas of chocolate. The notes list melting, sugar, caffeine that keeps children up, cavities, sourcing and not being vegan.
- He says that if you solve these problems you can increase the size of your market, for example with decaffeinated chocolate for children.

### Look for violations of basic economics

- He says to look for violations of basic economics, because then either the market is wrong or the economics is wrong.
- His example is gin and tonic. Schweppes tonic water has a lot of fructose and corn syrup, so a new tonic with better quality lets you taste the quality of the gin.
- In bottled tea he asked why brands kept adding extra sugar when it only cost more and did not improve the taste.

### Is it a good idea for you

- He says most ideas are good ideas for someone else, and you may be the wrong person to do them.
- His example is renting hotel rooms for any 24 hours, the way cars are rented. He asks why it would not work for him, and the answer is that somebody else can do it better.
- He says a hotel chain such as Taj could copy the 24 hour idea with very little effort.
- His test is whether the product will continue to succeed even after it has been copied.
- He says incumbents find it hard to copy when they would have to change an existing product and risk their existing customers, so ask whether you can make something they stand against.
- He says Honest Tea was not copied because there was not enough organic tea, and because it did not have to taste the same as the others.
- He says not to make something 10% better, but to make it radically different and better.

### Honest Tea

- He says neither founder of Honest Tea had any experience in tea or in consumer products.
- He says a combination of economic theory, luck and passion brought the success.
- He says the problem they saw was that bottled teas were too sweet, and they asked whether they could do for tea what Starbucks had done for coffee.
- He says to make every word on the pack count, because there are only a few words and people are wearing and consuming your brand.
- He says to act quickly and fix costs later, accepting higher variable costs in the short run.
- His two early questions are whether you can make it and whether people will buy it.
- He says Oprah came across the tea, liked it and wrote about it, and uses this to say an entrepreneur must be prepared.
- He says the company found a second market with Honest Kids.
- He counts as misses that someone else took the honest idea into diapers, home care and makeup, and that Honest Tea did not make a better-for-you soda like Poppi.

## Examples and cases used

- Wart remover: the buyer is the parent and the reason is protecting the child.
- M&M: a chocolate that answers the melting problem.
- Schweppes tonic water: a mixer whose sugar hides the gin, leaving room for a better tonic.
- Honest Tea: less sugar when rivals kept adding it, and a product incumbents could not easily copy.
- Taj hotels: an incumbent that could copy a 24 hour room rental idea with little effort.
