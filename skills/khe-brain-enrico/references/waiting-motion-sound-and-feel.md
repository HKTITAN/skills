# Waiting, motion, sound and feel

This theme is the layer of a product you sense before you think: how it makes you wait, how it moves, what it sounds like, how its text is drawn and how its buttons and materials feel in the hand. Most of it comes from the-sneaky-psychology-of-loading-screens, the-ridiculous-engineering-of-loading-screens, the-ridiculous-engineering-of-animations, why-our-tech-got-silent, the-ridiculous-engineering-of-text, the-lies-that-make-hardware-actually-work and what-happened-to-physical-tech, with details scattered across his hidden-details videos. Product facts and numbers are his research as of each video's date, his Flask examples are from his own product, and his statements about AI and future devices carry their year.

## Why does how you wait matter more than how long you wait?

- His core position on waiting is that the way a product makes you wait counts for more than the length of the wait, which he puts as "the psychology of waiting is more important than the wait itself". (The Sneaky Psychology Of Loading Screens at 0:45, https://www.youtube.com/watch?v=g8uhdyLTAvY&t=45s)
- He says small delays cost real money: by his account Amazon found that 100 milliseconds of added latency lost one percent of sales, and Google found that half a second of extra load time lost 20 percent of traffic. (The Sneaky Psychology Of Loading Screens at 0:00, https://www.youtube.com/watch?v=g8uhdyLTAvY&t=0s)
- He tells the Houston airport story to show the point: hiring staff to cut baggage wait times did not reduce complaints, but moving baggage claim far from the plane so people walked six times as long dropped complaints to almost zero. (The Sneaky Psychology Of Loading Screens at 0:45, https://www.youtube.com/watch?v=g8uhdyLTAvY&t=45s)
- He cites a 1985 paper, The Psychology of Waiting Lines, for the principle he states as "Occupied time feels shorter than unoccupied time." (The Ridiculous Engineering of Loading Screens at 9:00, https://www.youtube.com/watch?v=XqhduyTnEM8&t=540s)
- He says Uber shows the car moving on a map when it could just say 3 minutes, and Domino's shows each pizza step, because reducing the perceived wait is easier than delivering faster. (The Ridiculous Engineering of Loading Screens at 9:00, https://www.youtube.com/watch?v=XqhduyTnEM8&t=540s)
- He says explaining the work makes people wait longer and like the product more: Google Flights and Skyscanner show status messages while they search, and he cites a Harvard Business School study where users rated the product higher because they trusted the search was thorough. (The Ridiculous Engineering of Loading Screens at 8:15, https://www.youtube.com/watch?v=XqhduyTnEM8&t=495s)
- He says those status messages can be fake and still work: Skyscanner names real airlines for the route, but Google Flights shows the same pre-programmed text for the same time on every search. (The Ridiculous Engineering of Loading Screens at 8:15, https://www.youtube.com/watch?v=XqhduyTnEM8&t=495s)
- He uses the goal gradient effect, the idea that we speed up when the finish line is in sight, to explain why a bar that starts slow and speeds up near the end feels faster, even if you make the whole bar take longer. (The Ridiculous Engineering of Loading Screens at 9:45, https://www.youtube.com/watch?v=XqhduyTnEM8&t=585s)
- He says Ridge Racer on PlayStation in 1994 let you play a round of Galaxian while it loaded, and that Namco patented the idea so nobody else could put a mini game in a loading screen for 20 years. (The Ridiculous Engineering of Loading Screens at 10:30, https://www.youtube.com/watch?v=XqhduyTnEM8&t=630s)
- He argues that faster chips and networks will never make everything instant, because the faster they get the more we ask of them, as when video moved from 1080p to 4K as soon as speed allowed. (The Sneaky Psychology Of Loading Screens at 6:00, https://www.youtube.com/watch?v=g8uhdyLTAvY&t=360s)
- In 2022 he predicted that AR glasses would be the next big class of devices and would still have load times, and he said managing human impatience is a problem we will have to deal with forever. (The Sneaky Psychology Of Loading Screens at 6:45, https://www.youtube.com/watch?v=g8uhdyLTAvY&t=405s)

## How should a loading state be built?

- In 2022 he walked through how products handle a wait, starting with telling people how long it will take, which he says is very hard to estimate and creates an expectation, and then the spinning wheel. (The Sneaky Psychology Of Loading Screens at 1:30, https://www.youtube.com/watch?v=g8uhdyLTAvY&t=90s)
- He says time estimates fail on screens people are actively watching because they create an expectation, and even a one second miss breaks trust with the app, though an estimate can work for moving files in the background. (The Sneaky Psychology Of Loading Screens at 2:15, https://www.youtube.com/watch?v=g8uhdyLTAvY&t=135s)
- He is blunt that the spinning wheel is a poor answer: it blocks interaction, puts the focus on waiting, then drops you into a fully loaded screen your brain has to parse all at once. (The Sneaky Psychology Of Loading Screens at 3:45, https://www.youtube.com/watch?v=g8uhdyLTAvY&t=225s)
- He says skeleton loaders make the same wait feel shorter when tested against spinners, and that YouTube, Netflix, LinkedIn and Google Photos all use them. (The Sneaky Psychology Of Loading Screens at 4:30, https://www.youtube.com/watch?v=g8uhdyLTAvY&t=270s)
- His reason is that a skeleton points attention at what is coming, so your brain gets busy working out the hierarchy of the grey boxes instead of sitting in waiting mode. (The Sneaky Psychology Of Loading Screens at 5:15, https://www.youtube.com/watch?v=g8uhdyLTAvY&t=315s)
- He says skeleton loading is modular, so you can use the LinkedIn side menu before the feed arrives or tap a still-loading video in Google Photos and wait only for that. (The Sneaky Psychology Of Loading Screens at 5:15, https://www.youtube.com/watch?v=g8uhdyLTAvY&t=315s)
- He notes that skeleton loaders actually take more time, because the outlines and their animation must load too, yet they reduce perceived time and tell you the page structure before the content arrives. (The Lies That Make Your Tech Actually Work at 8:15, https://www.youtube.com/watch?v=rAVV4kAYlI8&t=495s)
- He says a spinner that flashes too briefly reads as an error, so on a form he sets a minimum spinner time of 1 second, which makes the wait longer but more reassuring. (The Ridiculous Engineering of Loading Screens at 0:00, https://www.youtube.com/watch?v=XqhduyTnEM8&t=0s)
- He calls an accurate progress bar a hard engineering problem: counting files breaks with one giant file, counting megabytes breaks because many small files take longer than one big one, and dividing by elapsed time breaks when the speed changes. (The Ridiculous Engineering of Loading Screens at 3:00, https://www.youtube.com/watch?v=XqhduyTnEM8&t=180s)
- He says most properly designed progress bars estimate from the average speed over a recent window such as the last 10 seconds, and in 2024 he said there is still no real solution to the problem. (The Ridiculous Engineering of Loading Screens at 3:45, https://www.youtube.com/watch?v=XqhduyTnEM8&t=225s; How Your Tech Tricks You at 7:30, https://www.youtube.com/watch?v=7nnyv4yJGJc&t=450s)
- He says a progress bar that is too smooth feels wrong, which is why the perfectly smooth bars on scammy sites feel weird. (How Your Tech Tricks You at 9:00, https://www.youtube.com/watch?v=7nnyv4yJGJc&t=540s)
- He cites Chris Harrison at Carnegie Mellon for the finding that stripes moving against the direction of progress make a bar feel 11 percent faster, and says this is why shimmer on progress bars is everywhere and not just decoration. (The Ridiculous Engineering of Loading Screens at 4:30, https://www.youtube.com/watch?v=XqhduyTnEM8&t=270s)
- He doubled the rotation speed of a spinner in Flask and says the identical loading time felt much faster, as if more was going on. (The Ridiculous Engineering of Loading Screens at 5:15, https://www.youtube.com/watch?v=XqhduyTnEM8&t=315s)
- He says many loading screens can be removed by assuming success, which is why Notion, Figma and Google Docs feel instant, and in Flask he shows the change at once, saves it from a background queue and tells the user only if a save fails. (The Ridiculous Engineering of Loading Screens at 0:45, https://www.youtube.com/watch?v=XqhduyTnEM8&t=45s; The Ridiculous Engineering of Loading Screens at 1:30, https://www.youtube.com/watch?v=XqhduyTnEM8&t=90s)
- His reason for optimistic updates is that the frustration of slowness is much higher than the need to know with full certainty that every action was saved before you see it. (How Your Tech Is Built For You To Fail at 12:45, https://www.youtube.com/watch?v=4HWPFd8CQo8&t=765s)
- He then qualifies it: instant is technically better but not always trusted more, and for an important form the slower version with a loading state is trusted more even though the data was saved in both cases. (The Ridiculous Engineering of Loading Screens at 2:15, https://www.youtube.com/watch?v=XqhduyTnEM8&t=135s)
- His rule is that whether to show loading depends on how important the action feels: dozens of small changes a minute should not show it, but a payment confirmation needs it to reassure people even if the bytes involved are tiny. (The Ridiculous Engineering of Loading Screens at 2:15, https://www.youtube.com/watch?v=XqhduyTnEM8&t=135s)
- He presents Gmail loading your account once the username is typed, and Instagram uploading the photo while you write the caption, as the same trick: act early on the likely outcome and throw the work away if you were wrong. (How Your Tech Is Built For You To Fail at 10:30, https://www.youtube.com/watch?v=4HWPFd8CQo8&t=630s)
- He says short-form feeds hide loading on purpose by preloading the first frame of the next video, so you see a preview and never realize you are waiting. (The Ridiculous Engineering Of Short Form Content at 3:45, https://www.youtube.com/watch?v=69Gw7aoWMMI&t=225s)

## Why do AI products make you wait on purpose?

- In 2026 he said waiting matters more than before because since ChatGPT arrived in November 2022 people spend far more time waiting on chatbots, and that wait time is directly tied to how intelligent we perceive these models to be. (The Ridiculous Engineering of Loading Screens at 11:15, https://www.youtube.com/watch?v=XqhduyTnEM8&t=675s)
- He shows two chatbots and says most people pick the one that takes longer and writes the answer word by word as giving the better, more accurate answer, because it feels smarter and more thoughtful, like it is actually thinking. (The Ridiculous Engineering of Loading Screens at 12:00, https://www.youtube.com/watch?v=XqhduyTnEM8&t=720s)
- He deliberately made Flask users wait longer: Flask gets the whole AI answer back at once, showing it all at once felt weird, so he reveals it word by word with a delay and says users trusted it more. (The Ridiculous Engineering of Loading Screens at 12:00, https://www.youtube.com/watch?v=XqhduyTnEM8&t=720s)
- His line is "When it comes to AI, we don't want fast." He means we want the fastest response we still trust, and he cites the effort heuristic, the belief that something that takes longer must be better. (The Ridiculous Engineering of Loading Screens at 13:30, https://www.youtube.com/watch?v=XqhduyTnEM8&t=810s)
- In 2026 he said Google's pride in speed now hurts its AI: AI Overviews had to be fast, which means less intelligent models, and every mistake breaks trust. (The Ridiculous Engineering of Loading Screens at 13:30, https://www.youtube.com/watch?v=XqhduyTnEM8&t=810s)
- His March 2026 prediction is that better AI will not end waiting, because people will always want the model that makes them wait since that is the most intelligent one, though not for 3 hours. (The Ridiculous Engineering of Loading Screens at 14:15, https://www.youtube.com/watch?v=XqhduyTnEM8&t=855s)
- In 2026 he said watching Claude Code read files, write code and run tests is itself a reward, citing what he calls the labor illusion from Harvard researchers, where a generic AI tool would hide the work behind a spinning wheel. (Why Everyone is Suddenly Obsessed With Claude Code at 4:30, https://www.youtube.com/watch?v=gzt52Trk9w0&t=270s)

## What is animation for, and when should it be removed?

- He argues interface animation is not decoration: changing the app-open animation so apps slide in from the right would rewire your sense of where apps live in the whole operating system. (The Ridiculous Engineering Of Animations at 0:00, https://www.youtube.com/watch?v=z3Lo5QT7nME&t=0s)
- He calls **spatial consistency** the first principle of animation: motion builds a map in space of where intangible things like apps live, as when the Dynamic Island expands into an app. (The Ridiculous Engineering Of Animations at 0:00, https://www.youtube.com/watch?v=z3Lo5QT7nME&t=0s)
- He says the foldable iPhone's fold animation is engineering, not an aesthetic trick: a hinge sensor measures the angle 60 times a second, the UI tilts in 3D around the center, and a progressive blur and vignette are added, and he says it worked because it was the first spatial consistency between the two screens of a foldable. (The Ridiculous Engineering Of Animations at 1:30, https://www.youtube.com/watch?v=z3Lo5QT7nME&t=90s)
- His first rule of animation is to copy nature, because our brains are hardwired for how real things move while digital things happen instantly. (The Ridiculous Engineering Of Animations at 2:15, https://www.youtube.com/watch?v=z3Lo5QT7nME&t=135s)
- He credits two Disney animators with writing the 12 principles of animation in 1981, and says slow in and slow out became easing, while squash and stretch is behind the feel of liquid glass in iOS. (The Ridiculous Engineering Of Animations at 3:45, https://www.youtube.com/watch?v=z3Lo5QT7nME&t=225s)
- He says a swipe that cannot be interrupted feels wrong because the real world does not work that way, and praises the iPhone app switcher for never dismissing an app until you actually flick it away. (The Lies That Make Your Tech Actually Work at 3:45, https://www.youtube.com/watch?v=rAVV4kAYlI8&t=225s)
- He says smooth scrolling copies a car slowed by friction, that a linear slowdown looks bad because most of nature is not linear, and that the bounce at the end of a list is a modelled spring. (The Lies That Make Your Tech Actually Work at 6:00, https://www.youtube.com/watch?v=rAVV4kAYlI8&t=360s; The Lies That Make Your Tech Actually Work at 6:45, https://www.youtube.com/watch?v=rAVV4kAYlI8&t=405s)
- After the rule of copying nature he adds: "But there's a problem. Nature is slow." He says Spotlight's liquid glass animation makes the search feature completely unusable because the point of a launcher is to open things fast. (The Ridiculous Engineering Of Animations at 7:30, https://www.youtube.com/watch?v=z3Lo5QT7nME&t=450s)
- He says an animation is there to teach, and by the hundredth time it teaches nothing and only makes you wait. (The Ridiculous Engineering Of Animations at 8:15, https://www.youtube.com/watch?v=z3Lo5QT7nME&t=495s)
- He says products built for real work are restrained: a right-click menu just appears, the chosen item blinks, and the menu fades out, with motion only on the way out to confirm the action. (The Ridiculous Engineering Of Animations at 9:00, https://www.youtube.com/watch?v=z3Lo5QT7nME&t=540s)
- He says knowing when not to animate is probably more important than knowing when to, and that he learned it in Flask, where animated sidebar items were cool once and then made the product clunky. (The Ridiculous Engineering Of Animations at 9:45, https://www.youtube.com/watch?v=z3Lo5QT7nME&t=585s)
- His Flask practice is instant for anything used hundreds of times a day, animated for things users must learn exist, and animated where the user is waiting anyway, such as a file upload. (The Ridiculous Engineering Of Animations at 9:45, https://www.youtube.com/watch?v=z3Lo5QT7nME&t=585s)
- He says some feedback must stay even with animations off: Pokémon Red and Blue still shake the screen when you are hit, because without it you would not know you took damage. (The Ridiculous Engineering Of Animations at 11:15, https://www.youtube.com/watch?v=z3Lo5QT7nME&t=675s)
- He says the Mac's desktop-switch slide is right for spatial consistency and still hated by thousands, himself included, so much that he never uses full screen on a Mac. (The Ridiculous Engineering Of Animations at 11:15, https://www.youtube.com/watch?v=z3Lo5QT7nME&t=675s)
- His third rule is to use speed through easing: the same object, action and duration feel mechanical with linear motion, slow with ease in, snappy with ease out and smooth with ease in out. (The Ridiculous Engineering Of Animations at 12:00, https://www.youtube.com/watch?v=z3Lo5QT7nME&t=720s)
- He says most motion on a phone is a spring defined by stiffness and damping, not a duration. (The Ridiculous Engineering Of Animations at 13:30, https://www.youtube.com/watch?v=z3Lo5QT7nME&t=810s)
- He says animation gives a product personality, the way Spider-Verse animates Miles on twos and Peter Parker on ones so their movement tells you who they are. (The Ridiculous Engineering Of Animations at 15:45, https://www.youtube.com/watch?v=z3Lo5QT7nME&t=945s)
- He made Flask's toolbar bouncy and slower as a test and says it at once felt less like a work tool, and he says Duolingo gives its streak the best animation and has bought three animation studios since 2022. (The Ridiculous Engineering Of Animations at 17:15, https://www.youtube.com/watch?v=z3Lo5QT7nME&t=1035s)
- He says animation can get a product in legal trouble: Robinhood showed confetti after a first trade and agreed in a 7.5 million dollar Massachusetts settlement to stop using it for good. (The Ridiculous Engineering Of Animations at 18:00, https://www.youtube.com/watch?v=z3Lo5QT7nME&t=1080s)
- He says sometimes the right move is to add time: when Wise made transfers instant users did not believe it, so Wise added a short animation of the money arriving and more people trusted the transfer. (The Ridiculous Engineering Of Animations at 15:00, https://www.youtube.com/watch?v=z3Lo5QT7nME&t=900s)
- He says what game developers call juice, tech people call craft: a Breakout clone made to feel good with color, eased motion, sound, particles and screen shake while the game underneath stays the same. (The Weird Way Tech Steals From Games at 6:00, https://www.youtube.com/watch?v=GypAKofWGBk&t=360s)
- In November 2025 he said playful animation is returning, and praised YouTube's topic-based like animations as shipped because they are fun even though they will not improve conversion or engagement. (How Silicon Valley Killed Fun at 15:45, https://www.youtube.com/watch?v=DYdLq9icgrI&t=945s)

## What makes a product feel fast?

- He says the app-open animation is **buying time**: the icon starts growing the moment you lift your finger while the app is still starting, and Apple's guidelines ask developers to draw the first frame within 400 milliseconds. (The Ridiculous Engineering Of Animations at 14:15, https://www.youtube.com/watch?v=z3Lo5QT7nME&t=855s)
- He says Android adds about 5 milliseconds of delay to touch on purpose and can predict up to 80 milliseconds ahead to align touches with screen refresh, so your finger position is calculated, not measured. (The Ridiculous Engineering Of Animations at 15:00, https://www.youtube.com/watch?v=z3Lo5QT7nME&t=900s)
- He says a phone does not register a tap when your finger lands but waits to see if you move: in Android's source the tap timeout is 100 milliseconds and the touch slop is 8 dp, and he reports the iPhone as about 150 milliseconds. (The Most Important Tiny Decision In Tech History at 4:30, https://www.youtube.com/watch?v=7EVZpW9Rdwk&t=270s)
- He says TikTok, Reels and Shorts built their own scroll physics because system scrolling is too slow, which is why a small swipe already moves you to the next video. (The Ridiculous Engineering Of Animations at 10:30, https://www.youtube.com/watch?v=z3Lo5QT7nME&t=630s; The Ridiculous Engineering Of Short Form Content at 0:00, https://www.youtube.com/watch?v=69Gw7aoWMMI&t=0s)
- He treats speed as worth deep engineering, saying he spent over a week reworking Flask's back end so a dropped file opens instantly instead of after a few seconds. (The Ridiculous Engineering Of Animations at 10:30, https://www.youtube.com/watch?v=z3Lo5QT7nME&t=630s)
- One of his own phone tips is to set all three Android animation scale options to 0.5x in developer options so the phone feels faster. (How I Finally Fixed My Digital Life at 10:30, https://www.youtube.com/watch?v=7PhEfKCHr7Y&t=630s)
- He says a dark mode background should not be pure black, because on an OLED screen black pixels switch off and take slightly longer to turn back on, which shows as lag when content scrolls. (Why Simple Tech is Almost Impossible to Build at 5:15, https://www.youtube.com/watch?v=qSQZ8B-2nU0&t=315s)
- He says the mouse is intuitive but slow, which is why products such as Arc, Linear and Superhuman make the keyboard and a command bar the main input. (The Secrets That Make Your Phone Actually Work at 9:45, https://www.youtube.com/watch?v=-YVtZV4DjCE&t=585s)
- He uses Fitts's law, that the time to hit a target depends on its distance and size, to explain why right-click menus feel good and why Spotify's desktop album panel opening on the far side of the screen feels wrong. (How Your Tech Is Built For You To Fail at 7:30, https://www.youtube.com/watch?v=4HWPFd8CQo8&t=450s; How Your Tech Is Built For You To Fail at 9:45, https://www.youtube.com/watch?v=4HWPFd8CQo8&t=585s)

## How should taps, buttons and gestures feel on a screen?

- He says anything happening on an interface needs feedback, because feedback confirms the system is working and a button without it looks off. (The Lies That Make Your Tech Actually Work at 1:30, https://www.youtube.com/watch?v=rAVV4kAYlI8&t=90s)
- His steps for a well-built button are feedback on touch, action on release and not on press, cancel if the finger leaves, an extra tap area around the button, and feedback again if the finger returns. (The Lies That Make Your Tech Actually Work at 1:30, https://www.youtube.com/watch?v=rAVV4kAYlI8&t=90s; How Your Tech Is Built For You To Fail at 6:00, https://www.youtube.com/watch?v=4HWPFd8CQo8&t=360s)
- He says the way to design good digital controls is to copy physical ones: a digital button gets a press state because a real button moves and reflects light differently, and it fires on release because a real button only fires at the bottom of its travel. (What Happened To Physical Tech at 2:15, https://www.youtube.com/watch?v=TIFF-rinlbo&t=135s)
- He says the phone keyboard looks normal but resizes each key's invisible touch area by the most likely next letter, so after typing th the area for e grows much bigger. (The Lies That Make Your Tech Actually Work at 0:45, https://www.youtube.com/watch?v=rAVV4kAYlI8&t=45s)
- He says letter pop-ups on a phone keyboard replace the physical feel of a key, and that the space bar has them switched off on both iOS and Android because it is the most used key and too big to miss. (Why Simple Tech is Almost Impossible to Build at 0:00, https://www.youtube.com/watch?v=qSQZ8B-2nU0&t=0s)
- He argues the **core input** of a device lets you predict which tech succeeds: on phones scrolling comes first and tapping second, so the screen itself becomes the control and the arrows and buttons disappear. (The Most Important Tiny Decision In Tech History at 5:15, https://www.youtube.com/watch?v=7EVZpW9Rdwk&t=315s; The Most Important Tiny Decision In Tech History at 6:00, https://www.youtube.com/watch?v=7EVZpW9Rdwk&t=360s)
- He says smart TVs are hated because an interface designed for scrolling is driven by four arrows and a button, and that when Apple shipped a touch-surface remote in 2015 everyone hated it and Apple went back to buttons. (The Most Important Tiny Decision In Tech History at 12:00, https://www.youtube.com/watch?v=7EVZpW9Rdwk&t=720s; The Most Important Tiny Decision In Tech History at 12:45, https://www.youtube.com/watch?v=7EVZpW9Rdwk&t=765s)
- In 2023 he said natural hand-based interaction for AR has to be built again from scratch over the next 10 years, that Apple's eye tracking cannot scale down to smart glasses, and that keyboards in AR will be a real pain. (The Lies That Make Your Tech Actually Work at 9:45, https://www.youtube.com/watch?v=rAVV4kAYlI8&t=585s; The Lies That Make Your Tech Actually Work at 10:30, https://www.youtube.com/watch?v=rAVV4kAYlI8&t=630s)
- In 2026 he said the Humane AI Pin's projector and clunky gestures looked broken even in the demo, while everyone who tried Meta's neural band, himself included, found the input natural. (The Most Important Tiny Decision In Tech History at 12:45, https://www.youtube.com/watch?v=7EVZpW9Rdwk&t=765s)

## Why did tech go silent, and what is sound still for?

- He says old tech sounds were functional: tasks in the 80s and 90s took time, so the startup sound let you walk away and hear when the computer was ready, and his own Windows 98 PC took more than 3 or 4 minutes to boot. (Why Our Tech Got Silent at 1:30, https://www.youtube.com/watch?v=_rFJutK25VU&t=90s)
- He says we perceive in the order sound, then icon, then text, so you knew there was an error from the sound before you read anything. (Why Our Tech Got Silent at 3:00, https://www.youtube.com/watch?v=_rFJutK25VU&t=180s)
- He says abstract interface sounds work because the brain has built-in rules, citing researchers who found that across cultures and ages people map high pitch to up and low pitch to down. (Why Our Tech Got Silent at 3:45, https://www.youtube.com/watch?v=_rFJutK25VU&t=225s)
- From that he says the right sound for an event is predictable: ascending tones for connecting, descending for disconnecting, blended high notes from one scale for success, and clashing lower notes for failure. (Why Our Tech Got Silent at 4:30, https://www.youtube.com/watch?v=_rFJutK25VU&t=270s)
- His first sound design rule is that length is the inverse of frequency: long sounds for rare events like turning on a computer, short ones for frequent events like a key press. (Why Our Tech Got Silent at 5:15, https://www.youtube.com/watch?v=_rFJutK25VU&t=315s)
- His second rule is stacking, layering several sounds to add nuance, with the Windows 95 startup sound by Brian Eno as the example of a long, layered sound that also called you back to a slow computer. (Why Our Tech Got Silent at 5:15, https://www.youtube.com/watch?v=_rFJutK25VU&t=315s)
- He says sound metaphors died with the visual ones: nobody crumples paper any more, and a laptop that wakes in milliseconds leaves nothing for a startup chime to announce. (Why Our Tech Got Silent at 9:00, https://www.youtube.com/watch?v=_rFJutK25VU&t=540s)
- He tells how Jensen Harris removed the startup sound from Windows 8 after realizing computers had become personal devices used in bedrooms next to sleeping children, and says Apple disabled its startup sound in 2016. (Why Our Tech Got Silent at 9:00, https://www.youtube.com/watch?v=_rFJutK25VU&t=540s)
- He says the phone finished sound off: ringtones were once a status symbol people paid for, now everyone is on silent, which is also why all short-form content has subtitles. (Why Our Tech Got Silent at 9:45, https://www.youtube.com/watch?v=_rFJutK25VU&t=585s)
- He says feedback did not disappear but moved to haptics, which is why most people turn off keyboard typing sounds and phones play no sound for alert boxes. (Why Our Tech Got Silent at 10:30, https://www.youtube.com/watch?v=_rFJutK25VU&t=630s)
- He cites researchers who compared notification sound on, vibration only and fully silent, and found the silent group checked their phones more, which he explains as the anxiety of never knowing whether something happened. (Why Our Tech Got Silent at 11:15, https://www.youtube.com/watch?v=_rFJutK25VU&t=675s)
- His verdict is "Sounds didn't disappear because they were stressful." He says they went because computers changed from work tools on desks to personal companions in pockets. (Why Our Tech Got Silent at 12:00, https://www.youtube.com/watch?v=_rFJutK25VU&t=720s)
- He says branding sounds grew louder as functional ones died, because flat interfaces after iOS 7 left brands unable to stand out visually, which is why Apple brought its boot sound back in 2020 and why Duolingo's sounds tell everyone nearby what you are doing. (Why Our Tech Got Silent at 12:45, https://www.youtube.com/watch?v=_rFJutK25VU&t=765s)
- He calls a volume control a lie because loudness perception is logarithmic, so the slider must follow an exponential curve to feel linear, and he says iOS and Android do this while YouTube's desktop slider keeps all its control in the last 10 percent. (How Your Tech Tricks You at 0:00, https://www.youtube.com/watch?v=7nnyv4yJGJc&t=0s; How Your Tech Tricks You at 0:45, https://www.youtube.com/watch?v=7nnyv4yJGJc&t=45s)

## What does text on a screen hide?

- He says an emoji is not one simple character: the family emoji is stored as the codes of four emojis joined by a special character he calls **digital glue**. (The Ridiculous Engineering Of Text at 0:00, https://www.youtube.com/watch?v=7DN3Ectm6UY&t=0s)
- He says the number of letters is not the number of shapes, because fonts merge some adjacent letters into one shape, and the system must also store where the cursor sits inside that shape so arrow keys still work. (The Ridiculous Engineering Of Text at 1:30, https://www.youtube.com/watch?v=7DN3Ectm6UY&t=90s)
- He says vector fonts solved scaling but not the pixel grid, so each time a letter is drawn the renderer bends its curves slightly to land on pixels and uses partly transparent pixels to smooth it. (The Ridiculous Engineering Of Text at 3:45, https://www.youtube.com/watch?v=7DN3Ectm6UY&t=225s)
- He says fonts ship with a list of thousands of letter pairs and how far apart each should sit, because an r and an n rendered badly can turn into a blobby m, and he points to one recent phishing attack that used exactly this trick, sending mail from "rnicrosoft.com" to pass as microsoft.com. (The Ridiculous Engineering Of Text at 4:30, https://www.youtube.com/watch?v=7DN3Ectm6UY&t=270s)
- He says the same font looks different on Windows and Mac because Windows renders for legibility on bad low-resolution screens while the Mac preserves the font's shape and smoothness, which he says gives the Mac a more premium feel. (The Ridiculous Engineering Of Text at 4:30, https://www.youtube.com/watch?v=7DN3Ectm6UY&t=270s; The Secrets That Make Your Phone Actually Work at 14:15, https://www.youtube.com/watch?v=-YVtZV4DjCE&t=855s)
- He says the text cursor blinks so it is easy to find at rest and stays solid while you type so it does not distract, something he says he noticed only after 25 years of using computers. (The Lies That Make Your Tech Actually Work at 3:00, https://www.youtube.com/watch?v=rAVV4kAYlI8&t=180s)
- He calls moving a cursor up and down a nightmare to build: letters have different widths, so you need a nearest-position algorithm, a memory of the column you came from, and a reset of that memory when you move sideways. (The Ridiculous Engineering Of Text at 6:00, https://www.youtube.com/watch?v=7DN3Ectm6UY&t=360s)
- He says fixed-width text is still right in places: in Flask he sets the video playback time in a monospace font so the colon does not jump as digits change, and he says YouTube's player does the same. (The Ridiculous Engineering Of Text at 6:45, https://www.youtube.com/watch?v=7DN3Ectm6UY&t=405s)
- He says QWERTY is slow, inefficient and uncomfortable because it was laid out to stop typewriter hammers jamming, and that with the whole world on it the layout is basically impossible to change. (The Secrets That Make Your Phone Actually Work at 9:00, https://www.youtube.com/watch?v=-YVtZV4DjCE&t=540s)
- In 2026 he drew a line between **fuzzy text** and perfect text: typing or dictating into an AI chat box works with typos and rambling, while he has never managed to use dictation for emails or messages. (The Ridiculous Engineering Of Text at 12:45, https://www.youtube.com/watch?v=7DN3Ectm6UY&t=765s)
- He says the enter key shapes what people write: in chat apps enter sends and a new line needs shift plus enter, so messages stay short, while in email enter makes a new line, so emails grow long and formal. (The Insane Design Of Messaging at 3:00, https://www.youtube.com/watch?v=6DSW1rN-ZV4&t=180s)

## What makes hardware feel solid and premium?

- He says the feeling that a tech product is solid and good to hold is not random but the result of design and engineering, and that four building blocks make physical tech products work, the first being the curve of the corners. (The Lies That Make Hardware Actually Work at 1:30, https://www.youtube.com/watch?v=wLIgrGAIRsE&t=90s)
- He says the AirPods case hides four magnets, two attracting at the front and two repelling at the back, balanced against the lid angle so the lid always snaps fully open or shut, and that the force chart is on page 33 of Apple's patent. (The Lies That Make Hardware Actually Work at 0:00, https://www.youtube.com/watch?v=wLIgrGAIRsE&t=0s; The Lies That Make Hardware Actually Work at 0:45, https://www.youtube.com/watch?v=wLIgrGAIRsE&t=45s)
- He says not all rounded corners are equal: a chamfer is G0, a plain circular fillet is G1, a MacBook Pro corner feels premium because it is G2 with curvature that rises and falls smoothly, and G3 is found in car design. (The Lies That Make Hardware Actually Work at 2:15, https://www.youtube.com/watch?v=wLIgrGAIRsE&t=135s)
- He says designing a premium corner is easy because it is just math in any CAD tool, and the hard part is paying for it, since cheap brands will not raise production cost for a fraction of a millimeter. (The Lies That Make Hardware Actually Work at 3:00, https://www.youtube.com/watch?v=wLIgrGAIRsE&t=180s)
- He says the squircle, the shape between a circle and a square used for iOS icons and the iPhone's own corners, is the software version of the same idea, and it lets everything sit concentric and aligned. (The Lies That Make Hardware Actually Work at 3:45, https://www.youtube.com/watch?v=wLIgrGAIRsE&t=225s; The Secrets That Make Your Phone Actually Work at 4:30, https://www.youtube.com/watch?v=-YVtZV4DjCE&t=270s)
- He argues against the common view that the click sound sells a button: what sells it is the sudden drop in resistance when a metal dome buckles under your finger. (The Lies That Make Hardware Actually Work at 5:15, https://www.youtube.com/watch?v=wLIgrGAIRsE&t=315s)
- He says almost every button, from phone power buttons to TV remotes and car keys, uses a tiny steel dome that collapses at a set point, and that a bare contact works but feels like nothing. (The Lies That Make Hardware Actually Work at 4:30, https://www.youtube.com/watch?v=wLIgrGAIRsE&t=270s)
- He names the number behind button feel as the **tactile ratio**, the force drop at the click divided by the peak force, where zero is mushy and 100 percent means the dome never pushes back up. (The Lies That Make Hardware Actually Work at 5:15, https://www.youtube.com/watch?v=wLIgrGAIRsE&t=315s)
- He says better feel costs durability: a dome with 180 grams of click force is rated for 5 million presses, while a much better feeling 300 gram one is rated for only 10,000. (The Lies That Make Hardware Actually Work at 6:00, https://www.youtube.com/watch?v=wLIgrGAIRsE&t=360s)
- He uses Apple's butterfly keyboard as the warning: in 2015 Apple cut key travel roughly in half, typing felt less tactile and dust could kill a key, and he says Apple paid 50 million dollars in a settlement and rolled it back in 2019, since a hardware mistake cannot be patched with a software update. (The Lies That Make Hardware Actually Work at 6:45, https://www.youtube.com/watch?v=wLIgrGAIRsE&t=405s)
- He says large glossy plastic makes a product look cheap, as the PlayStation 5 did to him before he touched it, because injection-molded plastic dries unevenly and a big shiny surface shows every imperfection where matte hides it. (The Lies That Make Hardware Actually Work at 9:00, https://www.youtube.com/watch?v=wLIgrGAIRsE&t=540s; How Your Tech Tricks You at 12:00, https://www.youtube.com/watch?v=7nnyv4yJGJc&t=720s)
- He says a better material can be the wrong one: the Game Boy Color and the original iMac used transparent plastic to make tech feel open, and rendered in aluminum they look like they do not belong. (The Lies That Make Hardware Actually Work at 9:45, https://www.youtube.com/watch?v=wLIgrGAIRsE&t=585s)
- On aluminum his line is "So what makes the metal feel premium is, well, cold." He says the cause is effusivity, how much heat a material draws from your hand, and that aluminum takes around 30 times more heat than plastic. (The Lies That Make Hardware Actually Work at 10:30, https://www.youtube.com/watch?v=wLIgrGAIRsE&t=630s)
- He says the most magical experiences fuse hardware and software and companies do not do it enough, citing Pixel volume controls that animate out of the physical side buttons and an unlock animation that grows from the thumb on the fingerprint scanner. (The Lies That Make Hardware Actually Work at 10:30, https://www.youtube.com/watch?v=wLIgrGAIRsE&t=630s)
- He says Teenage Engineering builds hardware and software as one experience: the OP-1 interface is built around four colored knobs whose colors appear on screen, and the TP-7's central disc spins during playback so you can scratch it like vinyl. (The Lies That Make Hardware Actually Work at 11:15, https://www.youtube.com/watch?v=wLIgrGAIRsE&t=675s)
- He says a hardware limitation can become a software feature, with the Dynamic Island turning a camera cutout into a functional element. (The Lies That Make Hardware Actually Work at 12:00, https://www.youtube.com/watch?v=wLIgrGAIRsE&t=720s)
- In September 2024 he said tech went too far in making everything digital, because pressing buttons and turning knobs is human nature, though he admitted haptic displays and configurable knobs have not caught on in the mainstream. (What Happened To Physical Tech at 6:45, https://www.youtube.com/watch?v=TIFF-rinlbo&t=405s; What Happened To Physical Tech at 7:30, https://www.youtube.com/watch?v=TIFF-rinlbo&t=450s)
- He says a blend of physical controls and digital flexibility ages far better than a touchscreen, and that the biggest sign a 10-year-old car is old is its touchscreen media controls. (What Happened To Physical Tech at 9:00, https://www.youtube.com/watch?v=TIFF-rinlbo&t=540s)
