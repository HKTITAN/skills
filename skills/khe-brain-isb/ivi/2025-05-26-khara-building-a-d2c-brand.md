# Building a D2C home care brand, Simran Khara

Simran Khara, founder of the home cleaning brand Koparo and earlier chief executive of Juggernaut Books, spoke to the cohort about building a consumer brand. She covered solving for the first hundred customers, finding and over-delivering on key buying factors, retention, pricing and channels, then took a long round of questions. The recording has many gaps, so this note keeps only what is audible.

## What was said

### Her route to starting up

- Khara says running Juggernaut Books, a digital media startup, taught her she could work with smaller budgets, fewer resources and more constraints, which made starting her own company thinkable.
- She says starting up after many years of work and as a mother was a decision she weighed seriously, because it makes life tougher for some years even if more fulfilling.
- She says entrepreneurship can be lonely, with many dark days and few good ones, so the work had better be something that gives you energy.

### Solve for the first hundred customers

- Khara says on day one you can only solve for your first hundred customers, and she means a hundred, not five hundred or a thousand.
- She says those hundred teach you enough to get to a thousand, and the thousand to ten thousand.
- She says trying to solve for ten thousand or a million people on day one, through decks and canvases in a classroom, does the founder a disservice.
- She says a founder should know exactly which hundred lives the idea will improve.

### Key buying factors and the first use

- She says to list the key buying factors for your product as exhaustively as you can, beyond the first one that comes to mind.
- She says you have to over-deliver on each of them, giving 11 where the customer expected 10, to create a moment of wow the first time the product is in the customer's hands.
- Her dishwashing gel example: buyers care about cleaning, and equally about removing odour, working in hard water, and foaming.
- She says Koparo set its surfactant loading higher than the nearest alternative, because customers needed to see a certain foam height to feel the product was strong.
- She describes the formulation rule as doing about 10% better on each key buying factor, which meant more surfactant, more foam and more fragrance, including a strong first whiff when the bottle is opened.
- She says the market is unforgiving of undifferentiated products and services, and the differentiation itself should delight.
- She says to create several points of differentiation, because you cannot know in advance which one will matter in the long term.
- She says pack size has to match how customers buy. Koparo standardised on 500 ml bottles across cleaners at first, but people buy dishwashing liquid in 750 ml, and she had to change it.
- She says selling a very large pack as a new brand looked counter-intuitive and was a bet that paid off.

### Talking to customers and reading reviews

- Khara says in the early days the founder must read every review personally.
- She says to phone customers and say you are the founder asking for feedback. It costs nothing and people want to talk, even if they ask you to call back.
- She says some product problems only surface in conversation, such as a customer whose dishes were clean but did not look clean.
- From reviews Koparo learned that customers liked a thicker gel, because they read thickness as more concentrated and more powerful.
- She says Koparo launched a range of cleaners after holding off for a long time, because customers kept asking for it unprompted.

### Retention

- She says the founder cannot outsource retention and has to work on that metric personally. Koparo still has no separate person for retention.
- She says acquisition matters for the first six months, but the business does not work unless repeat purchases start to kick in.
- She says Meta and Google performance marketing get harder every quarter, and since a brand reaches its customers through those channels it does not really own them.
- She says it is a very good sign when 50 to 60% of revenue comes from repeat customers and the business is still growing.
- She says customer delight is not a fuzzy idea and shows up in hard numbers such as retention.

### Pricing

- Khara says all first-time entrepreneurs get pricing wrong, because they reason backwards from believing the product is superior to what it should cost.
- She says only the customer can tell you the price, so run rapid experiments: halve it, double it. In India you can sell at any selling price below the MRP.
- She says to find the price where marketing return is most efficient, then hold it and stop changing it.
- Her illustration: a product at 200 rupees with a 75% margin, cut to about 150, takes the margin to 65%. The question is whether the extra units cover the discount.
- She says premiums in India are tight. Buyers accept 5, 10, 15 or 25% more, not double or triple.
- She says in home care the workable premium is close to 10%, and in personal care it can be about 20%.
- She says buyers want something better for the home, but will pay only so much more, partly because in many homes domestic help uses the product.

### Channels

- Asked how she competes with Unilever on distribution, Khara says she accepts that a challenger brand will never be as widely distributed, and that digital is a fairly even playing field where she can list every product.
- She says she used to watch marketplace rankings constantly and now watches overall revenue, because a brand can hold a high rank while burning money.
- She says marketplaces give a new brand better results in its early period, and that organic sales can catch up with paid sales within about a year.
- She says staying out of general trade is a decision about economics and sequencing. It needs a sales force and distributors, and she would make costly mistakes learning it.
- She says offline is still a huge channel and that brands in India eventually get made offline.
- She says she does not try to convert the 90% who buy offline. She serves the roughly 8 to 10% who buy online, and the rest are moving online on their own.
- She says a channel partner with limited space can credibly demand better terms, and each time you choose whether to give in or cut somewhere else, depending on how important the channel is to you.
- As alternatives to Meta for acquisition she points to brands built on consultations, apps or coaches.
- She says a social following takes discipline and consistency over the first two or three years, and not to go overboard on it.

### Planning and operations

- Khara says the discipline Koparo has done well is granular monthly planning, with repeat purchases planned explicitly.
- She says a plan should be precise enough to show which assumption failed: margin, repeat rate, a product line, a channel and product mix, or logistics cost.
- She says her first production run was probably the wrong quantity. The manufacturer's smallest batch was two tons, so she made two tons of everything.
- She says Koparo launched with four products and there was no scientific way of choosing them.
- She says Koparo ships by surface transport to keep the logistics cost of heavy liquid packs down.
- She says Koparo's packaging broke the category's colour codes. Friends in consumer goods warned it would look odd on a supermarket shelf, and in hindsight it worked because customers read it as showing nothing is hidden.
- She advises against leaving performance marketing to agencies. It is core, the team has to build the skill anyway, and agencies report wrong data, in her view without meaning to.
- She says a creative agency worked well for Koparo once, producing ads that made the brand look more serious and aspirational.

### Choosing a market, raising money, going solo

- Khara says there are two ways to pick a market: one with nobody in it, or a crowded one. They need different kinds of courage and neither is the right answer.
- For her, a 70,000 crore rupee industry is a positive, because others have built large brands in it.
- She says a good growth target is to beat the trajectory of a brand that launched earlier, since you have their learning to draw on.
- She says the first cheque comes from excitement about the founder and the thesis. From the second cheque you have to hit metrics, and even losses have to stay within respectable bounds. Her example is a gross margin north of 70%.
- She says she took outside money only when she was really sure, because once you take it you are on a treadmill for years and cannot hand it back.
- On being a solo founder, she says the question does get asked in the early years and there is no elegant answer. She does not think going solo hurts decision making, and says it makes decisions faster.
- She tells the cohort to question received wisdom, such as the belief that subscription can never work in India, and to use the programme as a sandbox where anything in the business can still change.

## Examples and cases used

- Juggernaut Books: her earlier role as chief executive. In its first week the company was hit by a Delhi High Court injunction.
- Unilever: the incumbent a challenger brand cannot match on distribution, but can sit beside on online marketplaces.
- Cureskin: a skin care company that acquires customers through an app and a doctor's prescription of its products, as an alternative to plain performance ads.
- SuperBottoms: a baby care brand that she says runs a separate brand sold only on Flipkart, as one answer to pricing differently by channel.

## Numbers given

- 50 to 60% of revenue from repeat customers, with growth continuing, as her marker of a healthy brand.
- A premium of about 10% in home care and about 20% in personal care, as her rules of thumb for India.
- 70,000 crore rupees as her figure for the size of her industry.
- Two tons as the smallest batch her first manufacturer would make.
