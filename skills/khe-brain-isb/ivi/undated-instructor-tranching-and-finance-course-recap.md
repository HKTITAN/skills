# Why investment comes in tranches, and a finance course recap

The second half of a finance class, after the break that ends the bridge financing case discussion. The same instructor worked through the dilution numbers of the clean technology case to show why staged funding is better for founders than taking all the money at once. The instructor then closed the finance course with a recap of what it had covered. Most of the arithmetic was done on the board and only partly reached the transcript.

## What was said

### The dilution arithmetic of the case

- The instructor says the founders in the case hold 38.5% before the bridge round.
- The instructor says the bridge is a flat round, at the same 19.5 million dollar valuation as the Series A, so 5 million of new money gives a post-money valuation of 24.5 million.
- The instructor says existing holders are scaled down by pre-money divided by post-money, here 19.5 over 24.5, which takes the founders to 30.6%.
- The instructor says the share a new investor asks for is the investment amount divided by the post-money valuation.
- The instructor says the projected Series B is 25 million at a 50 million pre-money valuation, giving 75 million post-money.
- The instructor says the round is called a bridge because Series B was promised on milestones that are not yet met, and the company is running out of time.
- The instructor says a bridge is smaller than a regular round and is meant to carry the company to its next stage.

### Why not take all the money now

- The instructor asks why the company should take 5 million now and 25 million a year later and not 30 million now.
- The instructor says one reason is incentives: staged money is an incentive to stay on the path.
- The instructor says investors who put in all 30 million today take greater uncertainty, not just until Series B but beyond it.
- In the instructor's calculation the valuation roughly doubles between the bridge and Series B, so 75 million at Series B is worth about 36.75 million today.
- The instructor says that with 30 million invested at that value only 6.75 million belongs to the existing holders.
- The instructor says that by taking the extra 25 million now the founders give up about 20 to 23% more.
- The instructor says founders always think they want all the money upfront, but they pay a risk premium on the entire amount and end up diluting more.
- The instructor says tranching is good for both sides because money is committed only when progress is made and uncertainty has fallen.
- The instructor says after milestones are met investors apply a lower discount rate because the risk of failure has gone down.
- The instructor says staging gives investors the option not to put in the next tranche, and a founder who takes everything now denies them that option.
- The instructor says two closely spaced tranches of one round, such as A1 and A2, are priced at the same valuation, and that valuation already assumes the second part comes only after further milestones.
- The instructor says in the case the investors released the second tranche even though not every milestone was met, because enough had been.
- The instructor calls these real options and says businesses face them too: you can start something with a little money and time before committing more.
- The instructor says that when a business is more capex heavy and needs a longer time, strategic investors might be the fit.

### What the finance course covered

- The instructor groups the course into three parts: taking the pulse of the business, planning its future, and raising funds.
- The instructor says taking the pulse covered ratio analysis and unit economics, and that tracking ratios over time gives early signs of what is going right or wrong.
- The instructor says unit economics tell you whether the business will be profitable and how much to spend on acquiring a customer.
- The instructor says the balance sheet shows a point in time and must always balance, while the income statement covers a period.
- The instructor says working capital days is the one ratio founders will find immediately useful, especially in projections.
- The instructor says at this stage of a business unit economics is absolutely crucial.
- The instructor says projections always start with revenue. It is the most important line and possibly the one you have least control over, and it needs a lot of market intelligence.
- The instructor says understanding the market is the first stage of a revenue projection.
- The instructor says in a projected balance sheet cash is the balancing figure, and negative cash shows how much funding the business needs to raise.
- The instructor says one application of the growing perpetuity formula is customer lifetime value.
- The instructor says cash is king, and the course showed how to get from profit to cash flow.
- The instructor says investors ask about the negative free cash flow each quarter or year, and about how the equity is currently divided.
- The instructor says the fundraising part began with when bootstrapping works and when outside money is needed, and ended with valuation approaches.
