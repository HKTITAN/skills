# Value propositions and the Calyx go-to-market case

A marketing class run as a workshop. The instructor, who is not named in the recording, had founders write a value proposition statement and commented on them, then ran a group discussion of a case on Calyx, a company that ships flowers direct to the recipient, and closed with a first look at pricing. This note keeps only the general teaching. The comments on individual founders' statements are left out.

## What was said

### Writing a value proposition

- The instructor says defining the customer and the value is one of the hardest steps, and go-to-market is also hard because many elements go into it.
- He says this step takes experimentation, primary research, validation and talking to people to find out whether you are reaching the right customers.
- He says once you are convinced on this step, the rest is more a matter of implementation.
- He separates features from value. When a founder lists several aspects of a product, he asks for the value in simple words.
- He says an attention-grabbing pitch line works only for an instant. "The value prop is what makes conversations last."
- He says a statement that names several values at once has too much going on. Most positioning statements are clear on one value.
- He says if you name the emotions that trouble your customer, the product has to solve for exactly those emotions.
- Asked whether a value proposition needs a quantified metric, he says it does not have to be quantifiable, and certainly not at this stage.
- He says value goes beyond functional, product and feature benefits. For a business customer, ease of doing business is itself a value proposition.
- He names identification value as another element: people want to belong to a brand for what it brings.
- He says you define the dimensions of the problem you solve because different segments want different things, for example reliability.
- He says the ideal customer profile is the set of people most likely to benefit, and it should be very specific.

### Differentiating a commodity

- The instructor says in markets seen as commodities, with little real difference between products, the only way to differentiate is emotional, psychological and through branding.
- His example is cola: Coca-Cola says it is happiness, and there is no happiness in the drink itself. He asks why anyone would choose a Coke over a Pepsi.
- He points to an energy drink sold on the line that it gives you wings and on its association with sports.

### Selling technology that replaces people

- The instructor says a pitch that a technology replaces workforce must factor in the cost of change. Firms think about these costs in terms of what it takes to transform a human organisation.
- He says switching is not free. It carries a substantial cost, because decision making changes along with the technology.
- He asks who gets fired if decisions are delegated to an AI, and says executives are paid for the decisions they make.
- His contact centre example: replacing three of five inbound agents is a pure cost proposition. A claim of better quality depends on how quality is benchmarked, and people on the ground will not accept it readily.

### The Calyx case

- The instructor says the case question is about managing profitable growth: how to improve financial performance, given three strategies that the company's marketing team has recommended.
- He asks the class to take the role of the protagonist and to judge the case in its own time, when online targeting did not work as it does now.
- He explains the model: flowers ship directly from the grower, are cut before they have matured to allow for transport, and travel in special packaging, so they last a long time once the recipient receives them.
- He says most flights out of Holland leave after two o'clock because the flower markets end by twelve, and that this is part of how the trade works with a perishable product.
- He says catalogues are the old version of B2C, and that people who are smart in B2C go back to what was learned in the catalogue business and carry those ideas over.
- He lists the consumer disadvantages the class found: waiting for delivery, flowers that do not look exactly like the picture because each flower is different, arrival in a shipping box and not in full bloom so there is no wow moment, and no consultation or curation.
- He names three customer segments for flowers: gifts, which he says are driven by guilt, buying for oneself, and decoration for homes, offices and weddings.
- He draws a value map of Calyx against local florists on attributes. Calyx is high on longevity and high on variety, which he calls the power of a catalogue. Local florists are middling on longevity and variety and very strong on presentation.
- He says local florists still have a business and the two coexist.
- He says presentation matters most for a gift.
- He says people who decorate usually have their own plan for how the space should look, so they need flowers that last and a wide choice, and they supply the presentation themselves.
- He concludes that the decoration segment fits what Calyx offers.
- He says none of the three strategies in the case will work. Internet advertising will not work and the existing approaches will not work.
- His answer is that the company needs to identify the integrators and start providing consulting services, and that this is where growth has to start.
- He says Calyx ended up with a bad strategy, lost its business and was split up.

### Takeaways he draws

- The instructor says you need to understand your customer segments and which attributes each segment cares about.
- He says product, market and value have to be coherent, and that coherence decides the how of going to market.
- He says most people jump to the how too fast, for example by deciding on digital advertising or putting a salesperson on the road.
- He says the right way to reach a customer depends on what you are selling, and if you have not worked that out you will lose money.
- He says to work out which attributes your product can offer and then decide your promotional style.
- He says the value proposition can be very different for different customer personas.
- His working order is: do the exercise, plot your value map and attributes, find the points you satisfy, and only then go to the how.

### First step into pricing

- The instructor says a sale usually happens when the benefits are more than the price.
- He says that between two products a customer buys the one whose value net of price is higher.
- He says people interpret a raw price number very differently depending on the context, which is why he starts pricing with biases.

## Examples and cases used

- Calyx, the direct flower delivery company: the fit between product attributes and a customer segment decides the go-to-market, and the strategies on offer in the case all miss it.
- Coca-Cola and Pepsi: commodity products differentiate through emotion and brand.
- An energy drink sold as giving you wings: branding by association with sports.
- Dutch flower markets and afternoon flights out of Holland: how the trade handles a perishable product.
- Amazon: his comparison for the near endless variety a catalogue can offer.
