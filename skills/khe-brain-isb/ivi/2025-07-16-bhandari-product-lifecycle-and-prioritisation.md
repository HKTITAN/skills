# Product thinking, task breakdown and prioritisation frameworks, Ashank Bhandari

Ashank Bhandari, who leads platform products at foundit, formerly Monster, taught an afternoon session on product management for founders. He covered what a product manager does, the insight to solution to execution loop, the product life cycle, how to break work into themes, epics, stories and tasks, and four prioritisation frameworks. The session used an Amazon wishlist exercise done in groups, two short videos, and a lot of questions from the room.

## What was said

### What product management is

- Ashank Bhandari compares a founder or product manager to a theatre director, who watches from the audience's seat and treats each element on screen as an actor with an objective and a story.
- He says brand marketing runs on its own objectives, while product marketing works hand in hand with the people building the product, and a good organisation runs product marketing in parallel with product management.
- He asked everyone to name a favourite product, then pointed out that every answer came from personal use. He says that degree of personal connection is what a product should aim for.
- He says product companies have a rhythm and a cycle to how they build, and that this cyclical learning is why product based companies have been so successful.
- He uses the cricket all-rounder as his picture of a founder: a generalist who fills many gaps, and who cannot keep doing everything as the company grows.
- He says the one critical role a product manager plays among specialists is to be the voice of the customer.
- He describes product managers as specialist generalists, and as problem solvers who scale the solution using technology.
- He says the difference between good and great founders is how consistently they stay in touch with their customers, not as a one time activity.
- He says an early stage founder is better off doing product management personally and should not hire product managers yet.
- He notes that several leading technology CEOs, Sundar Pichai among them, came up through product management.

### Insight, solution, execution

- His model is a loop of insight, creative solution and execution, repeated through iteration.
- He says insight answers why you are doing something, the solution answers what, and execution answers how.
- The class notes pair these with desirability, viability and feasibility in that order.
- He says the discipline that feeds the loop is staying connected to customers.

### The product life cycle

- He gives six phases: conceive, plan, build, launch, refine and retire.
- He says conceive is where ideas are born and the hard questions are asked, such as how large the market is and what the mission is.
- He says plan means picking a specific set of problem statements to solve out of everything you found.
- He says launch is not the end. After launch you keep checking whether the product is hitting its target, through data and through conversations with customers.
- He says product management grew up in technology first because software gives access to data at scale, and it is now spreading into other industries.
- On retirement, he says parts of a dead product can be salvaged. His example is BlackBerry's messenger being repurposed as a security offering after the phones died.
- He names time spent and scroll depth as two core measures of engagement.

### Getting insight from customers

- Asked about customers who state exactly which feature they want, he says to go deeper into why they want it and where they would use it.
- He ran a role play as an Instagram user demanding more reaction options, to show that the useful answer comes from understanding the user's journey and not from debating the feature.
- He says it is not the customer's job to articulate the problem. Their job is to have a need.
- He says changing user behaviour takes enormous effort and often fails even then, and he cites Kellogg's entry into India with the aim of converting the country to breakfast cereal.
- He says a founder has to learn to ask the right questions of data. At an e-commerce company his team asked for searches with high volume where customers took no action.
- His example from that work: a significant minority of Indian users spell a word like Bluetooth the way they say it, the catalogue has the correct spelling, and so the search fails. He says the platform has to adapt and cannot teach customers to spell.
- He says if you are building for everyone, you are building for no one.

### Layers of strategy

- He orders the layers as company mission and vision, business strategy, product vision, product strategy, then release plan and roadmap, sprint plan and daily plan.
- He says product vision and product strategy are most often blended into one, and that not being able to tell these layers apart at this stage is a fundamental problem.
- He ties this back to his loop: the product vision answers why and the product strategy answers what.

### Breaking work down

- He borrows the breakdown from Agile and Scrum: a theme at the top, then epics, then stories, then tasks.
- His example theme is an e-commerce wishlist. A story is written in the customer's voice, such as wanting to save a product to a wishlist to view it again later.
- He breaks that story into tasks such as putting a heart icon on each product page and creating the database entries for each customer.
- He says the ability to break work down is the fundamental step in prioritisation, and the frameworks come after it.
- He says work has to be cut small enough that building, testing and feedback fit in one cycle, with design ahead of development by a cycle or two.
- He says normally you work top down, and he made the class work bottom up from a list of about 40 tasks to build the skill of sorting a laundry list into logical buckets.
- He asked the class not to use a language model for the exercise because it would remove the learning.
- Asked by a four person technical team whether they need epics and stories, he says it is their choice and that being intuitive is fine as long as everyone's time is well used.

### MVP and its alternatives

- He says the first version of Flipkart was an order form: the founders bought the book from a physical shop and delivered it themselves.
- He introduces the most lovable product as the opposite approach, where a team that is confident in its insight builds a delightful experience from day one.
- He names DigiYatra as an MVP that has reached scale while solving one narrow problem.

### Four prioritisation frameworks

- MoSCoW sorts features into must have, should have, could have and will not have. He says it can be applied at the epic level, and everything under an epic inherits the bucket.
- For the wishlist he says the must haves are adding an item and viewing the list later. Removing an item, sharing and gifting can wait for later versions.
- He says what counts as a must have also depends on what competitors already offer and on how much funding you have.
- RICE scores reach times impact times confidence, divided by effort. He says the numbers come from your understanding of the product and the customer.
- He explains reach by comparing a wishlist, which only some visitors meet, with a change to the home page navigation, which everyone meets.
- He says effort comes from asking engineers and designers. In his wishlist example the engineer calls it an architectural change needing several new tables and about three weeks.
- Value against effort is a two by two grid. High value and low effort items are quick wins, which he says are rare and worth holding on to. High value and high effort items are big projects.
- He says value against effort has been enough in his experience for executive level decisions, and is the method he uses most.
- Weighted scoring assigns weights to factors, and he describes it as another mathematical approach alongside RICE.
- His guide for choosing: when project complexity is high and errors are costly, use the more precise mathematical methods. Early stage teams usually need only MoSCoW and value against effort.
- He says a framework does not guarantee success. It reduces the chance of failure, and it is a guidebook and not a rulebook.
- He says prioritisation is a mix of art and science.
- He says going live does not have to mean going live to every customer at once. A feature can be released incrementally.

### Leverage in your own tasks

- He played a short video on the LNO framework, which sorts a person's tasks into leverage tasks that return 10 to 100 times the effort, neutral tasks that return about what you put in, and overhead tasks that return less.
- The video's advice is to do leverage tasks at your most energetic time of day and with full perfectionism, and to spend less time on the other two kinds.

### What the Ather video showed

- He played part of an Ather Energy launch presentation to show product thinking in hardware.
- In the video, Ather says 85 percent of buyers chose the higher performance variants, and only 18 percent used the performance modes as their main mode.
- Ather's stated finding was that riders stopped using performance modes once a day's riding could not be covered in them. Its response was a battery with 25 percent more capacity.
- The video also introduces tyres said to give 22 percent better grip.
- Bhandari's point is that data at scale let a company with limited time and resources pick these few changes out of many possible ones.

## Examples and cases used

- Flipkart's first version: founders fulfilling orders by hand from a bookshop, as an MVP.
- Amazon wishlist: the running case for themes, epics, stories, tasks and every prioritisation framework.
- Amazon Pay: his example of a big project in the value against effort grid.
- Ather 450X launch: data showing a gap between what buyers bought and how they rode.
- BlackBerry messenger: salvaging part of a retired product.
- Kellogg in India: the difficulty of changing behaviour.
- DigiYatra: an MVP at scale.
- Google and Infosys: he says Infosys has roughly a lakh more employees than Google, to contrast product and services companies.

## Numbers given

- 85 percent and 18 percent: Ather's figures, from the video, for buyers of performance variants and riders who mainly use performance modes.
- 25 percent more battery capacity and 22 percent better tyre grip: Ather's figures from the same video.
- 10 to 100 times, about 1.1 times, and less than 1 times: the returns on leverage, neutral and overhead tasks in the LNO video.
- About three weeks: the engineering effort in his wishlist example.
