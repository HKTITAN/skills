# Term sheets and negotiating with investors, Amit Khanna

Amit Khanna, an angel investor who describes his firm as a family office, walked two cohorts through a sample term sheet he had circulated, stopping at the clauses he had highlighted. The second half was open questions on valuation, dilution, founder loans, co-investors and friends and family money. He refers to an earlier session with the same group. He says more than once that he is not a lawyer.

## What was said

### Why the term sheet matters

- Amit Khanna says the term sheet is the most important document in raising capital after the shareholders' agreement between co-founders.
- He says the definitive agreements emerge from the term sheet, and it is very hard, though not impossible, to negotiate anything contrary to it at the definitive stage.
- He tells founders to hire a good lawyer for the term sheet and not to save money on it or copy and paste another company's term sheet.
- He says a term sheet is not a guaranteed finish line, and he has seen deals fall away after one was signed.
- He says contentious items should be settled in the term sheet and not left open. His examples are a new stock option pool, the founder's salary and visibility on the founder's own exit.
- He says a well negotiated term sheet makes the definitive agreements much easier, because it is the reference for every later argument.

### Instruments

- He says a term sheet today rarely offers plain equity. Investors use a convertible structure or preferred equity because they want some flexibility and security.
- He says a convertible that converts at a discount to the next round is normally used when founder and investor cannot agree on a valuation today.
- He says the downside of that structure for the founder is that there is no valuation to show anyone. The benefit is less dilution if the next round is at a much higher valuation.
- He says any institutional-grade investor will ask for an instrument that is better than plain equity.
- Asked about optionally versus compulsorily convertible preference shares, he says the optional kind is better for the investor, who can stay a lender if the company does badly and convert if it does well.
- He says he prefers outcomes where everyone carries equity risk, because alignment matters and investors should be on the same side when things go badly.

### Use of funds and choosing investors by cheque size

- He says the use of funds clause gets written into the agreements, so founders should think hard about it.
- He says the amount you need decides which investors you approach. A large fund such as Peak XV will not write a 100,000 dollar cheque.

### Board seats and observers

- He says investors increasingly want the right to appoint a director without using it, because directors carry liability.
- He says in large private equity deals investors take the board seat and ask the company to buy directors' insurance.
- He says directors in India do get sued, for example over unpaid statutory filings, unpaid taxes or harm from a product, and the nuisance lasts for years whatever the final liability.
- He advises offering an observer seat when an investor asks for too many board seats. An observer sits in the meeting and carries no liability.
- A participant adds that the observer is an investor-side concept and is at most recorded in the minutes as present.
- He says serious board-seat discussions typically start at about a 10% holding, and above 20 to 25% the discussion becomes proportional.
- He says if a well-known person's presence on the board would benefit the company, give the seat whatever the size of the cheque.
- He says a company should have two or three directors, at most four, before investors come in.
- He says a startup board should not be more than five people, six at most, to keep the company nimble.
- He says investors get a veto in two ways: as shareholders through reserved matters that need their approval, such as starting a new line of business, and on the board through a quorum rule that requires the investor director to be present.

### Pre-emptive rights and follow-on money

- He says smart investors always ask for a pro rata right so they can put in more when the company is doing well and avoid dilution in the next round.
- He says an earlier investor coming back in is a strong reassurance, and from the second or third round new investors always ask whether the old investors are investing again.
- He says if the old investors are not investing again, that can become a big red flag.
- He advises asking an investor whether the fund has a mandate to invest in the next round too.
- He says between two otherwise equal investors he would pick the one who does follow-on rounds, even at a slightly lower valuation.

### Founder lock-in

- He says investors are investing in the founder, so they lock the founder in from selling equity and walking away. The period typically starts at three years.
- He says founders should not even try to negotiate the lock-in, because doing so signals that they want to exit and makes the investment questionable.

### Jurisdiction and dispute resolution

- He says founders should avoid Singapore as the jurisdiction, because a dispute would mean travelling there and paying Singapore lawyers.
- He describes the usual sequence. The founder asks for India. The investor asks for Singapore arbitration rules. If the fund's policy is fixed, the founder accepts Singapore rules but keeps the seat of arbitration in the founder's own Indian city.
- He says dispute resolution clauses set out trying to settle among the parties first and then arbitration, including how many arbitrators there are and who appoints them.

### Exclusivity and diligence costs

- He says investors ask for exclusivity because they are about to spend money and time on diligence and do not want the founder shopping the term sheet for a better offer.
- He says investors typically ask for six months of exclusivity, and two to four months is sufficient.
- He says each party normally bears its own expenses, and founders should push back if asked to pay for the investor's diligence.
- He says the worst outcome he would accept is the diligence cost being charged to the company, never to the promoters.
- He says he would agree to pay the diligence fee only if diligence finds the founder misrepresented something, for example turnover being half of what was stated.
- He says funds have a diligence budget for deals they look at, not only for deals they close.
- He says an investor who pays nothing has no skin in the game and walks away having learned the company and its industry.
- He says confidentiality starts at the term sheet and survives its termination, but there is no real protection against an investor using what they learned elsewhere.

### Lead investors and co-investors

- He says a founder with a lead term sheet can bring in smaller co-investors but should keep the lead informed, since the lead may not want certain names on the cap table.
- He says exclusivity is aimed at stopping the founder from taking the term sheet to someone who could write a bigger lead cheque.
- He says co-investors typically rely on the lead's diligence report and negotiated documents and do not get all the lead's rights.
- He says a founder can set a threshold, for example a board seat only at 10% and above, and has more bargaining strength with co-investors than with the lead.

### Dilution and founder stake

- He says each time new capital comes in, every existing holder is diluted in proportion, and founders accept it because a smaller share of a more valuable company is worth more.
- He says anti-dilution clauses are typically asked for by investors, not employees.
- He says investors worry when promoters fall to single-digit holdings, and he has seen new investors offer founders 5 to 10% in fresh equity over about five years against targets so that they have skin in the game.

### Deferring valuation with a time-linked discount

- He says the smart way to handle an early valuation argument is not to have it. Take the money now and give the investor a discount on the next round's valuation.
- He says this buys time to create value with the money before the dilution is fixed, and as an angel investor he would love that structure.
- He says his formula for the discount is 10 to 20% for each year the company has not raised its next round, and the total can be capped, for example at 40, 30 or 25%.
- He says when an investor fights for upfront equity instead of a discount, it shows they believe strongly in the idea and do not want to lose the deal.

### Founder loans

- He says smart investors will ask founders to convert their loans to the company into equity instead of repaying them from the raise.
- He says investors want the runway to be long and do not want their money used to repay the founder.
- He says taking a founder loan out gets harder with each round and matters less only when it is small against the round. His example is 50 lakh against a 50 crore round compared with 50 lakh against a 2 crore round.
- He says a bank loan that has to be repaid must be shown in the use of funds, and the investor will factor it into their return calculation.

### Leverage and conduct

- He says good ideas are few and capital is a commodity, so a founder with a good idea is needed by investors more than the founder needs them.
- He says founders have to create competition by finding the right lead investor, after which others follow.
- He says an investor sitting across the table to negotiate already likes the company, so founders should negotiate harder and not be defensive.
- He says, "Raise capital before you need it. Not when you need it."
- He says founders should do diligence on investors, including angels: search their names, and call founders they have backed to ask how they behave on the board, how much information they demand and whether they caused trouble at exit.
- He says founders should ask every investor what they bring besides capital.
- He urges founders to email investors every quarter with the next quarter's targets and specific asks, such as access to experts, help sourcing talent or an introduction to another portfolio company.
- He says this will not be a founder's only venture, and future investors will call earlier investors to ask how the experience was.
- On buying a smaller company to gain distribution, he advises a share swap into the parent instead of cash, which saves cash for growth and forces a valuation that helps later investor discussions.

### Angels, funds and friends and family

- He says an individual angel putting in a few lakhs will not run diligence, because it would cost more than the amount at risk.
- He says with angels the company leads with a stripped-down term sheet and gives away few rights.
- He says angels invest directly and a venture fund is the next, institutional level. Individuals pool money in a fund because the fund can afford diligence.
- He says friends and family money should be documented for the sake of the relationship, with no board seats or other investor rights and information limited to annual accounts.
- He says founders should tell friends and family plainly that this is high-risk equity and they should put in only what they are willing to lose.
- He says his own firm is a family office and not an institution, so it does not have to invest, and it has set up a fund for Indian public markets, where he sees more growth over the next few years and which gives liquidity.

## Numbers given

- About 10%: the holding at which, in his telling, serious board-seat discussions start. Above 20 to 25% it becomes proportional.
- Five, at most six: his limit for the size of a startup board. Two or three, at most four, before investors come in.
- Three years: where a founder lock-in period typically starts.
- Six months asked, two to four months sufficient: his figures for exclusivity.
- 10 to 20% a year: his formula for the discount on the next round in a deferred-valuation deal, with a cap such as 40, 30 or 25%.
- 5 to 10% over about five years: fresh equity he has seen new investors offer heavily diluted founders against targets.
