# Psychology and persuasion

For him psychology is the layer that decides whether a product works: people are intuitive and inconsistent, so products are built around their biases, and the same principle can help a user or exploit one. Most of this comes from his videos on buying tricks, Spotify Wrapped, Notion, notifications, short-form feeds, loading screens and games, with single claims scattered across many others. Product facts and study results are his research as of each video's date, some videos were made with a company's team, and his advice on notifications and on listening to users changed between 2021 and 2026, so check the year on each line.

## What role does he give psychology in tech products?

- He says every website and app uses psychology tricks on you, and that this is not the problem. The problem is that most people never realise it. (The Internet Wants Your Money Here's How To Outsmart It at 0:00, https://www.youtube.com/watch?v=4OLt9pFlRvo&t=0s)
- He says the hardest thing about building tech is not the clever hack or the complex build but humans and their weird psychology, which he calls the most unpredictable and counterintuitive part of tech. (How Your Tech Tricks You at 7:30, https://www.youtube.com/watch?v=7nnyv4yJGJc&t=450s)
- He says designing for human psychology is the most powerful tool tech companies have and not an afterthought, and that it leads to some genius features and some stupid ones. (The Secrets That Make Your Phone Actually Work at 4:30, https://www.youtube.com/watch?v=-YVtZV4DjCE&t=270s)
- He frames the core problem as a mismatch: people run on intuition and instinct, with brains that evolved to survive in the forest, while computers are cold, calculating and precise, so designers and engineers turn to copying nature to make them usable by normal people. (The Lies That Make Your Tech Actually Work at 3:00, https://www.youtube.com/watch?v=rAVV4kAYlI8&t=180s)
- He holds that a principle is neutral. He says Amazon and a scammy astrology site use the exact same ones, and "A psychology principle can be used both in ethical and unethical ways." (The Internet Wants Your Money Here's How To Outsmart It at 2:15, https://www.youtube.com/watch?v=4OLt9pFlRvo&t=135s; The Internet Wants Your Money Here's How To Outsmart It at 3:00, https://www.youtube.com/watch?v=4OLt9pFlRvo&t=180s)
- He says Amazon uses five different psychology principles in the four seconds it takes to click add to cart, and that once you learn the principles you see them everywhere. (The Internet Wants Your Money Here's How To Outsmart It at 0:00, https://www.youtube.com/watch?v=4OLt9pFlRvo&t=0s)
- He calls pull to refresh **geniusly evil**: it is intuitive and removes the refresh button, but paired with infinite feeds that show something new each time it drives the urge to keep pulling, and he says it is probably responsible for billions of dollars of extra engagement. (The Secrets That Make Your Phone Actually Work at 5:15, https://www.youtube.com/watch?v=-YVtZV4DjCE&t=315s; The Secrets That Make Your Phone Actually Work at 6:00, https://www.youtube.com/watch?v=-YVtZV4DjCE&t=360s)
- He does not exempt himself. He says the landing page for his own product Story Behind uses the same principles he exposes, and he challenges viewers to spot them all. (The Internet Wants Your Money Here's How To Outsmart It at 11:15, https://www.youtube.com/watch?v=4OLt9pFlRvo&t=675s)

## Which named effects does he explain, and where does he show them?

- He uses Hick's law, the idea that the more we are shown the longer the brain takes to process it, to explain **squeeze pages**: searching for Shopify from Google lands him on a page with no menu, no navigation and no other links, so the only thing left to do is buy. (The Internet Wants Your Money Here's How To Outsmart It at 0:45, https://www.youtube.com/watch?v=4OLt9pFlRvo&t=45s)
- He says Netflix took Hick's law to the extreme by making its whole website a squeeze page for one action, joining Netflix, with everything else pushed down below. (The Internet Wants Your Money Here's How To Outsmart It at 1:30, https://www.youtube.com/watch?v=4OLt9pFlRvo&t=90s)
- He explains reviews as social proof, since we are more likely to do what other people do. The unethical version he shows is fake purchase popups, made with apps that let a seller choose how many fake purchases appear per minute and what the buyers are called. (The Internet Wants Your Money Here's How To Outsmart It at 2:15, https://www.youtube.com/watch?v=4OLt9pFlRvo&t=135s)
- He says Booking.com's Genius programme counts the number of bookings and not the money spent because of commitment and consistency: the habit of going to Booking whenever you need a hotel is worth far more than one bigger stay. He says Duolingo streaks use the same principle. (The Internet Wants Your Money Here's How To Outsmart It at 3:00, https://www.youtube.com/watch?v=4OLt9pFlRvo&t=180s)
- He explains discount code popups through reciprocity. If we are given something we feel we owe something back, so a code that feels given to you personally makes you more likely to finish the purchase, the same way street vendors hand out samples. (The Internet Wants Your Money Here's How To Outsmart It at 3:45, https://www.youtube.com/watch?v=4OLt9pFlRvo&t=225s)
- He reads onboarding quizzes placed before sign-up as the sunk cost fallacy. Brilliant asks preference questions before he has given an email, so leaving at the sign-up step means losing the answers. He grants the quiz helps personalise the app but says its real job is sunk cost. (The Internet Wants Your Money Here's How To Outsmart It at 5:15, https://www.youtube.com/watch?v=4OLt9pFlRvo&t=315s)
- He explains the IKEA effect as people giving disproportionately higher value to things they build themselves, and calls it the most important reason people are obsessed with Notion: after hours of building your own template and database you value it far more than a normal to-do app. (Why Everyone Is Obsessed With Notion at 2:15, https://www.youtube.com/watch?v=aWGviOMQqSw&t=135s)
- He says the IKEA effect also has a cost for the company: because everyone builds a different system, Notion's users want completely different features, which makes deciding what to build next very hard. (The Ridiculous Engineering Of Notion at 4:30, https://www.youtube.com/watch?v=b00sgRR_Vc0&t=270s)
- He names **the paradox of Notion**: you spend more time tweaking the system meant to make you productive than being productive. He says that is not a bad thing, because building your perfect system is fulfilling. (Why Everyone Is Obsessed With Notion at 3:00, https://www.youtube.com/watch?v=aWGviOMQqSw&t=180s)
- He uses Maslow's hammer, a bias he says Abraham Maslow made famous in 1966, to explain why people try to solve every part of their lives with a Notion database: once you know a tool you want to use it for everything. (Why Everyone Is Obsessed With Notion at 3:45, https://www.youtube.com/watch?v=aWGviOMQqSw&t=225s)
- He says Notion built a killer **meta game**, the game about the game: tens of thousands of YouTube videos, thousands of template sellers and whole companies around it, all doing free marketing. (Why Everyone Is Obsessed With Notion at 4:30, https://www.youtube.com/watch?v=aWGviOMQqSw&t=270s)
- He explains in-game currencies with the **cashless effect**. Gems, coins and credits copy casino chips by putting a fake currency between you and the price so you lose track of real money. (The Internet Wants Your Money Here's How To Outsmart It at 6:45, https://www.youtube.com/watch?v=4OLt9pFlRvo&t=405s)
- He names the gambler's fallacy, believing a random event is more or less likely because of earlier outcomes, and shows it with his own urge to bet on black after red came up seven times at roulette. (The Spotify Shuffle Button is a Lie at 1:30, https://www.youtube.com/watch?v=NhAIA-o7f4M&t=90s; The Spotify Shuffle Button is a Lie at 2:15, https://www.youtube.com/watch?v=NhAIA-o7f4M&t=135s)
- He says Spotify Wrapped works on three carefully engineered psychological principles, and that it is not about the music or even the fear of missing out. The first ingredient, data, runs on the curiosity gap, and the third, sharing, is powered by the spotlight effect. (The Reason Everyone's Obsessed With Spotify Wrapped at 0:00, https://www.youtube.com/watch?v=EuzRWd5U6_I&t=0s; The Reason Everyone's Obsessed With Spotify Wrapped at 9:45, https://www.youtube.com/watch?v=EuzRWd5U6_I&t=585s)
- He describes the curiosity gap as our desire to seek out missing information. He says Spotify offers no listening statistics during the year on purpose, so you have to wait for Wrapped. (The Reason Everyone's Obsessed With Spotify Wrapped at 2:15, https://www.youtube.com/watch?v=EuzRWd5U6_I&t=135s)
- He says the anticipation effect is why Spotify changes the story every year. The numbers stay more or less the same, so the question becomes what the story will be this year, and without that Wrapped risks going stale the way YouTube Rewind did. (The Reason Everyone's Obsessed With Spotify Wrapped at 5:15, https://www.youtube.com/watch?v=EuzRWd5U6_I&t=315s)
- He names the spotlight effect as the third ingredient that makes Wrapped work: we believe we are more noticed and more unique than we are and we want to flex, so total minutes are shown as the main metric, like a video game high score. (The Reason Everyone's Obsessed With Spotify Wrapped at 8:15, https://www.youtube.com/watch?v=EuzRWd5U6_I&t=495s)
- He uses loss aversion to explain habit notifications: people fear losing what they have more than they want to gain something new, so Duolingo warns you about your streak and does not invite you to come and learn Spanish. (The Ridiculous Engineering Of Notifications at 3:45, https://www.youtube.com/watch?v=Dx2yPk0FsGM&t=225s)
- He cites unit bias, the idea that one unit of something always feels like the right amount, to explain why short-form feeds show one video at a time with no grid or menu. (The Ridiculous Engineering Of Short Form Content at 3:00, https://www.youtube.com/watch?v=69Gw7aoWMMI&t=180s)
- He names chronoception, the sense of time, as what feeds blur by removing every signal of beginning and end: videos loop, short ones have no play bar, and he compares it to casinos removing clocks and windows. (The Ridiculous Engineering Of Short Form Content at 3:00, https://www.youtube.com/watch?v=69Gw7aoWMMI&t=180s)
- He states Fitts's law as the time to reach a target depending on its distance and size, so the more important an action is the larger and closer it should be. He uses it to explain the edge-swipe back gesture and right-click menus that open where the mouse already is. (The Lies That Make Your Tech Actually Work at 8:15, https://www.youtube.com/watch?v=rAVV4kAYlI8&t=495s; How Your Tech Is Built For You To Fail at 7:30, https://www.youtube.com/watch?v=4HWPFd8CQo8&t=450s)
- He uses the Kiki and Bouba effect to explain rounded interfaces: pointy shapes feel intimidating and round ones playful, and a perfectly sharp angle is rare in nature and associated with blades. He says the effect is multiplied ten times when windows float in real space in AR and VR. (The Secrets That Make Your Phone Actually Work at 13:30, https://www.youtube.com/watch?v=-YVtZV4DjCE&t=810s)
- He explains the paradox of choice as too many options with no cue making people give up. He says it is why YouTube keeps the view count and why a grid of good long videos turns choosing into a pain, like scrolling Netflix for 20 minutes. (What If YouTube Removes The Views Count at 3:45, https://www.youtube.com/watch?v=chyB9Ot9QK8&t=225s; Social Media Is Broken But We Can Fix It at 4:30, https://www.youtube.com/watch?v=HqYIUV7Eqjk&t=270s)
- He cites the penny gap chocolate experiment to argue free is perceived as a different category and not just a lower price: with a Lindt at 15 cents and a Hershey's at 1 cent, 73% chose the Lindt, and when both dropped a penny most took the free Hershey's. (Tech's Weirdest Problem at 3:45, https://www.youtube.com/watch?v=dQe1r2MOwt8&t=225s)

## How do sites and apps get you to buy?

- He says companies send you to a squeeze page the moment you show purchase intent, which is why you land on them from ads. (The Internet Wants Your Money Here's How To Outsmart It at 1:30, https://www.youtube.com/watch?v=4OLt9pFlRvo&t=90s)
- He says the principles do not stop once you buy. They intensify, and the IKEA effect and in-game currencies are tactics aimed at people who are already users. (The Internet Wants Your Money Here's How To Outsmart It at 6:00, https://www.youtube.com/watch?v=4OLt9pFlRvo&t=360s)
- He says the complexity of a game's currency system tells you how much it wants to trick you: gems, coins, tokens, exchange rates, loot boxes and bundles all exist to hide the real price. (The Internet Wants Your Money Here's How To Outsmart It at 7:30, https://www.youtube.com/watch?v=4OLt9pFlRvo&t=450s)
- He says that to a subscription service you are literally a number, your lifetime value, worked out from the monthly price and the share of customers lost each month, and that every service treats it as its most important number. (The Internet Wants Your Money Here's How To Outsmart It at 8:15, https://www.youtube.com/watch?v=4OLt9pFlRvo&t=495s)
- He says lifetime value sets how much a company will spend to acquire you: as long as ads and promotions cost less than you are worth, it makes money, so the ads you see are carefully calculated. (The Internet Wants Your Money Here's How To Outsmart It at 9:00, https://www.youtube.com/watch?v=4OLt9pFlRvo&t=540s)
- He says churn is why unsubscribing is made hard. A rise of only 1% in churn drops lifetime value sharply, which is why some services use dark patterns and confusing 20-screen cancel flows, and he says this basic maths is why everything became a subscription. (The Internet Wants Your Money Here's How To Outsmart It at 9:45, https://www.youtube.com/watch?v=4OLt9pFlRvo&t=585s)
- He says products sell at the moment of loss, as games do: don't sell while people are thinking, sell the exact second they lose. A guest in the video gives Temple Run as the example, where you get a few seconds after losing to pay to continue. He says Google Meet does the same: it does not ask you to upgrade on the pricing page, it ends the meeting after a certain amount of time and offers the upgrade a few minutes before. (The Weird Way Tech Steals From Games at 12:45, https://www.youtube.com/watch?v=GypAKofWGBk&t=765s)
- He says the first small purchase changes how people see themselves. A $1 trial works like a game's icebreaker deal, where the size does not matter because now you are a spender. (The Weird Way Tech Steals From Games at 13:30, https://www.youtube.com/watch?v=GypAKofWGBk&t=810s)
- He says artificial scarcity is underused in software. An extra install costs nothing, yet Arc required an invite for its first years, which he calls genius because it controlled growth and built exclusivity and belonging. (Why Everyone Is Obsessed With Arc Browser at 4:30, https://www.youtube.com/watch?v=UdopamaLJC8&t=270s)
- He says humans are fascinated by having something no one else can have, and that luxury brands are masters at using it through raffles and limited pieces. For luxury a high price is a key ingredient, because it signals that the product is better and more desirable. (Why Luxury Apps Don't Exist at 3:00, https://www.youtube.com/watch?v=X1HqzLbGznc&t=180s; Why Luxury Apps Don't Exist at 6:45, https://www.youtube.com/watch?v=X1HqzLbGznc&t=405s)
- He says free and subsidised products trained people to see valuable services as worth zero, so people feel more ripped off by Netflix or Spotify than by an iPhone with a fatter margin. (Tech's Weirdest Problem at 7:30, https://www.youtube.com/watch?v=dQe1r2MOwt8&t=450s; Tech's Weirdest Problem at 8:15, https://www.youtube.com/watch?v=dQe1r2MOwt8&t=495s)
- He says a view count is social proof for the viewer and a tie-breaker when everything else is equal, which creates a loop where the video with more views gets clicked more and recommended more. (What If YouTube Removes The Views Count at 1:30, https://www.youtube.com/watch?v=chyB9Ot9QK8&t=90s; What If YouTube Removes The Views Count at 2:15, https://www.youtube.com/watch?v=chyB9Ot9QK8&t=135s)

## How do products get you to stay and come back?

- In 2021 he called notifications one of the most powerful human conditioning tools in existence, comparing them to Pavlov's bell: an input that produces a conditioned response. (How Notifications Are Messing With Your Life at 0:45, https://www.youtube.com/watch?v=OXqpy_H8onc&t=45s)
- He takes the habit loop of trigger, action and reward from the book The Power of Habit, and says a notification is the trigger. (How Notifications Are Messing With Your Life at 1:30, https://www.youtube.com/watch?v=OXqpy_H8onc&t=90s)
- His 2021 recipe for an effective notification has three ingredients: actionability, meaning a clear call to action that matches what happens after the tap, context, meaning it arrives exactly when you need it, and testing. (How Notifications Are Messing With Your Life at 3:45, https://www.youtube.com/watch?v=OXqpy_H8onc&t=225s; How Notifications Are Messing With Your Life at 4:30, https://www.youtube.com/watch?v=OXqpy_H8onc&t=270s)
- By 2026 he called notifications the single most powerful habit tool because they are the only **push lever** a product has. Everything else needs the user to act first. (The Ridiculous Engineering Of Notifications at 4:30, https://www.youtube.com/watch?v=Dx2yPk0FsGM&t=270s)
- He says the best products use notifications to build a habit and not to get a tap right now, and that Duolingo's engineered reminder led to a 40% reduction in subscription cancellations and 4.5 times growth in daily active users. (The Ridiculous Engineering Of Notifications at 0:00, https://www.youtube.com/watch?v=Dx2yPk0FsGM&t=0s; The Ridiculous Engineering Of Notifications at 0:45, https://www.youtube.com/watch?v=Dx2yPk0FsGM&t=45s)
- He says the secret of a notification that changes behaviour is specificity and not personalisation. Putting your name in does not work, while a photo preview of a real event from the same day years ago does. (The Ridiculous Engineering Of Notifications at 1:30, https://www.youtube.com/watch?v=Dx2yPk0FsGM&t=90s)
- He says some notifications are vague on purpose to open a curiosity gap. Loom tells him someone viewed his video so he wonders whether it was his boss, and Instagram does not name who mentioned you in a story, which earns it a free visit to the app. (The Ridiculous Engineering Of Notifications at 3:00, https://www.youtube.com/watch?v=Dx2yPk0FsGM&t=180s; The Reason Everyone's Obsessed With Spotify Wrapped at 2:15, https://www.youtube.com/watch?v=EuzRWd5U6_I&t=135s)
- He says timing is really about context: Duolingo waits 23.5 hours after your last session because you proved you were free at that time yesterday, and he says getting the timing right alone lifts taps by around 60%. (The Ridiculous Engineering Of Notifications at 3:45, https://www.youtube.com/watch?v=Dx2yPk0FsGM&t=225s)
- He says repetition turns a reminder into a habit. He cites Duolingo's data that after seven days in a row you are 2.4 times more likely to come back on your own, until not doing it feels wrong. (The Ridiculous Engineering Of Notifications at 7:30, https://www.youtube.com/watch?v=Dx2yPk0FsGM&t=450s)
- He says short-form feeds place like, comment and share in the thumb zone because the platforms want them pressed, and put the controls that give you power over the feed at the top where they are cumbersome to reach. (The Ridiculous Engineering Of Short Form Content at 1:30, https://www.youtube.com/watch?v=69Gw7aoWMMI&t=90s; The Ridiculous Engineering Of Short Form Content at 2:15, https://www.youtube.com/watch?v=69Gw7aoWMMI&t=135s)
- He says the single video is not what a short-form platform values. The real value is you getting addicted to the algorithm, which is why a TikTok found on Google sits behind a sign-in wall. (The Ridiculous Engineering Of Short Form Content at 4:30, https://www.youtube.com/watch?v=69Gw7aoWMMI&t=270s)
- He says replacing taps with scrolls made products more addicting. Pull to refresh in place of a refresh button and infinite scroll in place of pages look innocent but changed how people spend their time. (The Most Important Tiny Decision In Tech History at 9:45, https://www.youtube.com/watch?v=7EVZpW9Rdwk&t=585s)
- He says the core input decides how long you stay: on a phone you glide through Instagram without choosing and 40 minutes pass, while in a browser with the same content you close it after five. (The Most Important Tiny Decision In Tech History at 8:15, https://www.youtube.com/watch?v=7EVZpW9Rdwk&t=495s)
- In 2026 he said Claude Code hooks people like a strategy game and not like social media: each prompt is a short loop of action and reward, the growing file tree is the progress bar, and there is no natural stopping point. (Why Everyone is Suddenly Obsessed With Claude Code at 1:30, https://www.youtube.com/watch?v=gzt52Trk9w0&t=90s; Why Everyone is Suddenly Obsessed With Claude Code at 2:15, https://www.youtube.com/watch?v=gzt52Trk9w0&t=135s; Why Everyone is Suddenly Obsessed With Claude Code at 3:45, https://www.youtube.com/watch?v=gzt52Trk9w0&t=225s)
- He says unpredictable rewards are more addictive than guaranteed ones, citing a principle from The Art of Game Design. Claude Code's result is sometimes brilliant and sometimes almost works, and that near miss is the **slot machine moment** that triggers one more prompt. (Why Everyone is Suddenly Obsessed With Claude Code at 3:45, https://www.youtube.com/watch?v=gzt52Trk9w0&t=225s)
- In 2025 he said a chatbot interface is designed to make you stay where Google is designed to make you leave, because the main action after an answer is to ask something else. (How ChatGPT Is Weirdly Turning Into Facebook at 6:00, https://www.youtube.com/watch?v=C7Z11ghwevQ&t=360s)

## How do defaults and familiarity steer people without forcing them?

- He says the power of defaults is far bigger than people imagine. In 2022 he showed organ donor rates in European countries splitting into a very low and a very high group, with the only difference being the default: opt-out in Austria, opt-in in Germany. (Ads Are Coming To Your Lockscreen For Real at 6:00, https://www.youtube.com/watch?v=SQ_hBlGNR4c&t=360s)
- He read the Glance lock screen's very low opt-out rate as default bias and not approval: the average person accepts the default, while he and a few other nerds would install something else. (Ads Are Coming To Your Lockscreen For Real at 6:00, https://www.youtube.com/watch?v=SQ_hBlGNR4c&t=360s)
- He says defaults control the behaviour of millions for good and for bad, names Netflix autoplaying the next episode as an example, and says most people never change settings. (The 7 Levels of Tech Design at 7:30, https://www.youtube.com/watch?v=FQVRfadJkJk&t=450s)
- He says Google pays Apple 20 billion dollars a year to be the Safari default and estimated it would otherwise lose 60 to 80% of iPhone search traffic, because people use whatever is already there. (The 7 Levels of Tech Design at 9:00, https://www.youtube.com/watch?v=FQVRfadJkJk&t=540s)
- In 2024 he said Apple was using default bias to take AI: using ChatGPT means downloading, installing, making an account and opening an app every time, while asking Siri needs none of that. (How Apple Stole AI From Everyone Else at 3:00, https://www.youtube.com/watch?v=gV1f-lS0C9g&t=180s)
- He says WhatsApp turns read receipts on by default while iMessage asks you, and people given the choice tend to reveal less, so being seen without a reply carries weight on one and not the other. His summary: "Same feature, different default, different relationship." (The 7 Levels of Tech Design at 8:15, https://www.youtube.com/watch?v=FQVRfadJkJk&t=495s; The 7 Levels of Tech Design at 9:00, https://www.youtube.com/watch?v=FQVRfadJkJk&t=540s)
- He says ecosystems kill choice without forcing anyone. They make their own product the only logical pick while leaving the impression you can choose anything, and the magic breaks as soon as you step outside. (How Tech Secretly Killed Choice at 5:15, https://www.youtube.com/watch?v=UdzT1YxeZsY&t=315s)
- He says what the enter key does shapes the messages people send. Where enter sends, writing stays short and casual, and where enter makes a new line you get long emails with paragraphs and salutations. (The Insane Design Of Messaging at 3:00, https://www.youtube.com/watch?v=6DSW1rN-ZV4&t=180s)
- He uses Jakob's law to explain why all chat apps look alike: people spend most of their time on other products, so they want yours to work the same way. He says a better chat design probably exists but habits make it almost impossible to switch. (The Insane Design Of Messaging at 15:00, https://www.youtube.com/watch?v=6DSW1rN-ZV4&t=900s)
- He says old browsers all look the same because of familiarity bias, and that Arc got around it with a strong onboarding sequence and a personal card that makes you feel part of a club. (Why Everyone Is Obsessed With Arc Browser at 3:45, https://www.youtube.com/watch?v=UdopamaLJC8&t=225s)
- He uses loss aversion to explain why worse tools survive: even if the Dvorak layout were twice as fast you would not switch, because throwing away years of learning hurts more than the gain. (The Weird Reason Your Tech Stays Broken at 12:00, https://www.youtube.com/watch?v=-51ocAD6zBI&t=720s)
- He says change itself feels wrong even when the new thing is better. Snapchat's 2018 redesign drew a petition with 1.2 million signatures in a week and a 7.2% drop in the stock in a day, and he says the redesign was not wrong. (The Weird Reason Your Tech Stays Broken at 12:45, https://www.youtube.com/watch?v=-51ocAD6zBI&t=765s)

## How do products manage what you perceive and trust?

- He tested Spotify's shuffle with a 100-song playlist from 10 artists against a random number generator. The truly random order had clusters of the same artist back to back and the shuffle did not, though he notes it is a small sample. (The Spotify Shuffle Button is a Lie at 0:00, https://www.youtube.com/watch?v=NhAIA-o7f4M&t=0s)
- He says Spotify's shuffle started truly random and was changed because people complained they kept hearing the same artist. His verdict on the complainers and on himself at the roulette table: "humans suck at dealing with randomness". (The Spotify Shuffle Button is a Lie at 0:45, https://www.youtube.com/watch?v=NhAIA-o7f4M&t=45s; The Spotify Shuffle Button is a Lie at 1:30, https://www.youtube.com/watch?v=NhAIA-o7f4M&t=90s)
- He says shuffle is made less random so that it feels more random. People thought the iPod Shuffle was broken when it played the same artist twice in a row, so Apple introduced Smart Shuffle to keep similar songs apart. (The Secrets That Make Your Phone Actually Work at 3:00, https://www.youtube.com/watch?v=-YVtZV4DjCE&t=180s)
- He holds that how a product makes you wait matters more than how long the wait is. He said it in 2022 and repeated it in 2026. (The Sneaky Psychology Of Loading Screens at 0:00, https://www.youtube.com/watch?v=g8uhdyLTAvY&t=0s; The Ridiculous Engineering of Loading Screens at 0:00, https://www.youtube.com/watch?v=XqhduyTnEM8&t=0s)
- He says the Houston airport ended baggage complaints by changing the wait and not shortening it: moving baggage claim so people walked six times as long dropped complaints to almost zero, where hiring more staff had not. (The Sneaky Psychology Of Loading Screens at 0:45, https://www.youtube.com/watch?v=g8uhdyLTAvY&t=45s)
- He says instant is technically better but not always trusted more. For actions people consider important, such as a payment, they need a loading state to feel reassured even if the data involved is tiny. (The Ridiculous Engineering of Loading Screens at 2:15, https://www.youtube.com/watch?v=XqhduyTnEM8&t=135s)
- He cites a Harvard Business School study on flight search: when the product showed status messages about its work, users waited longer and rated it more favourably because they trusted the search was thorough. (The Ridiculous Engineering of Loading Screens at 8:15, https://www.youtube.com/watch?v=XqhduyTnEM8&t=495s)
- He says those status messages can be fake and still have the same effect: Google Flights shows the exact same pre-programmed text for the exact same time on every search. (The Ridiculous Engineering of Loading Screens at 8:15, https://www.youtube.com/watch?v=XqhduyTnEM8&t=495s)
- He cites a 1985 paper, The Psychology of Waiting Lines, for the rule that occupied time feels shorter than unoccupied time. It is why Uber shows the car moving and Domino's shows each pizza step. (The Ridiculous Engineering of Loading Screens at 9:00, https://www.youtube.com/watch?v=XqhduyTnEM8&t=540s)
- He ties the goal gradient effect to progress bars: we speed up when the finish line is in sight, so a bar that starts slow and speeds up near the end feels faster even if it takes longer overall. (The Ridiculous Engineering of Loading Screens at 9:45, https://www.youtube.com/watch?v=XqhduyTnEM8&t=585s)
- In 2026 he cited what he says Harvard researchers call the labor illusion: when you see work being done for you, you value the result more. Claude Code shows itself reading files and running tests where a consumer AI tool would hide it behind a spinner. (Why Everyone is Suddenly Obsessed With Claude Code at 4:30, https://www.youtube.com/watch?v=gzt52Trk9w0&t=270s)
- In 2026 he said waiting is directly correlated to how intelligent we perceive AI models to be. Shown two chatbots and asked which gives the better answer, most people pick the one that takes longer and writes its answer word by word, because it feels smarter and more thoughtful. (The Ridiculous Engineering of Loading Screens at 11:15, https://www.youtube.com/watch?v=XqhduyTnEM8&t=675s; The Ridiculous Engineering of Loading Screens at 12:00, https://www.youtube.com/watch?v=XqhduyTnEM8&t=720s)
- He names the effort heuristic, the belief that something that takes longer must be better, and says it applies to machines too: with AI people want the fastest response they still trust. (The Ridiculous Engineering of Loading Screens at 12:45, https://www.youtube.com/watch?v=XqhduyTnEM8&t=765s)
- He deliberately made users of his product Flask wait longer. The full AI answer came back at once and showing it felt weird, so he revealed it word by word with a delay, and users trusted it more. (The Ridiculous Engineering of Loading Screens at 12:00, https://www.youtube.com/watch?v=XqhduyTnEM8&t=720s)
- He says Wise's transfers became instant and users did not believe it, so Wise added a short animation of the money arriving, after which more people trusted the transfer. (The Ridiculous Engineering Of Animations at 15:00, https://www.youtube.com/watch?v=z3Lo5QT7nME&t=900s)

## Why does he say to watch users and not listen to them?

- He calls it the most important and counterintuitive rule in product management: "Don't listen to users but watch them as carefully as possible." (How Your Tech Tricks You at 15:45, https://www.youtube.com/watch?v=7nnyv4yJGJc&t=945s)
- His proof is Word against Notion. He says Microsoft listened to feature requests until Word became bloated, while Notion and similar tools observed that 95% of the time people use Word for notes, basic to-dos and daily life, and no user would have asked for a minimalist app with databases and cloud sync. (How Your Tech Tricks You at 15:45, https://www.youtube.com/watch?v=7nnyv4yJGJc&t=945s)
- He says users are bad at innovating but already struggle with the problems the next billion-dollar company will solve. Gmail's undo send and iOS filling in one-time SMS codes came from watching what people do. (How Your Tech Tricks You at 16:30, https://www.youtube.com/watch?v=7nnyv4yJGJc&t=990s)
- In 2026 he repeated the rule as never listen to your users, and applied it to people who want old YouTube back: the features they dislike are there because they work. (The Ridiculous Engineering Of YouTube Videos at 3:45, https://www.youtube.com/watch?v=giP1iIbSVh0&t=225s)
- He says what people say they want is not what they need in most cases. A random person would say yes to skipping to the climax of a video, which he argues would ruin entertainment videos. (I Redesigned YouTube's Worst New Feature at 3:00, https://www.youtube.com/watch?v=ELoNeW7GKog&t=180s)
- His earliest statement points the other way. In 2021 he said you can only win by listening to the people who use your product, using the Spotify shuffle complaints as the example of a company that did. (The Spotify Shuffle Button is a Lie at 3:45, https://www.youtube.com/watch?v=NhAIA-o7f4M&t=225s)
- In 2025 he accepted that talking to users is sound advice and then presented building for yourself, as the Notion Mail founders did, as an even better way. (The Ridiculous Engineering Of Notion at 9:00, https://www.youtube.com/watch?v=b00sgRR_Vc0&t=540s)
- For validating an idea he says to ask about real past behaviour and not opinions about the future: ask people to walk you through the last time they did the thing, and show your idea only in the last few minutes. (Do You Have a Genius Startup Idea at 5:15, https://www.youtube.com/watch?v=HABFEs90MZ8&t=315s)
- He says Google Maps shows what watching real use gives you: one-finger zoom exists because people on bikes cannot pinch, and Maps noticed many people preview a route without ever pressing start. (The Most Dangerous App That Everyone Loves at 6:45, https://www.youtube.com/watch?v=P2qOiKA56tI&t=405s; The Most Dangerous App That Everyone Loves at 7:30, https://www.youtube.com/watch?v=P2qOiKA56tI&t=450s)
- In 2021 he said the winners are not the ones with the best idea but the ones who design better tests, citing Google trying 41 shades of blue for links, and that you are part of hundreds of tests in the products you use daily. (How Notifications Are Messing With Your Life at 5:15, https://www.youtube.com/watch?v=OXqpy_H8onc&t=315s)
- He also names the cost of testing everything. A/B tests hand decisions to people who do not care: a feature loyal users hate is kept because it wins new ones, and a feature they begged for is buried because it confuses newcomers. (Why Tech Gets Terrible When It Gets Popular at 7:30, https://www.youtube.com/watch?v=BQLVgmhgtpQ&t=450s)
- He says usability testing made interfaces boring, because the plainer option wins the measured test and personality, quirks and Easter eggs count as noise. He adds that usability is great and that he worked for a usability testing tools company. (What Happened To User Interfaces at 3:45, https://www.youtube.com/watch?v=HdozqSVF-VM&t=225s; What Happened To User Interfaces at 4:30, https://www.youtube.com/watch?v=HdozqSVF-VM&t=270s; How Silicon Valley Killed Fun at 5:15, https://www.youtube.com/watch?v=DYdLq9icgrI&t=315s)

## Does he think the people behind it are evil?

- He calls it a misconception that people at tech companies have evil agendas and spend their days pulling people into doom scrolling. He says they are ordinary people, himself included, and that he knew this from working as a product manager. (The Real Reason Your Tech Makes You Unhappy at 0:00, https://www.youtube.com/watch?v=Vbg5TeoLTW0&t=0s)
- He describes how tech products are built as a loop: list feature ideas, judge each against the team's OKR (objective and key result), build, A/B test on half the users, and release what improves the metric. (The Real Reason Your Tech Makes You Unhappy at 1:30, https://www.youtube.com/watch?v=Vbg5TeoLTW0&t=90s; The Real Reason Your Tech Makes You Unhappy at 2:15, https://www.youtube.com/watch?v=Vbg5TeoLTW0&t=135s)
- He says the loop is not the problem. What decides whether a product delivers value or addiction is the objective it is pointed at and how that is measured. (The Real Reason Your Tech Makes You Unhappy at 2:15, https://www.youtube.com/watch?v=Vbg5TeoLTW0&t=135s; The Real Reason Your Tech Makes You Unhappy at 3:45, https://www.youtube.com/watch?v=Vbg5TeoLTW0&t=225s)
- He says engagement won as the objective because it is easy to measure, and because the monetisation models that rely on it (ads, in-app purchases and, more debatably, subscriptions) became the standard. Once more engagement equals more money, he says it is very easy to get into predatory tactics, dark patterns and promoting addiction. (The Real Reason Your Tech Makes You Unhappy at 6:45, https://www.youtube.com/watch?v=Vbg5TeoLTW0&t=405s; The Real Reason Your Tech Makes You Unhappy at 9:00, https://www.youtube.com/watch?v=Vbg5TeoLTW0&t=540s; The Real Reason Your Tech Makes You Unhappy at 9:45, https://www.youtube.com/watch?v=Vbg5TeoLTW0&t=585s)
- He says nobody set out to design the short-form feed to be manipulative. Platforms run hundreds of experiments and keep what raises engagement, and the winners exploit psychology: "Not because they chose to, but because the math just converges there." (The Ridiculous Engineering Of Short Form Content at 12:45, https://www.youtube.com/watch?v=69Gw7aoWMMI&t=765s; The Ridiculous Engineering Of Short Form Content at 13:30, https://www.youtube.com/watch?v=69Gw7aoWMMI&t=810s)
- He says the retention chart turns storytelling into more of a science than an art, with every word placed to open curiosity gaps and tension, and admits he did exactly that in the intro of the video where he says it. (How YouTube is Designed To Make YouTubers Quit at 10:30, https://www.youtube.com/watch?v=lGZvhW27WX0&t=630s)
- He says his own videos are optimised with music, text, images and story to keep you watching, because YouTube plays the time game too. (What Happens Now That We All Hate Using Our Phone at 4:30, https://www.youtube.com/watch?v=2LpR9zhEl2A&t=270s)
- He says habit-building notifications are neither good nor evil. The same mechanism can serve language learning or workouts, and the same habit can then be used to monetise you. (The Ridiculous Engineering Of Notifications at 8:15, https://www.youtube.com/watch?v=Dx2yPk0FsGM&t=495s)
- He says a design pattern only spreads if it is good for the company that adopts it, so a pattern that simply makes social media less addictive will not get adopted. (Social Media Is Broken But We Can Fix It at 12:00, https://www.youtube.com/watch?v=HqYIUV7Eqjk&t=720s)
- He puts part of the blame on users. He says we are participants and not victims of some evil tech company, because we complain about what does not work and then reject attempts at something new. (The Weird Reason Your Tech Stays Broken at 13:30, https://www.youtube.com/watch?v=-51ocAD6zBI&t=810s)

## How does he say to defend yourself?

- He does not believe government rules will stop manipulation, and says complaining on social media does even less. The best thing we can do is understand what is going on, which he says schools should teach in place of how to make slides. (The Internet Wants Your Money Here's How To Outsmart It at 10:30, https://www.youtube.com/watch?v=4OLt9pFlRvo&t=630s)
- His method is to learn the principles, notice them in each product, and then decide consciously, which includes walking away. He abandoned video games he liked because they pushed the cashless effect too far, and refused to buy from sellers whose practices were too shady for him. (The Internet Wants Your Money Here's How To Outsmart It at 10:30, https://www.youtube.com/watch?v=4OLt9pFlRvo&t=630s)
- He says every product he uses has these principles and that this is fine because he chooses with open eyes: "I use technology. I don't let technology use me." (The Internet Wants Your Money Here's How To Outsmart It at 10:30, https://www.youtube.com/watch?v=4OLt9pFlRvo&t=630s; The Internet Wants Your Money Here's How To Outsmart It at 11:15, https://www.youtube.com/watch?v=4OLt9pFlRvo&t=675s)
- His 2021 method is a monthly audit: open the digital wellbeing tool on your phone, see how many notifications each app sent, and ask of each app whether its notifications bring any value to your life. He prefers this to Do Not Disturb because it removes the problem at the origin. (How Notifications Are Messing With Your Life at 7:30, https://www.youtube.com/watch?v=OXqpy_H8onc&t=450s; How Notifications Are Messing With Your Life at 8:15, https://www.youtube.com/watch?v=OXqpy_H8onc&t=495s)
- In 2023 he said about 80% of notifications are completely useless and proposed a **notification witch hunt**: for a week, stop at each one and ask whether you could just open the app yourself later, and if so turn that app's notifications off entirely. (How I Finally Fixed My Digital Life at 2:15, https://www.youtube.com/watch?v=7PhEfKCHr7Y&t=135s)
- In 2026 he qualified this. He cited research where people switched off all notifications: they got more done but felt more anxious and lonelier, every participant turned them back on, and they still checked their phones. (The Ridiculous Engineering Of Notifications at 7:30, https://www.youtube.com/watch?v=Dx2yPk0FsGM&t=450s)
- He says built-in screen time limits fail because they are too easy to remove, and recommends **hardcore mode**: a physical NFC tag needed to unlock chosen apps, a stripped-down app with no feed, or deleting the app and using the web version. (How I Finally Fixed My Digital Life at 0:45, https://www.youtube.com/watch?v=7PhEfKCHr7Y&t=45s; How I Finally Fixed My Digital Life at 1:30, https://www.youtube.com/watch?v=7PhEfKCHr7Y&t=90s)
- He says to question your defaults for the software you spend 10 to 50 hours a week in, because people just trust the default, which is why Google pays Apple billions to be one. (How I Finally Fixed My Digital Life at 3:00, https://www.youtube.com/watch?v=7PhEfKCHr7Y&t=180s)
- He does not treat every persuasive design as something to block. He clicks the Google Photos memories notification every single time and says it turns a boring gallery app into something exciting. (How Notifications Are Messing With Your Life at 6:00, https://www.youtube.com/watch?v=OXqpy_H8onc&t=360s)
