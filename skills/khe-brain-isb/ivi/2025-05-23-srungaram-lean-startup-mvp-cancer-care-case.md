# Lean startup workshop 1, building an MVP for distributed cancer care, Sonali Srungaram

Sonali Srungaram, an ISB alumna and founder of a cancer care company, ran the first of the lean startup workshops. She used her own venture as the case: she set out the state of cancer care in India, asked the room which problem they would pick, and then walked through the choices that shaped her minimum viable product. She closed with general advice on money, time, outsourcing and persistence, and took questions. Testing the MVP and the pivot decision were left for the next session.

## What was said

### Putting the product in front of customers

- Sonali Srungaram says founders will always love their own product, but it takes time for someone else to even like it, so the product has to be put in front of customers, however scary, measured, and then built up.
- She says a founder who never has to change the idea or pivot is lucky but also unfortunate, because that founder never really learns.
- She says the method of the workshop holds however tough the industry or complicated the problem. She and the professor mapped her five years of decisions onto the lean startup framework to build the case.
- She says founders must prepare for unknown variables, such as a regulator changing the rules or a change in tax or import law.

### The cancer care problem she started from

- She says India has about 3,000 oncologists across government, trust and private hospitals.
- She says 90% of cancer treatment facilities are in 10 cities, because specialists who have trained for 12 to 15 years look for jobs in metros.
- She says cancer needs three kinds of oncologist, surgical, medical and radiation, and that on average a patient needs two of the three treatments.
- She says seven out of ten cancer patients in India stop treatment, because it is long, difficult and expensive.
- She puts the average cost of treatment at 4.5 lakh rupees, paid over three or four months.
- She says only 17% of India has health insurance, and most of that is state government cover. She gives the average package as 2.5 lakh rupees for a family of five.
- She says only 20 to 25% of cancer patients reach a private sector hospital. The government sector treats most of the rest.
- She says a large part of what a patient spends goes on travel, overnight stays and an attendant's lost work days, and that this carer load is why family members rotate and tire.

### Choosing which problem to solve

- The room listed the problems as too few oncologists, access, affordability and finance, support for carers, allied services, screening, hospital processes and R&D. She says a small startup cannot take all of them and must pick one.
- She says she could not solve the shortage of oncologists, because she had no teaching institution and a new college takes years to graduate a batch.
- She says the main internal debate was access against affordability. They chose access, on the reasoning that taking treatment closer to the patient removes the logistics cost and so also helps affordability.
- She says her first venture offered allied services such as psychology, nutrition and genetics. It struggled because treating doctors would not refer patients to an outside provider, and the cases it did get were mostly palliative.
- She says screening is needed but hard for a private company to bring to market. People do not come forward, the price points are hard to match, and at scale it depends on government, which she calls a poor payer.
- She says she was pushed for years to run the venture as an NGO and decided against it, because without making money she could not pay to attract the talent a credible institution needs.
- She says price signals quality to many customers. Her example is tea at a roadside stall against tea at a five-star hotel.

### Scoping the MVP with limited money

- She says she had about 8 crore rupees and 18 months to prove the model, and that about half of the first money raised was the founders' own.
- She says angels and investors ask how much of the founder's own money is in the venture, and discount a claim of sweat equity alone.
- She gives the cost of the three building blocks. A medical oncology ward with critical care takes about 2 months and 30 lakh rupees. An operating theatre takes about 4 months and 3 crore rupees. A radiation bunker takes about a year, 30 crore rupees and atomic energy regulator approval.
- She says 80% of cancer patients in India need medical oncology, 30% use surgery and 40% use radiation. Surgery is low because cancers are detected late.
- Her decision was to split the stack. They built medical oncology fully in-house, took a hybrid route on surgery by hiring surgeons and renting operating theatres, and outsourced radiation entirely.
- She describes the MVP as not a comprehensive cancer hospital but a treatment centre offering what 80% of patients need.
- She says the hypothesis was whether medical and surgical oncology could be taken 150 kilometres from the city centre. The distance was chosen so that a doctor living in the city could travel out in the morning, treat, and return the same evening.
- She says the company was the first distributed cancer hospital in India, and that doctors told the founders the idea was crazy.
- She says it took 18 months to build and test the model, because a single patient needs about nine months to complete treatment.

### People as the core

- She says they chose to spend on people and not on infrastructure, and hired medical oncologists full time because the doctors were core to the model and the treatment protocols had to stay inside.
- She says they hired younger doctors, not senior stars. She could not afford stars, and she needed doctors who would work with the model and accept the travel.
- She says the younger doctors they hired had trained in government hospitals with 100 to 150 outpatients a day, which gave them wide experience.
- She says they recruited from campus when other cancer hospitals had frozen hiring at the start of COVID, and that doctors who came from the districts were willing to go back and serve them.
- She says a part-time surgeon did not work, because the surgeon was never free when the rented operating theatre was. They moved to a full-time surgeon.
- She says a medical oncologist commands about twice a surgeon's salary in the market. They put both on pay parity so that the two would work as a team and not compete for cases.
- She says many hospital doctors in India carry sales targets, and that this stops them sharing patients and leads to dangerous clinical outcomes.
- She says her pay structure was a fixed fee, a performance incentive, and equity. The doctors valued salary far more than equity at the time.
- She says she pays doctors 10 to 15% above the market rate. Her example is 3 lakh rupees a month where the market pays 2.5 lakh.
- She says a founder recruiting scarce talent has to pitch like a company does on campus, with a career structure and a reason to join.
- She says it helps to have a co-founder who pushes back, and credits her co-founder with the pay parity idea.
- She says a founder is a harder negotiator when sure of the product, and that early on they sometimes gave away too much and balanced it in later increments.

### Customer segment, pricing and feedback

- She says it is not the founder's job to decide what the customer needs or can afford. The job is to make options available and choose where to play, knowing you cannot serve every segment.
- She chose the middle class as the segment, between government and trust hospitals below and premium corporate hospitals above, whose prices she puts at least 4 times higher.
- She used two-tier pricing: chemotherapy in the district cost less than in the city, because she wanted patients to stay in the district so the model could be proved there.
- She says a founder with incumbents can price by looking at them, and either undercut or add value. A founder with a new category has to experiment, and even with a product that is consumed quickly should expect three to nine months to find the right price.
- She says there is no shame in copying a comparable company's pricing as a starting point.
- She says she believes in focus groups and in talking to customers, and that a customer buying once is a small win but not a great one.
- She says patient feedback changed the product. Patients rejected the leather recliners used in premium chemotherapy lounges and wanted beds, and nurses found patients preferred being called aunty and uncle to sir and madam.
- She says the centre was designed so that a patient's file and bed are ready on arrival and the doctor sees them within 20 minutes.
- She tells founders to know whether they are a repeat business or a one-time business. Hers is one-time, so she has no repeat sale and can only aim to keep the patient for the whole treatment.
- She says a founder whose business needs constant selling must invest in the sales process, however good the product.

### Build, rent or outsource, and protecting the core

- She says renting is fine when it gets a prototype to market faster and cheaper.
- Her rule is to outsource what is commoditised and keep in-house whatever makes the product or process special.
- She says the process for giving chemotherapy 150 kilometres away, including drug transport, mixing, checks and discharge, was kept as a core in-house process and shared only on a need-to-know basis.
- She says founders in tech and data should protect their IP, because what took a year to build can be copied in three to six months.
- She says a copyright or patent only gives the right to defend, and that a court case in India takes years, so it is better to be careful first.
- She says trust has to be earned and does not come from a signed employment contract. A hired developer can be given one module without the whole plan.
- She says employees can become competitors and incumbents will fight a new entrant, and that once established the founder will face the next upstart.
- For hardware and deep R&D, she says the timeline is years, not months. Her advice is to connect with those who can give infrastructure and funds, such as universities and teaching hospitals where equipment makers give grants, and to accept a smaller ownership share.

### Where the model went

- She says 150 kilometres proved too long a journey, so they broke it into three levels. A level 1 super speciality centre in the city handles all treatments, a level 2 centre about 20 kilometres out handles about 80%, and district centres handle about 50%.
- She says the same small team of doctors rotates: full days at level 1, half days at level 2, and once a week in the districts.
- She says the venture opened two units on 4 February 2020, a month before COVID. It kept paying its doctors through a year of very little work, when revenue was 10 to 15% of the expected level.
- She says an investor came in during 2021 and the deal closed in October 2021. The investor got a low price for taking the risk in a downturn, and she does not regret the deal.
- She says health tech investors rejected the company for not being digital enough, and that it took two to three years to find investors who understand hospitals. Her advice is to look for like-minded investors and not a broad audience.
- She says healthcare investors told her distributed care would never match the unit economics of a 100-bed cancer hospital in a metro. Five years on she says the company is getting close to those metrics.
- She says the model has moved from renting infrastructure, to its own stand-alone clinics, to building its own hospital, because doctors and competition demand it.

### Money, time and persistence

- She says successful founders are not the ones with unlimited resources but the ones who did very well with limited resources.
- She tells founders to measure how long their money will last and to face hard money questions early.
- She says to spend on what you need, not what you want. If the business is fundamentally about marketing, marketing comes first even with only a prototype.
- She says a founder who wants employees on time has to be on time, and that missing your own deadline means you are bad at estimating or lax about time.
- She advises spending more time thinking before doing: "Figure all of this out in your head, execution will flow."
- She says persistence is as important as brain power, and that there is no shame in scrapping the work and starting over.
- She says a founder building a product or service cannot ignore social factors. Her example is women in the districts who were not allowed by family to come for screening.

### Hospital economics

- She says a basic multi-speciality hospital costs about 1 crore rupees per bed to build, and that civil work with mechanical, electrical and plumbing is 30 to 40% of that.
- She says the key metric of a hospital is average revenue per operating bed, and compares it to a hotel that makes money on occupied rooms.
- She gives 40,000 rupees as the revenue per operating bed of a good multi-speciality hospital, and one lakh for a premium chain.
- She says about 30% of a surgery bill goes to the surgeon, and with the anaesthetist and duty staff about half the bill goes to people.
- She gives 15% as the average EBITDA margin in the hospital sector, and says an average hospital can survive only about three months without patients.
- She says medical oncology drives about 50% of a cancer hospital's revenue, and surgical and radiation about 25% each.
- She says healthcare costs have not fallen over time because the disease burden moved from infectious to lifelong non-communicable diseases, and infrastructure and equipment keep getting more expensive.

## Examples and cases used

- Her own distributed cancer care venture in Telangana: the case for the whole session.
- Tea at a roadside stall and at a five-star hotel: the same product at 30 or 500 rupees, used to show that price signals value and that founders must choose a segment.
- Hotels: money is made on the occupied room, as hospitals make it on the occupied bed.
- Radiation equipment makers: she says they fund teaching hospitals so that doctors train on their machines, which makes universities a route for hardware founders.
- Siemens buying Varian: a diagnostic equipment maker moving into radiation machines by acquisition.
- Large pharma companies: she says a blockbuster drug costs close to 10 billion dollars, and that big firms now buy smaller outfits to do the R&D.
- Photon and proton beam therapy: she gives about 2.5 lakh rupees against 6.5 lakh, to ask what value the customer places on the difference.
- A state screening project in her earlier venture that covered 31.2 lakh people in 5 districts: impactful, but not workable as a private business.
- Investment banks and star traders: her comparison for investing heavily in the doctors who bring in the numbers.
