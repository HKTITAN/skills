# How the Atrium funds invest at seed stage

A member of the investment team that manages three related funds, Atrium Angels, Atrium Ventures and Consumer Collective, presented to the cohort and then took questions. The speaker's name is not given in the recording. The speaker said they are an engineer by training who came into venture investing through a larger fund. Most of it was question and answer.

## What was said

### The three funds

- The speaker says the funds enter at pre-seed and seed stage.
- The speaker says Atrium Angels invests only in ventures with an ISB link, Atrium Ventures has no such condition, and Consumer Collective invests only in the consumer sector.
- The speaker lists the consumer sector as consumer brands, enablers, consumer tech, consumer health and consumer gaming.
- The speaker says Consumer Collective is very young, started about three and a half to four months before the session.
- The speaker says the funds have made about 40 investments so far.
- The speaker says all three are pooled capital and not angel syndicates, so each comes onto a cap table as a single entity.
- The speaker says each fund has its own investment committee and its own pool of limited partners, and the same team manages all three.
- The speaker says separate limited partner pools mean one fund can invest in a space where another Atrium fund already holds a company, because the investors getting the exposure are different people.

### Cheque size, equity and convertibles

- The speaker says the funds write small cheques of 50 lakh to 1 crore rupees and do not lead rounds, so the lead investor sets the valuation.
- The speaker says the funds are not particular about the percentage of equity they get. It is simple arithmetic from the cheque and the round.
- The speaker says bigger funds typically want 10 to 12 percent for their first cheque, and these funds have no such minimum.
- The speaker says founders typically dilute 15 to 20 percent per raise, and less in a bridge round.
- The speaker says a pre-seed or seed deal is either a priced round or a convertible, and many of the funds' deals are convertibles.
- The speaker says a convertible sets a floor and a cap. In the example the floor is 40 crore and the cap is 60 crore: a next round at 80 crore converts at 60 crore, and a next round below 40 crore gives the investor the lower valuation.

### Valuation at seed

- The speaker says pre-seed valuation depends on the business model and on what similar companies have been valued at, and after Series A it becomes a multiple of revenue or EBITDA.
- The speaker says seed deals typically happen at 4 to 7 times top line, with most around 5 times and 7 times only for something extraordinary.
- The speaker says consumer companies typically get about 5 times top line and B2B companies a lower multiple of about 3 times.
- The speaker says a founder can benchmark against global competitors, but valuation also depends on the founder's profile and execution history, how much risk has been removed, market conditions and recent deals.

### Process

- The speaker says founders can reach the team by WhatsApp, email or LinkedIn.
- The speaker says the team holds three to four discussions with the founders, and the last is an investment committee call.
- The speaker says the investment committee includes the team and sector experts, everyone votes, and a majority decides.
- The speaker says the process takes about three weeks on average, depending on how fast the round is coming together.

### What changes a no into a yes

- The speaker says the team passes when a company is in a very competitive space and has no traction to show it is doing something different.
- The speaker says such a company can come back, for example eight months later, with a changed go-to-market, traction, paying customers and customers switching from competitors, and that gives more conviction.
- The speaker says that in a newer space with few competitors the team can still invest with no revenue and no traction.

### Views on sectors

- The speaker says people in India do not pay for SaaS, and a founder building SaaS should build it for the US or Europe.
- The speaker says the count of small businesses in India gives impressive market size numbers, but those businesses do not spend the money.
- The speaker says the only SaaS companies the team has seen work are the ones selling outside India.
- The speaker says India has less risk capital for AI companies than the US, and gives that as the reason there is no outlier yet.
- The speaker says astrology and puja services are a lucrative and competitive market in which several players can coexist.
- The speaker says younger, nuclear families still want to perform rituals without knowing how, which creates demand for on-demand services where the priest and materials are booked together.
- The speaker says the team is looking at experience businesses, because people surrounded by devices want an outlet, a community and offline experiences.
- The speaker says AI content tools will eventually put many ad agencies out of business, because agencies used to apply one playbook to ten brands.
- The speaker says they passed on an AI companion app for ethical reasons, although its adoption and engagement were very high and most chats happened between 1 am and 4 am.
- The speaker says that app planned to monetise by selling to users on the basis of the personal information they had shared with the agent.
- The speaker says the funds also invest in companies with a social element, as long as the bet is a commercial one.

### What the speaker liked in portfolio companies

- The speaker describes a lab-grown diamond jewellery retailer in the portfolio that uses in-store cameras and RFID trays to record which pieces a visitor spends time on.
- The speaker says that retailer messages similar designs to a visitor who leaves without buying, and store staff can show a returning visitor what they liked last time.
- The speaker says the same profiles, built by pin code and by store, help that retailer manage inventory.
- The speaker describes a sneaker brand in the portfolio as design-led, community-led and identity-led, with all content and manufacturing in-house and no external agency.
- The speaker says in-house manufacturing gives the sneaker brand room to experiment, such as a 15 pair release priced at 35,000 rupees to test what people will pay.
- The speaker says the bet on House of Chikankari was on capital efficiency and the overall build, not on one craft.
- The speaker says House of Chikankari had no website in its first year and sold through Instagram with in-house content, offered no cash on delivery for two years, and was largely bootstrapped.
- The speaker says handmade products limit how fast supply can scale and create an inventory problem, and the brand is widening its range and planning a printed line for the mass market.
- The speaker's advice to a founder told their craft brand is too niche is to expand the portfolio.
- The speaker says founding teams that include a chartered accountant tend to be careful with capital.

### Help beyond the cheque

- The speaker says the funds connect founders with people who help on branding, packaging and inventory planning, with no fees involved and no obligation to use them.
- The speaker says the team makes introductions from its network to any founder it is in touch with, including founders it did not invest in.
- The speaker says a small fund has the time to help companies it did not fund, which a large fund with a large portfolio does not.
- The speaker tells founders to use the ISB alumni network and says alumni from 20 to 25 years back respond when contacted.

## Examples and cases used

- House of Chikankari: a handcrafted apparel brand backed for its capital efficiency, with Instagram-only selling in its first year.
- A lab-grown diamond jewellery retailer: store cameras and RFID used for personalisation and inventory planning.
- A sneaker brand: in-house design, content and manufacturing giving high margins and room to test prices.
- A convertible with a 40 crore floor and a 60 crore cap: how the conversion valuation is set by the next round.
- An AI companion app: high engagement with no monetisation, which the speaker declined on ethical grounds.

## Numbers given

- 50 lakh to 1 crore rupees, the funds' cheque size.
- About 40 investments so far across the funds, as the speaker states.
- 15 to 20 percent, the dilution the speaker says founders typically take per raise.
- 10 to 12 percent, the stake the speaker says bigger funds want for a first cheque.
- 4 to 7 times top line for seed valuations, about 5 times for consumer and about 3 times for B2B, as the speaker gives them.
- About three weeks from first contact to decision.
- An average order value close to 7,000 rupees at the sneaker brand, and close to 6,000 rupees made on a shoe with a 7,000 rupee MRP, as the speaker states.
