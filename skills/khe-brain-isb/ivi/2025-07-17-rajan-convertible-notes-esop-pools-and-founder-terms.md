# Convertible notes, ESOP pools and founder terms, Kaushik Rajan

Kaushik Rajan, who describes himself as a venture capital lawyer for most of his career, took the cohort through the fundraising documents and the terms founders get wrong. The recording caught only fragments of a longer class with slides, so this note covers the parts that came through: convertible note discounts, caps and floors, ESOP pool maths, founder salary and exit clauses. He took questions throughout.

## What was said

### The deal sequence and early mistakes

- The class notes list the deal sequence as NDA, term sheet, due diligence, share subscription agreement, shareholders agreement, then conditions precedent and closing.
- Kaushik Rajan says most founders agree to whatever comes in their first term sheet.
- He says founders come to him at the seed stage after a pre-seed round, and it is very difficult to get out of what was agreed earlier.
- He tells founders to check whether a fund has already invested in a competitor in their space at the same stage.
- He says a fund's investment in a competitor at a different stage is less of a threat, because the fund may see a fit and suggest a merger.
- He says once someone becomes a shareholder they know what is happening inside the company, and he tells of a reputed fund that sold its shares in a startup to one of its own investors, a large conglomerate. He does not name the fund.

### Negotiating with investors

- He says a negotiation point does not always need an accept or reject answer. Sometimes the founder should keep the conversation going, which he compares to batting time in cricket, until both sides reach a consensus.
- On founder pay, he tells founders to state the amount they need as a safety net and the rationale for it, then let both sides sleep on it.
- He says investors look at the salary a founder proposes, and that a promoter's employment agreement is a transaction document.
- He gives one to two lakh rupees as the range for founder salaries at the seed stage.
- He describes each investor as wanting the founder to keep running until their one rupee becomes five, after which the next investor takes it from five to twenty five, while the founder is the one getting burnt out.

### Priced rounds and runway

- He defines a priced round as one where a valuation is fixed and the investor puts in a set amount at that rate.
- He says the money can come in as CCPS or as equity, and that all investors now ask for CCPS because it sits above equity in the hierarchy.
- He says the expected runway from a round varies between 21 and 27 months, with 24 months as the general expectation.

### Convertible notes: discount, cap and floor

- He says the tenure of a convertible note is usually about two years, and tells founders to keep it at two years or more so they are not under pressure to raise within a shorter time.
- He gives a 15 to 20% discount on an annualised basis as the market rate for convertible notes.
- He says founders need to know the market rate because some angels ask for a 50% discount, and an uninformed founder sometimes says yes.
- He says a flat discount is better for the founder than an annualised one, because the founder is not pushed by the fear of giving a larger discount if the next round takes longer to close.
- He explains that with a cap, the note converts at the discounted figure if that is lower than the cap, and at the cap if the discounted figure is higher. This is the investor's protection.
- He explains that with a floor, the note converts at the higher of the floor and the discounted figure, which protects the founder.
- He says a cap and a floor are not actual valuations. They are protections, and the conversion comes out between the two.
- He tells founders not to set the floor too low.
- He says the reason people use convertible notes is to avoid a long negotiation on valuation, so founders should not fret over whether the cap or floor is slightly off. His example is that the difference comes to 7% against 7.5%.
- He says valuation is an art and not a science, and that what the market tells you is the valuation.
- He says accelerator programmes give uncapped notes, and that if the investor has not asked for a cap it is fine to go without one.
- He compares a down round to "seeing your baby undergo open heart surgery" and tells founders to avoid one.

### Liquidation preference

- He says liquidation preference works as last in, first out, with 1x of the investor's investment value as the amount.

### ESOP pools

- He says most investors will tell a founder to create an ESOP pool of about 10% at the time of investment.
- He gives the maths: if the investor takes 10%, the founder creates an 11.11% ESOP pool before the round, and it comes down to 10% after the investment.
- He explains ESOPs with two schools. The kiddie school is the option pool and the adult school is the cap table.
- He says when an option holder leaves the kiddie school for the adult school it is called exercise. The option becomes an equity share, and that is when actual dilution happens for everyone on the cap table.
- He says that while options are unvested and unexercised, an investor who holds 10% on paper actually holds about 11.11% of the company, and the MCA filings will show the investor at more than 10%.
- He says tax hits at the point of exercise and nothing can be done about that.
- He says an employee can delay exercise until one day before the company is sold, then exercise and sell the shares in the sale.

### Exit clauses and the end goal

- He tells founders not to accept a founder buyback or put option as an exit clause. A company buyback is acceptable.
- He calls operational break-even the holy grail and says that is what founders should look at.
- He says money raised from others is always borrowed, and describes a founder with 20 million of debt as carrying a burden.
- Asked for example term sheets, he first says no, then says he will try to share some with the names removed if he can find them.

## Numbers given

- 15 to 20% a year: Kaushik Rajan's figure for the market discount on a convertible note.
- 50%: the discount he says some angels ask for.
- About two years: the usual tenure of a convertible note, in his telling.
- 21 to 27 months, generally 24: the runway investors expect a round to last.
- 11.11% before the round becoming 10% after: his ESOP pool example when the investor takes 10%.
- One to two lakh rupees: his range for founder salaries at the seed stage.
- 1x: the liquidation preference multiple he describes.
