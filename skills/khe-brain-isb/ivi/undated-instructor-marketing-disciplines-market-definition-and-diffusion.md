# Marketing disciplines, market definition and diffusion of innovations

A marketing professor, who calls himself a colleague of ISB faculty and a textbook author, taught this class as one day of a multi-day marketing course. The first half was a live case exercise in which three founders in the room presented and the class debated need, market definition, segments and positioning. The second half was a lecture on launch strategy and the five factors that drive adoption of a new product. The recording starts mid-lecture.

## What was said

### Be very good at one discipline

- The instructor says a winning company is excellent at one discipline, and that discipline becomes its culture and constrains which markets and businesses it can choose.
- He names three disciplines through examples: cost control at Walmart, bringing the latest products to customers at Hewlett Packard, and psychological benefit and trust at Johnson and Johnson.
- He says a company that tries to be everything to everybody ends up in the middle and fails, and he cites Sears and Kmart as retailers that declined because they could not execute one discipline well.
- He tells founders not to pick a discipline from the inside out. The companies he cites first looked at what value they could create best, then grew into a discipline as they learned.

### Need and market definition

- He defines a latent need as one the customer does not know about or is unwilling to state when asked directly.
- He says a market is defined by people who have a need, are willing to pay and are able to pay. The product does not appear anywhere in a market definition.
- He says to think from the consumer's problem, not from a building or a product. His example is that nobody sets out to build an airport. People have a transport problem and the airport and its features are the solution.
- He says the better practice is to define the market relatively broadly and then define segments inside it, because that leaves more room to act.
- He says a founder who defines the market as only the segment the existing product fits ends up running a small lifestyle company.
- He says developing the product first and then looking for a market lowers the chance of success dramatically, and that research supports this.
- He tells of an engineer with a product and intellectual property who did the market exercise, found the product was not commercially viable, and took a job instead of going ahead.
- He says "Somebody's market is somebody's segment", meaning the line between market and segment depends on how broadly the founder starts.
- He says initial segments are a first cut. After concept testing and more data, founders typically revise them.
- He says when needs differ by region or group, each segment needs its own position and values, and the product design may have to change too.

### Mass customisation versus personalisation

- He lists four ways to handle differences among customers: mass marketing, traditional segmentation, mass customisation and personalisation.
- He says in mass customisation the company creates conditions in which customers customise the solution themselves, because they know more about their own problem.
- He says in personalisation the company slices the market into ever smaller groups and uses technology to make serving them viable.

### The snake chart

- He describes the snake chart as a cheap tool to use before a perceptual map. It compares solutions to the same problem within one segment.
- To build it, he says to define the dimensions of the need from market research and judgment, such as cost, space taken and difficulty, and mark them on one axis.
- Each solution is then rated high, medium or low on every dimension and drawn as one line, so each line on the chart is one solution.
- He says a founder designing a new solution uses the chart to see where existing solutions sit and where to place their own.
- He says the chart also tells an audience why the solution is different and better than others.
- He says the competitor grid in an investor deck does the same job, and the snake chart is more nuanced because it grades high, medium and low instead of present or absent.
- He says a founder must be clear whether the ratings are their own assessment or consumer perception, because positioning is pure consumer perception.

### Positioning statement versus position

- He says the positioning statement is an internal document that guides strategy, target customers and competitors, and the team returns to it to stay consistent.
- He says the position actually achieved in the market is one to three key ideas per segment that make a customer of that segment say "I want this".
- He says who the customers are belongs in the internal positioning statement, not in the position the customer perceives.
- He says when one solution serves varied segments, the position can differ by segment while a few higher-level elements, such as safety and security, stay common because they are the brand.

### Platform businesses

- He says network effects dominate platform businesses. He gives the telephone as a direct network effect and Uber drivers and riders as an indirect one.
- He says a two-sided platform must create value for segments on both sides, and there must be common ground between them. If those conditions are not met, the platform fails.
- He says platform businesses are very hard, and the reward for the winner is a very sustainable advantage that nobody can dislodge.
- He says the platform that runs fastest wins, and investors will not fund the second or third player.
- He says a platform that sets quality standards must keep its revenue model transparently separate from those standards, or users will not trust the listings.
- He says restricting listings to those who pay destroys the real value an information platform creates.
- He says a platform that only gives users information so they can solve a problem is in a different business from a company that solves the problem itself, and the founder must know which one they are.

### Focus when raising money

- His advice to a startup seeking funding is to pick one major unsolved problem it can solve better than others, hit it hard, get a foothold and show immediate traction, and think about expanding only after that.
- He says taking on several different problems at once means a different set of activities for each, and the startup gets stuck.
- He says clarity about concepts and language matters in fundraising, because a founder who understands them can correct an investor who is thinking wrongly.
- He says the biggest red flag for an investor is how a founder treats the investor's money, for example staying in expensive hotels, and that investors also look at the founder's own skin in the game.

### Launch strategy

- He separates the equilibrium strategy, which is the position for the whole segment, from the launch strategy, which is how the startup tweaks things inside that position to get traction quickly.
- He says a startup that spreads its launch money across the whole market fights on too many fronts, gets no quick traction and dies.
- He says low sales is a consequence, not the problem. Founders should brainstorm the factors that could make the product fail and track those, because they are the switches that improve sales.
- He says the launch is a make or break period for a startup.

### Two families of adoption theory

- He says one family of theories explains adoption by differences among people, and the other by differences among products.
- In the people family he names the technology adoption cycle and the book Crossing the Chasm, which claims innovators are fundamentally different from the majority, so many products are adopted by the first group and then get no traction.
- In the product family he names Everett Rogers and his five factors of diffusion. He says his own textbook lists six, which collapse into the five.
- He says the two families complement each other, and knowing the product factors lets a founder tweak and manage them before launch.
- He says all five factors are perceptions held by the consumer.

### The five factors

- Relative advantage is the degree to which an innovation is perceived as better than the idea it replaces, relative to the other options. A consumer who does not perceive it will not buy.
- Compatibility is the fit with previously introduced ideas, values, beliefs, needs, habits and experiences, meaning the consumer's sense of how things ought to be.
- Complexity is the degree to which the innovation is perceived as difficult to understand and use.
- Trialability and observability are the remaining two. He treats observability as how visible the product in use is to other people.
- He says founders usually think only about relative advantage and ignore the other four.

### A checklist for raising relative advantage at launch

- First, find sub-segments inside the target segment that will see more relative advantage than others, hit them hard and let word of mouth carry the rest.
- Second, deal with price by removing sticker shock, for example through bundling.
- Third, use communication. He calls these three the minimum to consider.
- He says to analyse all five factors first and mark each as good or bad, and only then design fixes, without mixing the solution into the diagnosis.
- He says "High level analysis is dangerous in marketing". The founder must go to the consumer and see what they actually do.
- He says successful founders who reached their strategy by trial and error can map their success back onto these factors, and thinking about them beforehand greatly improves the odds.
- He says marketing is about problems and solutions, not soaps and shampoos, which is why he teaches with unusual examples.

## Examples and cases used

- Walmart: its culture is cost control down to every penny, it hires people who save money, and its warehouse is fully automated. Changing that culture would unravel the company.
- Hewlett Packard printers: the company made its own products obsolete each year instead of waiting for a competitor, using 30 to 40 cross-functional teams to design new printers.
- Johnson and Johnson: it builds trust with the doctor, then asks what else it can supply to the same doctor, leveraging that relationship.
- Sears and Kmart: once dominant US retailers that declined because they did not execute one discipline well.
- OYO: its quality standards are standalone and transparent and are not tied to its revenue model.
- Sony Aibo: used stealth positioning with early buyers and took their feedback to develop the product, which he calls positioning used in product development.
- A premium adhesive bandage with silver: most buyers pick bandages without thought, so the launch should target sportspeople, mothers of small children and people with weak immunity or diabetes, who look for the best. Observability was poor because nobody can tell which brand is on a wound, and a visible colour or pattern fixes that. He says the product succeeded.
- Wine with a screw cap versus a cork: two identical wines are judged differently because of the belief that cork is better, which he calls a compatibility problem for wine sellers.
- The QWERTY keyboard: designed for mechanical typewriters so that letters often used together sat far apart. A later keyboard designed the opposite way was better and got almost no market share. He uses it to show how powerful compatibility is.
- Segway: only observability was high. Buyers did not perceive relative advantage at the price, and there was no shared instinct about where it should ride, so some smaller cities and towns banned it. He says the launch should have targeted airports, universities, factory floors, security guards and tour guides, worked with civic authorities, police and tour operators in specific small towns to set rules and release it on set paths, and offered paid trial rides in malls. He says the company later narrowed its focus this way and got the traction it has.
- Religious movements: he walks the five factors through how a new sect grows. It starts with vulnerable sub-segments, prices at zero or below by giving food and help, keeps the message simple, dresses and speaks like the audience for compatibility, and holds gatherings and processions in public for trialability and observability.

## Numbers given

- 30 to 40: the cross-functional teams he says Hewlett Packard had designing new printers.
- Three: the minimum levers he lists for raising relative advantage at launch, which are sub-segments, pricing and communication.
