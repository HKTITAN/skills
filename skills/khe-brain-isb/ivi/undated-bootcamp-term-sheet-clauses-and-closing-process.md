# Term sheet clauses and the closing process

Speaker and date are withheld. This note keeps general teaching points only and leaves out the speaker's own frameworks, examples and materials. It covers how to read a term sheet, the main economic and control clauses, what is binding, who pays the costs, the legal steps to close an angel round in India, and revenue-based financing.

## What was said

### Reading a term sheet

- The instructor splits a term sheet into economic rights, which cover value and shareholding, and control rights, which cover what needs the investor's permission.
- The instructor says a standard term sheet template is available on the Startup India website.
- The instructor tells founders to always check whether a stated valuation is pre-money or post-money.
- The instructor says the share subscription agreement records the transaction, meaning who puts in how much at what valuation for how many shares. The shareholders agreement records rights and restrictions.
- The instructor says early rounds combine the two into one document, and every existing shareholder has to sign it, not only the founders.
- The instructor says a round is properly closed only when the shareholders agreement is made part of the articles of association and filed.
- The instructor recommends the book Venture Deals by Brad Feld to any founder about to sign a term sheet.

### Primary and secondary sales

- The instructor says that in a primary issue the company issues new shares and receives the money, so every existing holder keeps the same number of shares at a smaller percentage.
- The instructor says that in a secondary sale an existing holder sells shares and the money goes to that holder, not to the company.
- The instructor says secondary sales usually happen at a discount to the round price, which lowers the incoming investor's blended entry price while the headline valuation stays the same.
- The instructor says founders who have worked for years on a minimal salary often negotiate a small secondary sale with an incoming investor in later rounds, and investors do not view this badly.
- The instructor says most investors accept a founder secondary of up to 5% of the round.
- The instructor's rule of thumb is that total founder salary in the first year after funding should be no more than about 10% of the amount raised.

### Board and voting clauses

- The instructor advises offering board observer rights when an investor asks for a board seat, since a seat carries a vote.
- The instructor's thumb rule is to give a board seat only when total dilution passes 40% or a single investor holds more than 20 to 25%.
- The instructor says most board meetings happen only on paper, and in a dispute the easiest claim for an investor to make against a founder is that no meeting notice was ever received.
- The instructor says affirmative voting rights, also called reserved matters, list decisions that need the investor's written approval.
- The reserved matters the instructor lists are raising capital, taking debt, changing the business model, adding a co-founder or director, hiring above a salary level, related-party transactions and raising founder salary.
- The instructor says information rights usually mean a monthly management report, and calls this standard.

### Economic protection clauses

- The instructor says pre-emptive rights let an investor put money into future rounds to keep their percentage, and calls this standard.
- The instructor says anti-dilution applies when a later round is at a lower valuation.
- The instructor tells founders never to accept full ratchet anti-dilution and to insist on weighted average, with the formula written into the shareholders agreement.
- The instructor calls a 1x liquidation preference the standard and says to negotiate anything above it.

### Transfer and exit clauses

- The instructor explains right of first refusal as the investor's right to be offered the founder's shares first, at the offered value, before the founder goes to the market.
- The instructor explains right of first offer as protection for the founder: an investor who wants to sell must come to the founders first, which gives 90 to 120 days to arrange a buyer.
- The instructor says founders can list competitors the investor may not sell to, and investors will limit how many names are on that list.
- The instructor explains drag along as the investor's right to force the founders to sell on the same terms to a buyer who wants the whole company, when the founders cannot otherwise give an exit.
- The instructor explains tag along as the investor's right to sell proportionately whenever the founders sell.
- The instructor calls both drag along and tag along standard clauses.

### What is binding, and conditions

- The instructor says a term sheet is not binding on either side except for clauses such as confidentiality and exclusivity.
- The instructor gives 60 days as an example of an exclusivity period.
- The instructor says a founder cannot tell another investor who issued a term sheet or at what valuation. The most the founder can say is that better terms are on the table.
- The instructor says a standstill provision means no major business decisions between term sheet and closing.
- The instructor says conditions precedent must be finished before the money moves, and conditions subsequent can be finished after.
- The instructor says the real negotiation after due diligence is moving fixes into conditions subsequent, with a window such as 120 days, so that they do not hold up the money.

### Costs and advisers

- The instructor says the company pays the due diligence and legal costs of the round, so the founder should negotiate a cap.
- The instructor says founders should also ask for a clause that the company pays nothing if the investor backs out.
- The instructor says total transaction costs above 5% of the round are too much.
- The instructor advises offering to have the company's own lawyer draft the agreements from the term sheet.
- The instructor advises using a lawyer or accountant who has done fundraising transactions before, not a friend or the cheapest quote.

### Closing an angel round step by step

- The instructor advises drafting the term sheet yourself and negotiating it with one or two representatives of the angels, with the other angels giving those representatives a power of attorney.
- The instructor says term sheet approvals can be collected in writing by email.
- The sequence the instructor gives is: collect investor identity documents, draft and sign the subscription and shareholders agreement, raise authorised capital through board and shareholder resolutions and filings, get a registered valuer's certificate, open a separate share application bank account, call for money, file the allotment, and only then move the money to the main account.
- The instructor says the money cannot be used until the shares are allotted and the filing is approved.
- The instructor says the process takes at least two months, and founders should plan for two to three months from term sheet to usable money.
- The instructor says investors who join later sign a deed of adherence to the same shareholders agreement, so only the later steps are repeated.
- The instructor says an investor cannot hand over money early during this process, which is why a convertible note is used as a bridge.
- The instructor suggests keeping a convertible note agreement ready and asking a committed investor for part of the money on a note while the round closes.
- The instructor says a company cannot have more than 200 investors on its cap table.

### Due diligence

- The instructor says due diligence works from financial and legal checklists and takes over a month on its own.
- The instructor says founders usually find their compliance is less complete than they believed, and should prepare with a checklist beforehand.
- The instructor says most companies never get their share certificates stamped by the state stamp duty department, and this surfaces later in due diligence or in a notice with penalties.

### Revenue-based financing

- The instructor describes revenue-based financing as debt for businesses with predictable revenue, used mainly for working capital locked in inventory.
- The instructor says repayment is a percentage of monthly revenue, so it falls in a weak month.
- The instructor says the headline rate plus processing and other fees takes the real cost above 15% a year, and it can reach 18%.

## Numbers given

- 10% of the amount raised: the instructor's ceiling for total founder salary in the first year after funding.
- 5% of the round: the instructor's figure for an acceptable founder secondary.
- 20 to 50%: the discount to the round valuation at which the instructor says secondary sales happen.
- 40% total dilution, or 20 to 25% held by one investor: the instructor's thumb rule for when to give a board seat.
- 90 to 120 days: the time the instructor says a right of first offer gives the founders.
- About 4% of the round on average, with 5% as the ceiling: the instructor's figures for fundraising costs including banker and due diligence fees.
- About 1 lakh rupees: the instructor's lean figure for the legal and filing work of a small round.
- 2 to 3 months: the instructor's estimate from signed term sheet to money in the bank.
