# Investor returns and what gets funded

Speaker and date are withheld. This note keeps general teaching points only and leaves out the speaker's own frameworks, examples and materials. It covers the return an investor requires for a given risk, why banks and venture funds behave differently, how a venture fund's economics force it to look for very large winners, how different investor types behave, and how to approach investors.

## What was said

### Required return rises with risk

- The instructor says a financial investor comes in to make a financial return, so a founder's competition for capital is every other place the investor could put the money.
- The instructor says a founder who raises must be ready for financial objectives to take priority over other objectives the business started with.
- The instructor explains the required return as the risk-free rate plus beta times the gap between the market return and the risk-free rate.
- The instructor uses a bank deposit at about 6 to 6.5% as the risk-free return and a stock index at 12% as the market return, so a beta of one gives the market return.
- The instructor says the index counts as low risk because it has a long data history, liquidity, regulation and small ticket sizes.
- The instructor says risk rises with illiquidity, an unvalidated business model, an unknown market size and an unproven founder, and the return an investor asks for rises with it.
- The instructor places a private company with an unvalidated model at the top of the risk ladder, with money locked in for 8 to 10 years and no certainty of a payout.
- The instructor says the return an investor expects is a return on the investor's capital and not a profit margin.
- The instructor tells founders not to put beta or discounted cash flow into an early-stage financial model, because those tools belong to mature companies whose next five years are predictable.

### Why banks lend the way they do

- The instructor says a fixed deposit pays more than a savings account because the depositor has locked up liquidity, which lets the bank predict its funds and lend them out.
- The instructor says a bank earning a spread of about five points loses its whole margin if one loan in twenty loses its principal.
- The instructor says that is why a bank asks for past financials, profitability, collateral and personal guarantees.
- The instructor says a business a bank agrees to fund is low risk by definition, so a bank loan at about 12% is cheap capital.
- The instructor gives 10.75 to 12% as the range for bank lending, and about 18% a year for lenders who charge 1.5% a month.

### How an investor sizes up a business

- The instructor says an investor works from the top down: country risk first, then industries growing faster than the market, then the size of the company.
- The instructor says a small business in a slow-growing industry has already lowered its chances of raising funds.
- The instructor says a small business that grows only at its industry's rate is just surviving.
- The instructor says headcount is a large factor in how a financial investor assigns risk, and a very small team is put in the high-risk category by default.
- The instructor says growth investors want growth in value and not only in revenue, and that the growth has to hold every year for 8 to 10 years.
- The instructor says investors will not fund a business that has no answer to how it will stop others copying it.
- The instructor says a new product with no moat means spending years building a market that a large company can then enter and take.

### How a venture fund's economics work

- The instructor says a venture fund charges a yearly management fee of 2% and takes 20% of the profit above a hurdle rate, with 12% given as an example of a hurdle.
- The instructor says the fees over the fund's life come out of the money raised, so less than the full fund is invested.
- The instructor says a fund spreads its money across many startups and expects most to fail, so the few survivors have to return the whole fund.
- The instructor says simply returning the fund's money after eight years is not acceptable to the fund's own investors, who could have bought listed shares.
- The instructor says this is why a venture investor needs each startup to show a path to about 20 times the money in about five years.
- The instructor says exit clauses run to five or six years because a fund spends its first two years finding deals.
- The instructor says the growth multiple investors expect falls by stage, to about 3 times in later rounds and about 1.5 times close to an IPO.
- The instructor says a founder cannot easily step off the venture path, because without an IPO the investors need an exit through a buyback, and most companies cannot pay that even when profitable.

### Doubling time thumb rules

- The instructor teaches the rule of 72: divide 72 by the yearly rate to get the years needed to double money, or by the years to get the rate.
- The instructor adds 114 for tripling and 144 for quadrupling, and says these are approximations that hold over four to five years and more.

### Funding cycles

- The instructor says that when stock markets give very high returns it is hard for startups to raise, because a business cannot promise more than the market is already paying.
- The instructor says the reverse holds when market returns are low.
- The instructor says funding follows liquidity, and more money flows to funds and startups when interest rates are low.
- The instructor says founders should check where venture money is going by sector before raising.

### Corporate venture funds and family offices

- The instructor says corporate venture funds and family offices invest for reasons beyond financial return.
- The instructor says a large consumer company that invests gets early access to a growing brand, can buy it if it does well, and keeps a rival off the cap table.
- The instructor says a corporate investor will usually ask for a first right to acquire or a path to majority ownership, and will not promise to acquire the startup.
- The instructor says that once one large corporate has invested, a rival corporate's fund will not invest, and other bidders stay away.
- The instructor advises agreeing at the time of investment how the company will be valued in a future acquisition, because the founder's bargaining power is gone after that.
- The instructor says the person running a corporate venture fund is an employee judged against other venture investors, so a founder should still pitch a venture-style return.
- The instructor says family offices have lower return expectations than venture funds.

### Signals between investors

- The instructor says that when a well-known fund backed a company and then does not follow on, other investors read the company as a laggard and stay away.
- The instructor says an investor who already backs a close competitor, or who lost money in the category, is a poor target.
- The instructor says a founder can negotiate that an investor will not also invest in competitors.

### Approaching investors

- The instructor says to check an investor's recent deals, cheque size and co-investors before pitching.
- The instructor says to approach the person at a fund who covers the relevant sector and whose role is finding deals.
- The instructor treats investor outreach as a sales funnel and says a founder needs a tracker of every investor conversation.
- The instructor says to pitch first to less important investors as practice, so that the data and answers are ready before the most wanted investors are reached.
- The instructor says funds that do little publicity receive fewer pitches, which makes them a better opportunity.
- The instructor advises pitching to angel networks and not to individual angels one by one.
- The instructor says pre-seed investors look at market size, the team's ability to execute, credibility and traction.
- The instructor says international investors who write large cheques are not relevant to a founder raising a round of a few crore rupees.

## Numbers given

- 6 to 6.5% risk-free and 12% market return: the inputs the instructor uses in the required return formula.
- 2% a year and 20% of profit above a hurdle: the fund fee structure the instructor describes.
- About 20 times in about five years: what the instructor says a venture investor needs from a startup.
- 8 to 10 years: how long the instructor says venture money stays locked in.
- 72, 114 and 144: the instructor's thumb rule numbers for doubling, tripling and quadrupling.
