# Choosing investors, dilution and control, Sonali Srungaram

Sonali Srungaram, founder of a cancer care company, gave the third of her guest lectures to the cohort. This first half of the session covered how to pick and manage investors, the funding rounds, how much ownership founders should try to keep, board seats, bank account control and simple ways to value an early company. She taught from her own experience of raising money and took questions throughout.

## What was said

### Kinds of investor

- Sonali Srungaram describes three kinds of investor: the hands-off one who wants a 3x to 5x return in five years and checks in now and again, the mentor who knows the sector and wants to guide, and the controlling one.
- She recommends mentor investors but warns that founder and mentor will diverge at some point, and the founder has to be willing to have that conversation and chart their own path.
- She says a controlling investor will pressure a founder into what the investor wants, such as a sale within two years so the investor gets an exit.
- She says investors taking control of an entire company is quite common.
- She says seed stage needs patient capital, meaning someone willing to wait while many things are still unproven, and not an investor in scaling mode.
- She says investors who have done many seed rounds are calmer and more patient to talk to, and that founders should check which companies a fund actually backed at seed, because every fund says it does seed.

### Checking an investor before signing

- She tells founders to ask around the market about a fund and to cold call companies the fund has invested in and ask what the investor has been like.
- She says to look at how a fund exited its other companies. If past exits were amicable, that is in the fund's nature. If they were not, be wary.
- She says a founder must look willing to exit through a sale or an IPO, because an investor who cannot get an exit will not be interested.
- She says founders should not behave as if the one pitch room is the only option, and should search reviews and call people before signing.
- She says not to choose an institutional investor for the money alone. The question is what else they bring, such as credibility, industry insight or connections.
- Her rule is to give less equity when the investor brings only money, and to consider giving a little more when they bring something like government connections that the company needs.

### Contracts and the founder's employment terms

- She says every discussion with an investor must be put in writing.
- She says investors write their expected exit, in three or five years, into the contract.
- She says a founder needs a lawyer on their own side reading every document, and warns that lawyers can be desensitised and call harsh terms normal.
- She says investors add an employment contract for the founder that sets how long the founder must stay, when they can leave and when their own shares vest.
- Her own example is an investor who required six months notice from her while keeping the right to remove her in one month if she became disabled.
- She says the friendly investor steps back at contract stage and the lawyers do the hard negotiating, so founders should not be surprised or get emotional.
- She says calling the investor to intervene changes only a word or two in the contract.
- Her advice is to fight for terms of your own. If the investor wants you tied in for five years, make sure the salary and other terms you want are written in too.
- She says a pitch that mentions the founder leaving, or a co-founder taking over, will not work, because investors keep the founder in place until their exit is safe.

### Managing investors after the deal

- She recommends an arm's length relationship with regular touch points, because a founder who goes silent for four months will have investors asking for the books and an audit.
- She says a proactive quarterly update keeps investors at bay and makes them see the founder as disciplined.
- Her company first ran monthly investor updates, which took a week of preparation each month, and later moved to quarterly.
- She says to send the same quarterly report to every investor, including friends who invested as angels, because being professional makes people feel their money is safe.
- She says investor management becomes a big job, so co-founders should decide who handles investors and who handles operations, and still brief each other weekly or monthly.
- She says an employee can handle investor work only if deeply trusted, and that founders must still attend the big meetings because investors expect it.
- She says first pitch meetings are with analysts whose job is to filter, not with the fund's founders.
- Her company has four to five large angel investors and four institutional investors. It holds a quarterly investor meeting with a quorum of 50% attendance, plus the annual general meeting.
- She says each fund has one person who deals with the founder one on one, and that ad hoc calls with that person run alongside the structured meetings.
- She says a respectful relationship with the lead investor works well. Her lead investor takes calls late at night to help solve problems.

### The funding rounds

- She lays out the path as pre-seed, seed, Series A, then B and C, and says founders can skip steps.
- She says most private limited companies go up to Series B or C, and that after C it is natural to go for a sale or an IPO.
- She says every round comes with expectations of delivery, such as opening more cities, and nobody gives large money without them.
- She says institutions rarely fund pre-seed. It comes from the founder's own money and personal network, in tickets such as 5 or 10 lakh rupees.
- She advises not raising less than one crore rupees at pre-seed if possible, and says only a very frugal founder can manage on 40 to 50 lakh.
- She says to raise enough for an 18 month runway, worked out from actual costs such as rent, salaries, utilities, laptops and machinery.
- She says a founder who planned to raise one crore should not stop looking after getting 40 lakh, because a half-built product leaves the founder stuck.
- She says seed is the first round where an institution can come in, with tickets from about half a million dollars, which she puts at about 4 crore rupees, up to about 40 crore.
- She says at seed stage founders can email every seed investor they can find, because the worst outcome is no reply.
- She says Series A is the step into the big leagues and needs far more homework, and that B and C are similar to A.
- She says at Series D the pressure changes from revenue and scaling to regulation, because the company is near a sale or an IPO.
- She describes the whole path as a journey of 7 to 13 years and says founders can take their time.
- She says every investor wants a faster exit at their target valuation and the founder wants more time, so the timing is a negotiation.

### Working capital questions from investors

- She says an investor who asks about working capital is worried about cash flow: a company can look good on the books and have no money in the bank.
- Her example is selling through a marketplace, where the product has moved but the marketplace holds the money as the seller's receivable.
- She says the answer investors want is that the founder collects on time and will not tie up their money in working capital.
- She says taking a loan for working capital is a bad idea because the interest will kill the business.
- Her hospitals were paid on a three month cycle while she paid suppliers in one month. She pushed both sides to two months.
- She says the aim is to pay late and receive early, though the opposite is the usual situation for a small company.
- She says a founder can ask a supplier for 30 to 45 days of credit after about two years of paying reliably.
- She says many companies go bankrupt while profitable on paper because there is no cash in the bank.

### How much ownership to keep

- She says dilution compounds like interest and happens within five to six years, faster than founders expect.
- Her recommended minimums for the founder group are more than 75% at pre-seed, more than 50% at seed, above 40% at Series A, 26% at Series B and C, and 10% at Series D or IPO.
- She says these figures are her recommendations and not based on data, and that not every founder will hold them.
- She says 10% ownership lets a shareholder call an emergency general meeting.
- She says nobody takes a holder of less than 5% seriously, and that small angel tickets end up below 5% with no controlling stake.
- She says 26% lets a founder block big financial decisions, so the company cannot be sold without their approval.
- She says 51% makes the founder the largest shareholder, able to hold their ground on strategy even under investor pressure. "Don't lose your 51%."
- She says company secretaries and accountants talk about three thresholds, 26, 51 and 76, and that her figure of about 40% is not really one of them.
- She says ownership gives rights such as calling meetings and blocking, but on the board each director has one vote.
- She says two co-founders at 50 and 50 must decide at the start who has the veto, because a tie has to be broken somehow.
- She says a founder who built the idea and put in most of the money before a co-founder joined can offer 10%, 15% or 25% and not 50%.
- She says most rounds dilute between 15% and 30%, that institutional investors expect 25% and push for 30%, and that 10% a round will not happen.
- She says her own larger institutional investors each came in at about 10%, and two came together so she gave up 20% at once.
- She says giving less than 15% to 20% in an angel round is hard, and a 5 lakh cheque should still get a respectable share such as 1.5% or 2%.
- She tells every founder to build a dilution model in a spreadsheet before pitching and decide in advance what percentage they will accept and where they will walk away.
- She says that if every round in her model is set to 30%, the founder ends below 26%.

### Her dilution example

- In her model the founders put in 20 lakh and raise 80 lakh from angels for 20%, which values the company at 4 crore.
- At the next round an investor takes 25%, so the founders' 80% is multiplied by 75% and becomes 60%.
- She says the angel round is raised on an emotional pitch, on trust in the founder and the sector, and not on an analytical one.

### Debt and equity

- She says a founder with revenue should take debt and keep the equity.
- She says anyone lending money expects at least the interest of a safe bank fixed deposit, which she puts at 5% to 6% a year.
- She says banks ask for collateral, including the founder's personal assets, and start EMIs at once.
- She says venture debt gives a little more breathing space than a bank loan but costs the same in the end.
- Her own preference is equity because it gives time for revenue to stabilise. She accepts equipment financing because the lender can take the machine back.
- She says business communities such as the Marwadi and Gujarati communities fund ventures through their own networks, which works differently from institutional funding.

### Board seats

- She says power in a company lies first with shareholders and then with the board of directors.
- Her rule on board seats is not to offer them. Those who want one will ask.
- She says an angel putting 5 lakh into a one crore round does not get a board seat.
- She says the first big institutional investor will demand a board seat, and smaller ones will ask for a board observer.
- She describes a board observer as someone with the right to sit in on board meetings without a vote, and says it protects the investor and that few enforce it.
- She says the board votes on everything, including the founder's salary, strategy, expansion and senior hires above a salary level, so seats should be treated as precious.
- She advises against using a board seat to retain a person and suggests another contractual obligation in its place.
- She says a friendly or family director is only possible to appoint at incorporation, and only helps if that person will always vote with the founder.
- She says whenever a founder gives something away, they give control away.
- She says a founding entity that spins off a company can hold its own board seat in the spin-off, which gives the founder a second seat.
- She says dual share classes of the kind used in the US tend to come at IPO level.

### Control of the bank account

- She says not to give everyone access to the company's current account. One person should control it, two at most.
- She says most banks offer a maker and checker setup, where one person uploads a payment and another logs in to authorise it, and this can be requested when the account is opened.
- She says that if a joint account holder misuses funds, the bank will not act until the board passes a resolution to revoke that person's access.
- She says an emergency general meeting can be called at short notice, even 24 hours, when the company is at risk.
- She says revoking bank access later is very hard and damages the relationship, so a founder should think many times before offering it. "Before you give, think hundred times."
- She says someone should check the bank balance and every debit and credit once a month.

### Valuing an early company

- She says market capitalisation is the number of shares multiplied by the share price, and that for a private company the share price is a negotiation between a founder who wants it high and an investor who wants it low.
- She says the standard valuation methods do not work for a very small company and that multiples need revenue.
- She describes the revenue multiple method as annual revenue times the multiple at which a comparable company is valued.
- Her illustration is a snacks company pointing to Haldiram, supposing it is valued at ten times revenue, and asking for a similar multiple.
- She says most multiples on Shark Tank run from 3x to 10x.
- She says to argue from industry standards and not ask for more than them, because the founder will not get it.
- She says when comparable companies differ, take an average and do not pick only the most flattering one, because the multiple has to be justified.
- She calls industry averages a lighthouse: right in direction but not always right for a particular company.
- She says a company that is not profitable uses a revenue multiple and a profitable one uses an earnings multiple.
- She says discounted cash flow is the industry standard that large entities use, but it is not much use to a company at this stage.
- She describes liquidation value as what selling every asset would fetch, and as the minimum a company should be valued at.
- She says a pitch that goes well reaches the valuation and percentage discussion within a month or two, so founders must prepare for it before the pitch.
- She says founders who put in none of their own money will be told they have no skin in the game.
- She says to pay an adviser a salary or fee where possible and not part with shares.

## Examples and cases used

- Her own cancer care company: monthly investor updates cut back to quarterly, a six month notice clause in her employment terms, supplier and hospital credit cycles pushed to two months, and two institutional investors taking 10% each.
- HCG and KKR: she says that when a move like KKR buying HCG happens in her sector, her investors immediately call to ask about her plans, as an example of ad hoc investor calls.
- Haldiram: the comparable she uses to explain asking for a revenue multiple.
- Swiggy and Zomato: she calls them notorious for not paying people, in her point about a marketplace holding a seller's cash.
- Shark Tank: her reference point for 3x to 10x revenue multiples.
- Hindustan Unilever, Procter and Gamble, Reliance: named to explain market capitalisation.
- Radiation bunker for a hospital: equipment she would finance by EMI and not buy outright.

## Numbers given

- 3x to 5x in five years, the return she says a hands-off investor expects.
- 18 months, the runway she says each raise should cover.
- One crore rupees, the least she advises raising at pre-seed, with 40 to 50 lakh possible only for a very frugal founder.
- Half a million dollars, about 4 to 4.25 crore rupees by her conversion, up to about 40 crore, her range for a seed round.
- 75%, 50%, 40%, 26% and 10%, her recommended founder group ownership floors from pre-seed to IPO.
- 15% to 30%, her range for dilution in most rounds.
- 5% to 6% a year, the fixed deposit interest she says any lender will expect at least.
- 20 lakh from founders plus 80 lakh from angels for 20%, giving a 4 crore valuation, her worked example.
- 50% attendance, the quorum her company uses for its quarterly investor meeting.
