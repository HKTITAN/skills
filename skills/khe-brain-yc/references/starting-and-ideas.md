# Starting and ideas

This file answers whether to start a startup, when in a life to do it, and how to find and test an idea. Paul Graham, Michael Seibel, Jared Friedman, Dalton Caldwell, Tamanna Khemani and Kat Mañalac speak in it, with Paul Buchheit quoted inside one of Graham's essays. Read every line with its year and its audience: the advice to students differs between 2005, 2006, 2007 and 2014 and between speakers, and the lines are kept side by side without being reconciled.

## Should I start a startup at all?

- Seibel's essay says the reason to start is that one type of person only works at peak capacity when there is no predictable path, the odds of success are low and failure is their own responsibility. (Should I start a startup?, https://www.ycombinator.com/library/60-should-i-start-a-startup)
- Seibel gives people in tech, especially those at or applying to big companies, three questions: do you like being the underdog, do you seek the hard challenges most people avoid, and do you thrive on personal responsibility for success or failure. (Should I start a startup?, https://www.ycombinator.com/library/60-should-i-start-a-startup)
- Seibel says three yeses mean only that a startup is maybe for you, and that the many jobs at big companies cannot give this experience. (Should I start a startup?, https://www.ycombinator.com/library/60-should-i-start-a-startup)
- Seibel's essay promises no wealth, saying "the odds are against you becoming rich", and promises only that a tech startup is among the hardest things a person can pick. (Should I start a startup?, https://www.ycombinator.com/library/60-should-i-start-a-startup)
- Seibel says in a talk the library dates to 2018 that he first assumed anyone with the money, time and ability should start a startup, and that working at YC quickly showed him this was a bad idea. (Should I start a startup?, https://www.ycombinator.com/library/60-should-i-start-a-startup)
- Seibel sorts people into three groups: a few who can only work in a radically entrepreneurial job, whom he guesses may be only one percent of the population, a small group equally capable as founders or inside big companies, and by far the biggest group, who operate best within a large company. (Should I start a startup?, https://www.ycombinator.com/library/60-should-i-start-a-startup)
- Seibel's test for which group you are in is to ask where you apply effort with no outside push and where you delivered your best results, and he says anyone 20 to 25 has enough life experience to know. (Should I start a startup?, https://www.ycombinator.com/library/60-should-i-start-a-startup)
- Seibel says that without an irrational motivation to do startups the work will be frustrating and you will not understand the risk and reward. (Should I start a startup?, https://www.ycombinator.com/library/60-should-i-start-a-startup)
- Graham's 2007 essay reports that of the eight startups in Y Combinator's summer 2005 batch at least four looked like successes and three were probably dead, and that he expects the share of founders who wish they had taken a regular job to stay close to zero. (Why to Not Not Start a Startup, https://paulgraham.com/notnot.html)
- Graham's 2007 essay calls determination probably the single best predictor of success, and guesses the test is whether you are driven enough to work on your own projects. (Why to Not Not Start a Startup, https://paulgraham.com/notnot.html)
- Graham's 2007 essay will not advise anyone with a family to support to start a startup, and offers a consulting business turned gradually into a product business as a lower-risk route whose chances he calls very small. (Why to Not Not Start a Startup, https://paulgraham.com/notnot.html)
- Graham's 2007 essay accepts not wanting the commitment as a legitimate reason not to start, since a startup that succeeds will take at least three or four years, and adds that by his account a regular job will probably hold you as long. (Why to Not Not Start a Startup, https://paulgraham.com/notnot.html)
- Graham's 2007 essay says people who need to be told what to do should, in his words, probably not start a startup or even work at one, because until a company has about twelve people nobody should be directing anybody. (Why to Not Not Start a Startup, https://paulgraham.com/notnot.html)
- Graham's 2007 essay says that in America nobody will blame you for a failed startup if the effort was serious, and that the big-company managers he asked all preferred a failed founder to someone who spent the same time at a big company. (Why to Not Not Start a Startup, https://paulgraham.com/notnot.html)
- Graham's 2014 essay says a startup that succeeds takes over your life for several years at the least, maybe a decade, maybe the rest of your working life, so the opportunity cost is real. (Before the Startup, https://paulgraham.com/before.html)
- Graham's 2014 essay says you cannot tell in advance whether you are up to it: after 9 years of judging founders he found little correlation between their attitude on arrival and how their companies did. (Before the Startup, https://paulgraham.com/before.html)
- Graham's 2014 essay says someone absolutely terrified of starting a startup should, as he puts it, probably not do it, and someone merely unsure can only find out by trying, though he tells the college students he is addressing not to try yet. (Before the Startup, https://paulgraham.com/before.html)
- Khemani and Mañalac's guide for students lists among its reasons not to start: you are still in school, you have no co-founder, you are not ready to work for as long as a decade, you need structure, or you hate uncertainty. (Startups for students, https://www.ycombinator.com/library/D0-startups-for-students)

## Should I start as a student or straight out of college?

- Graham's 2005 essay describes funding founders younger than most investors would back as an experiment to find the lower bound on founder age, reasoning that some undergraduates are as capable as most grad students. (Why Smart People Have Bad Ideas, https://paulgraham.com/bronze.html)
- Graham's 2006 essay says Y Combinator at first encouraged people to start while still in college, and had since decided the line should be to wait until graduation. (A Student's Guide to Startups, https://paulgraham.com/mit.html)
- Graham's 2006 essay gives the reason: a startup begun in school has a built-in escape hatch, because if it goes nowhere you still read as a student and nobody regards you as a failure. (A Student's Guide to Startups, https://paulgraham.com/mit.html)
- Graham's 2006 essay states the policy as funding only the undergraduates who cannot be talked out of it, and says anyone not certain should wait. (A Student's Guide to Startups, https://paulgraham.com/mit.html)
- Graham's 2006 essay puts the sweet spot for founders at what he calls probably the mid-twenties, going by whose applications he was most excited to see. (A Student's Guide to Startups, https://paulgraham.com/mit.html)
- Graham's 2006 essay says what goes wrong with young founders is that they build things resembling class projects, which lack an iterative definition of a real problem and lack intensity. (A Student's Guide to Startups, https://paulgraham.com/mit.html)
- Graham's 2006 essay says a founder of 24 beats one of 20 mainly by knowing what he is trying to avoid, having learned from working how money and work are tied together. (A Student's Guide to Startups, https://paulgraham.com/mit.html)
- Graham's 2007 essay withdraws his earlier advice that founders be at least 23 and work at another company for a few years first, crediting the startups he had funded with changing his mind. (Why to Not Not Start a Startup, https://paulgraham.com/notnot.html)
- Graham's 2007 essay still calls 23 a better age than 21, but says the cure for being too inexperienced is to start a startup, since a normal job may turn you into "a tame animal". (Why to Not Not Start a Startup, https://paulgraham.com/notnot.html)
- Graham's 2007 essay advises people to go ahead and start right out of college, saying they will by his own estimate probably fail and that failure still gets them to the goal faster than a job. (Why to Not Not Start a Startup, https://paulgraham.com/notnot.html)
- Graham's 2007 essay also says people a year or two out in the world make better founders than those straight from college, because they know how much jobs are worth avoiding. (Why to Not Not Start a Startup, https://paulgraham.com/notnot.html)
- Graham's 2014 essay, written for college students, answers plainly that you should not start a startup in college, because a real startup takes over your life and you stop being a student. (Before the Startup, https://paulgraham.com/before.html)
- Graham's 2014 essay says age 20 is not the optimal time, that most people should still be searching breadth-first at 20, and that waiting costs nothing because you are more likely to succeed later. (Before the Startup, https://paulgraham.com/before.html)
- Graham's 2014 essay allows one case he calls unlikely: if at 20 a side project takes off the way Facebook did, running with it may be reasonable. (Before the Startup, https://paulgraham.com/before.html)
- Graham's 2014 essay says the early 20s allow things you cannot do as well before or after, such as plunging into projects on a whim and travelling cheaply with no deadline, and that for ambitious people this exploration can be extremely valuable. (Before the Startup, https://paulgraham.com/before.html)
- Seibel's letter to college students rejects the idea that your 20s are for finding yourself and calls them your most valuable working years, with few obligations and a very low burn rate. (A letter to college students, https://www.ycombinator.com/library/4d-a-letter-to-college-students)
- Seibel's letter calls ages 22 to 32 your best startup years and says every year not spent doing a startup loses 10% of that prime time. (A letter to college students, https://www.ycombinator.com/library/4d-a-letter-to-college-students)
- Khemani and Mañalac write that they tell most students to finish their degree, and that dropping out may be worth considering if the startup is all you can think about and takes all your free time. (Startups for students, https://www.ycombinator.com/library/D0-startups-for-students)

## What should I do in college if I want to start later?

- Graham's 2006 essay is sceptical of classes and books about startups and says the way to learn is to watch startups in action, preferably by working at one. (A Student's Guide to Startups, https://paulgraham.com/mit.html)
- Graham's 2006 essay says the best place to work before starting your own is, in his estimate, probably a startup, because it is the right experience and will be over quickly whichever way it ends. (A Student's Guide to Startups, https://paulgraham.com/mit.html)
- Graham's 2006 essay tells anyone planning a startup to write none of its code while still employed, because investors or acquirers will ask whether any of the code legally belongs to someone else. (A Student's Guide to Startups, https://paulgraham.com/mit.html)
- Graham's 2012 essay says a computer science student who wants to start a startup is better off taking a class in something like genetics, or working at a biotech company, than taking a class on entrepreneurship, because problems show up where domains meet. (How to Get Startup Ideas, https://paulgraham.com/startupideas.html)
- Graham's 2014 essay says the best college for a future founder is the classic one of education for its own sake, learning powerful things, and not a vocational version built around entrepreneurship. (Before the Startup, https://paulgraham.com/before.html)
- Graham's 2014 essay says an undergraduate who knew all about the mechanics of startups would alarm him, because young founders tend to go through the motions, which he names "playing house", and neglect making something people want. (Before the Startup, https://paulgraham.com/before.html)
- Graham's 2005 essay says people in their early twenties have spent 15 to 20 years on problems set by others, and concludes: "They're good at solving problems, but bad at choosing them." (Why Smart People Have Bad Ideas, https://paulgraham.com/bronze.html)
- Seibel's letter tells college students that people who cannot start a startup in their late 20s and 30s are often blocked by an earlier decision: not learning to code, not meeting anyone in college who could, raising expenses without saving, or putting down roots in a city that is not startup friendly. (A letter to college students, https://www.ycombinator.com/library/4d-a-letter-to-college-students)
- Seibel tells students to learn to code without needing to major in computer science, and calls not knowing how the number one barrier to starting a tech startup. (A letter to college students, https://www.ycombinator.com/library/4d-a-letter-to-college-students)
- Seibel warns students that debt is pushing more graduates into big companies, where added expenses such as a car and a nice apartment price them out of starting a company. (A letter to college students, https://www.ycombinator.com/library/4d-a-letter-to-college-students)
- Khemani and Mañalac recommend that a student intern at a company past its Series A, where there is more structure and capacity for mentorship, unless the student is very self-directed or wants to learn from one particular founder. (Startups for students, https://www.ycombinator.com/library/D0-startups-for-students)

## Founder, executive or employee: which path?

- Seibel lays out the three paths he has met most in tech careers, founder, executive and employee, and says he is surprised how often people focus on only one without considering the others. (Three paths in the tech industry: founder, executive, or employee, https://www.ycombinator.com/library/4h-three-paths-in-the-tech-industry-founder-executive-or-employee)
- Seibel attaches no value judgment to the three paths, having seen friends lead successful and fulfilled lives on each, and admits that as a YC partner and former founder he is guilty of pushing his own. (Three paths in the tech industry: founder, executive, or employee, https://www.ycombinator.com/library/4h-three-paths-in-the-tech-industry-founder-executive-or-employee)
- Seibel lists the costs of the founder path: great stress even in success, personal earnings that he says probably will not be maximized, hurdles of money, skills and location to get started, and a commitment of a decade or more for large-scale success. (Three paths in the tech industry: founder, executive, or employee, https://www.ycombinator.com/library/4h-three-paths-in-the-tech-industry-founder-executive-or-employee)
- Seibel says a would-be founder's first goal is to gather the prerequisites: teammates with the technical skill to build the MVP, a financial plan that gives 6 to 12 months to work on the idea, a problem the team is passionate about, and least important but still required, an idea for solving it. (Three paths in the tech industry: founder, executive, or employee, https://www.ycombinator.com/library/4h-three-paths-in-the-tech-industry-founder-executive-or-employee)
- Seibel calls hustling around a missing prerequisite a popular mistake: a team without the technical skill to build its MVP should not use a dev shop and should befriend people with those skills and convince them to join. (Three paths in the tech industry: founder, executive, or employee, https://www.ycombinator.com/library/4h-three-paths-in-the-tech-industry-founder-executive-or-employee)
- Seibel's advice when hard barriers stop you from starting is to move to a tech hub, preferably the Bay Area, and work at a tech company until you have saved the money, met the teammates or found the problem. (Three paths in the tech industry: founder, executive, or employee, https://www.ycombinator.com/library/4h-three-paths-in-the-tech-industry-founder-executive-or-employee)
- Seibel says experience is not one of the prerequisites and is massively over-valued by people thinking about starting, because most of what you need to know about problem, customer and solution is learned after you start. (Three paths in the tech industry: founder, executive, or employee, https://www.ycombinator.com/library/4h-three-paths-in-the-tech-industry-founder-executive-or-employee)
- Seibel says of the executive path that producing results is not necessarily how you move up, internal politics usually count as much or more, and it needs either picking a company that will keep growing or moving diagonally between established name-brand companies. (Three paths in the tech industry: founder, executive, or employee, https://www.ycombinator.com/library/4h-three-paths-in-the-tech-industry-founder-executive-or-employee)
- Seibel says of the employee path that it offers stable income, more work with fewer meetings and often more time for friends and family, while you often do not control what you work on and it is harder to become very wealthy. (Three paths in the tech industry: founder, executive, or employee, https://www.ycombinator.com/library/4h-three-paths-in-the-tech-industry-founder-executive-or-employee)
- Seibel's essay warns that time on a large big-company salary tends to raise a person's expenses and so cut the odds that they ever start a startup, even if they mean to one day. (Should I start a startup?, https://www.ycombinator.com/library/60-should-i-start-a-startup)

## Where do startup ideas come from?

- Graham's 2012 essay says the way to get startup ideas is to look for problems, preferably ones you have yourself, and not to try to think of startup ideas. (How to Get Startup Ideas, https://paulgraham.com/startupideas.html)
- Graham's 2012 essay says the very best ideas tend to share three things: the founders want it themselves, can build it themselves, and few others realize it is worth doing. (How to Get Startup Ideas, https://paulgraham.com/startupideas.html)
- Graham's 2012 essay compresses the method into "Live in the future, then build what's missing", taking the first half from Paul Buchheit's phrase for people at the leading edge of a fast-changing field. (How to Get Startup Ideas, https://paulgraham.com/startupideas.html)
- Graham's 2012 essay says to turn off every filter except what is missing, particularly the question of whether it could be a big company, which he says there is plenty of time to ask later. (How to Get Startup Ideas, https://paulgraham.com/startupideas.html)
- For founders who need an idea now, Graham's 2012 essay says to look in areas where you have expertise, to start from things you need, and to recall anything at a previous job that people said they would buy at once if someone made it. (How to Get Startup Ideas, https://paulgraham.com/startupideas.html)
- Graham's 2012 essay recommends that people who need a new idea seek out ideas that are unsexy or involve schleps, and not try to start Twitter, because such ideas are too rare to find by looking. (How to Get Startup Ideas, https://paulgraham.com/startupideas.html)
- Paul Buchheit, quoted in a note to Graham's 2012 essay, tells companies with bad ideas to go and sell the product before building it, because they often come back with a real idea found while trying to sell the bad one. (How to Get Startup Ideas, https://paulgraham.com/startupideas.html)
- Graham's 2007 essay gives a brief recipe: find something missing in your own life and supply it however specific to you it seems, since a narrow genuine need is a better start than a broad hypothetical one. (Why to Not Not Start a Startup, https://paulgraham.com/notnot.html)
- Graham's 2014 essay says the best startups nearly always need to begin as side projects, since a great idea is so far from the norm that a mind deliberately hunting for company ideas would throw it out. (Before the Startup, https://paulgraham.com/before.html)
- Graham's 2014 essay gives college students three steps for becoming someone ideas come to: learn a lot about things that matter, work on problems that interest you, and do it with people you like and respect. (Before the Startup, https://paulgraham.com/before.html)
- Friedman says in a talk the library dates to 2022 that noticing ideas organically beats sitting down to think of them, and that for someone planning to start far in the future it helps to become expert in something valuable through a job at the forefront of a field, with a job at an existing startup being particularly helpful. (Jared Friedman: How to get startup ideas, https://www.ycombinator.com/library/8g-how-to-get-startup-ideas)
- Friedman ranks seven recipes for generating ideas from best to worst, and puts first starting from what your team is especially good at, a recipe he says about half of the most successful YC-funded companies trace back to. (Jared Friedman: How to get startup ideas, https://www.ycombinator.com/library/8g-how-to-get-startup-ideas)
- Friedman's exercise for the first recipe is to go through every company you have worked at, internships included, and ask what you learned there that others do not know, what seemed broken, and what was built in-house that other companies might need. (Jared Friedman: How to get startup ideas, https://www.ycombinator.com/library/8g-how-to-get-startup-ideas)
- Friedman's other six recipes, in his order from better to worse: things you wish someone would build for you, what you would work on for 10 years even without success, recent changes in the world, variants of recently successful companies, asking people for problems they want solved, and industries that seem broken. (Jared Friedman: How to get startup ideas, https://www.ycombinator.com/library/8g-how-to-get-startup-ideas)

## How do I judge an idea?

- Graham's 2012 essay says a startup can build something many people want a little or something a few people want a lot, and tells founders to choose the second. (How to Get Startup Ideas, https://paulgraham.com/startupideas.html)
- Graham's 2012 test for an idea: ask who wants this so much right now that they will use a crude first version from a two-person startup they have never heard of, and treat no answer as a sign the idea is, in his words, probably bad. (How to Get Startup Ideas, https://paulgraham.com/startupideas.html)
- Graham's 2012 essay adds that deep narrow demand is not enough alone: Facebook was a good idea because its small first market had a fast path out, and he says such paths are hard to see in advance. (How to Get Startup Ideas, https://paulgraham.com/startupideas.html)
- Graham's 2012 essay says worrying that you are late is a sign of a good idea, that startups are so rarely killed by competitors the risk can almost be discounted, and that only a competitor with lock-in is a reason to drop it. (How to Get Startup Ideas, https://paulgraham.com/startupideas.html)
- Graham's 2012 essay says a crowded market is fine if you have a thesis about what everyone else in it overlooks, and that the thesis must be more precise than making a version that does not suck. (How to Get Startup Ideas, https://paulgraham.com/startupideas.html)
- Graham's 2005 essay says to re-examine an idea coldly after plunging in and ask whether people will pay for it, and whether of all the things you could make it is what they would pay most for. (Why Smart People Have Bad Ideas, https://paulgraham.com/bronze.html)
- Graham's 2007 essay tells founders to make something great without worrying too much about making money, and to seek out ideas that would be popular but seem hard to make money from. (Why to Not Not Start a Startup, https://paulgraham.com/notnot.html)
- Graham's 2007 essay says lacking a good idea is in one sense no problem because most startups change theirs, and guesses that in the average Y Combinator startup 70% of the idea is new after the first three months. (Why to Not Not Start a Startup, https://paulgraham.com/notnot.html)
- Friedman says not to wait for a brilliant idea and not to jump into the first one: spend at least a couple of weeks deciding, and treat the initial idea as a good starting point that will morph. (Jared Friedman: How to get startup ideas, https://www.ycombinator.com/library/8g-how-to-get-startup-ideas)
- Friedman scores an idea by averaging four criteria, a formula he credits to his colleague Dalton and adjusted: how big the idea is, founder/market fit, how sure you are that you are solving a big problem, and whether you have a new important insight. (Jared Friedman: How to get startup ideas, https://www.ycombinator.com/library/8g-how-to-get-startup-ideas)
- Friedman names four filters that make founders reject their best ideas unconsciously: the idea seems hard to get started, the space is boring, the idea sounds too ambitious, or competitors already exist. (Jared Friedman: How to get startup ideas, https://www.ycombinator.com/library/8g-how-to-get-startup-ideas)
- Friedman says to err toward spaces with existing competitors, because founders who enter empty spaces usually find nobody wants the product, and the ideal is a market where you have noticed something every competitor missed. (Jared Friedman: How to get startup ideas, https://www.ycombinator.com/library/8g-how-to-get-startup-ideas)
- Khemani and Mañalac tell students to focus on what people are actually willing to spend time or money on, not just on what they say they want. (Startups for students, https://www.ycombinator.com/library/D0-startups-for-students)

## Which ideas look good and are traps?

- Graham's 2012 essay calls solving problems no one has by far the most common startup mistake, and says he made it himself in 1995, spending 6 months on a company to put art galleries online. (How to Get Startup Ideas, https://paulgraham.com/startupideas.html)
- Graham's 2012 essay says trying to think up ideas yields what he calls "sitcom" ideas such as a social network for pet owners: friends say they could maybe see using it, and that reaction summed over everyone gives zero users. (How to Get Startup Ideas, https://paulgraham.com/startupideas.html)
- Graham's 2005 essay names the biggest cause of bad ideas the still life effect: you plunge into a random idea and at each point feel too much time has gone in to change, and his fix is to accept that time invested does not make an idea good. (Why Smart People Have Bad Ideas, https://paulgraham.com/bronze.html)
- Graham's 2005 essay warns against choosing an idea because it seems cool, since work people like pays badly by supply and demand, and says the aim should be to make money and maybe be cool. (Why Smart People Have Bad Ideas, https://paulgraham.com/bronze.html)
- Graham's 2005 essay says fear of funded competitors made him choose an impoverished market, and that VC-backed startups turned out not to be fearsome and e-commerce surprisingly easy to compete in. (Why Smart People Have Bad Ideas, https://paulgraham.com/bronze.html)
- Friedman warns against starting with a solution, such as an app that is Uber for plumbers, which he labels a "Solution In Search of a Problem", because founders who begin there usually never find the problem. (Jared Friedman: How to get startup ideas, https://www.ycombinator.com/library/8g-how-to-get-startup-ideas)
- Caldwell and Seibel, in a talk the library dates to 2024, define a tarpit idea as one of the idea spaces that keep attracting many applicants and never work. (Tarpit Ideas: The Sequel, https://www.ycombinator.com/library/LH-tarpit-ideas-the-sequel)
- Caldwell and Seibel say a tarpit is not just a bad idea: tarpits are a smallish set, and an idea counts only if other founders come up with it constantly. (Tarpit Ideas: The Sequel, https://www.ycombinator.com/library/LH-tarpit-ideas-the-sequel)
- Caldwell and Seibel say tarpit ideas draw praise from friends where most startup ideas get an honest no, using an app for deciding which restaurant to go to as the example. (Tarpit Ideas: The Sequel, https://www.ycombinator.com/library/LH-tarpit-ideas-the-sequel)
- Caldwell and Seibel give another mark of a tarpit: the idea has been tried many times, many people failed, and nothing has changed since. (Tarpit Ideas: The Sequel, https://www.ycombinator.com/library/LH-tarpit-ideas-the-sequel)
- Caldwell and Seibel say the list is not static: since LLMs arrived they are open to classic tarpit ideas no longer being tarpits, provided the founder can explain why the new technology makes the thing achievable now. (Tarpit Ideas: The Sequel, https://www.ycombinator.com/library/LH-tarpit-ideas-the-sequel)
- Caldwell and Seibel name one common kind of tarpit as a belief that the world should work differently, where everyone has to adopt the scheme at once, such as an app for coordinating plans with friends. (Tarpit Ideas: The Sequel, https://www.ycombinator.com/library/LH-tarpit-ideas-the-sequel)
- Caldwell and Seibel name the other kind as some form of arbitrage or getting rich very quickly, where a founder hopes a moment in time lets them skip the long work of a startup. (Tarpit Ideas: The Sequel, https://www.ycombinator.com/library/LH-tarpit-ideas-the-sequel)
- Caldwell and Seibel say that when founders ask whether their idea is a tarpit, the answer is to ask whether they have talked to any users, since the advice in their videos is to talk to users and get a customer. (Tarpit Ideas: The Sequel, https://www.ycombinator.com/library/LH-tarpit-ideas-the-sequel)

## What if the idea is research I did at a university?

- Friedman writes, for people who did scientific research at a university, that the company has little chance unless someone who worked on the original research leaves their university role to start it. (How to spin your scientific research out of a university and into a startup, https://www.ycombinator.com/library/8f-how-to-spin-your-scientific-research-out-of-a-university-and-into-a-startup)
- Friedman says scientists should not stay in academia and recruit a CEO: the best CEO at this stage is one of the people who did the research, since great CEOs rarely take an unfunded idea-stage company and the inventors' domain knowledge is worth more than general business skill. (How to spin your scientific research out of a university and into a startup, https://www.ycombinator.com/library/8f-how-to-spin-your-scientific-research-out-of-a-university-and-into-a-startup)
- Friedman says a scientific founding team needs nobody with business experience, noting that most scientists Y Combinator funds have none. (How to spin your scientific research out of a university and into a startup, https://www.ycombinator.com/library/8f-how-to-spin-your-scientific-research-out-of-a-university-and-into-a-startup)
- Friedman, in an undated post, says not to wait for venture money before leaving the university: founders typically work full-time for a year or more before the company is ready for a multi-million dollar round from VCs, and those who will not quit first get stuck in a catch-22. (How to spin your scientific research out of a university and into a startup, https://www.ycombinator.com/library/8f-how-to-spin-your-scientific-research-out-of-a-university-and-into-a-startup)
- Friedman says early work on a new technology goes faster inside the university, that this later flips, and that waiting too long to leave is by far the more common mistake than leaving too early.
