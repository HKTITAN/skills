# Product metrics and the GAME framework

This is the first half of a class by a faculty instructor who is not named in the recording. It follows an earlier session on prototypes and MVPs and covers how to choose and build metrics, with a short exercise where founders applied the framework to one feature of their own product. The class then broke for ten minutes before a second half on go-to-market, which is a separate note.

## What was said

### Why the choice of metric matters

- The instructor says metrics are the instruments that drive a company towards success or failure, and measuring the wrong metric can make a company fail.
- The instructor says picking a metric can change the culture of a company completely.
- The instructor says a company chasing one correct metric can still get into trouble if it ignores another, as when an airline optimised for full flights through overbooking and did not attend to customer service.
- The instructor says founders are good at strategy and big picture talk, but people building companies have to talk in numbers.

### What a metric is

- The instructor defines a metric as a collectible, quantifiable measure that tracks a level of performance, of one aspect of the product, of the whole product, or of the business.
- The instructor says a metric only counts if it measures something that adds to what the customer cares about and what the product enables.
- The instructor says the product or service should be instrumented to collect data while usage is happening, and calls running a survey afterwards an archaic way to collect it.
- The instructor says measuring after the fact gives only lagging indicators.
- The instructor says revenue is always a lagging indicator, while how often someone reaches the checkout page of a website is a leading indicator.
- The instructor says data collected during use gives leading indicators, which let a company change what it does with customers in time to hit the lagging indicator.
- The instructor compares a leading indicator to a doctor taking a patient's temperature, which signals how the body will perform in the coming hours.
- The instructor says granular metrics are better than aggregate ones, because an aggregate like revenue cannot be attributed to a cause, while aspect level measures can be tied to a change in revenue.
- The instructor says a metric must capture performance over time: 100 users today says little, 100 users consistently over the last month is a good indicator.

### The GAME framework

- The instructor says GAME stands for Goals, Actions, Metrics and Evaluations, and describes it as a bottom-up framework built on user actions.
- The instructor says GAME suits the MVP phase, when a company should measure actions and not a vision.
- The instructor says GAME starts from the goal of the user, the job the user is trying to get done, while the North Star framework starts from the goal of the business.
- The instructor says the second step is to list the actions the user must take in the product to reach that goal, and the third is to decide what to measure about those actions and how.
- The instructor says that at this stage the company is measuring the user's activities and not the goal of the product.
- The instructor says evaluation means repeatedly asking whether a measure rightly represents the health of the business.
- The instructor's test for a measure such as hours spent is whether more is better or less is better: a productivity tool does not want more hours spent, a social media platform does.
- The instructor pictures the product as a box with user inputs of need, time and money going in and outcomes coming out, with the user's actions in between being the thing to measure.
- The instructor says actions can be measured by how often, how deeply and how regularly they are done, and how long the user took to reach value.
- The instructor says the North Star framework works top down: state the company's goal, decide which numbers show progress, then decide which activities move those numbers.
- The instructor says a company needs both frameworks and that they converge, because the only goals are the user's and the company's.
- The instructor says to apply the framework to one product feature at a time, because working at the aggregate level causes confusion.
- The instructor says every type of user on a platform needs its own actions and measures, for example sellers and buyers separately, which are then aggregated at platform level.

### Actions: acquisition and activation

- The instructor groups user actions under the AARM framework, starting with acquisition, where the user discovers the product and becomes a user.
- The instructor defines an active user as someone who has derived value from the product at least once in a given time frame.
- The instructor says the definition of an active user is specific to each product, because usage cycles differ. A quarterly reporting tool cannot be judged on daily use.
- The instructor says that for a product meant to be used at some frequency, the company has to choose a heuristic for the right frequency.
- The instructor says a user who is not active and engaged cannot be retained.
- The instructor's pressure cooker example separates the two: buying the cooker is acquisition, and cooking with it for the first time is activation.
- The instructor says a fully disconnected product cannot measure activation, only retention or repeat purchase.
- The instructor says founders are pushed towards technology enabled products because such products can be measured at every point.
- The instructor says only the founder knows the point at which a user gets value from the product. In a community product, getting an answer to a question is value and logging in is not.

### Actions: retention, engagement and monetisation

- The instructor says retention is arguably more important than anything else, and becomes really difficult when the buyer and the user are different people.
- The instructor's examples of buyer and user being different are parents paying for children and any B2B product.
- The instructor says that when a product gives different value to different parties, all of them must be kept happy or retention will not happen.
- The instructor's SaaS example is a company email service, which is renewed only if employees use it and it also meets the buyer's needs on cost, security and privacy.
- The instructor says engagement leads to retention.
- The instructor says payment from a user can be money, time, data or a referral.
- The instructor says value creation and value capture can happen at different points: a Google search user pays in data, and Google captures the value through ads.
- The instructor says a company must decide which user action triggers charging, as when iCloud starts charging after the free storage is used up.
- The instructor argues that for a purchase, value is created at the point of the decision to buy and not at the sale, which is why companies take money to lock in a price and refund it if unused.

### Ways to measure an action

- The instructor says a direct metric counts an action the user actually took, such as a page browse, an add to cart, a purchase, a swipe or a like.
- The instructor says a proxy metric assumes an action took place. A video view is a proxy, counted when the frame stays on screen for more than a set number of seconds.
- The instructor says individual metrics count single actions, while an aggregate combines them, as when engagement is defined as two swipes, one like and one subscribe.
- The instructor says a magnitude is a raw count such as 500 interactions today, while a ratio is a count per user or per time period.
- The instructor says an intrinsic measure is read straight from one number, such as logins, paying customers or conversion rate.
- The instructor says a heuristic measure combines several intrinsic measures by a formula, such as paying customers who were active in the last 30 days.
- The instructor says that for B2B SaaS on monthly subscription, paying customers active in the last 30 days is the best predictor of how many will renew.
- The instructor says engagement is a heuristic built from many other metrics, and that heuristics are usually aggregates but not all aggregates are heuristics.

### B2B, B2C and the B2B2C label

- The instructor says there is nothing called B2B2C: a company either sells to a consumer or to an institution, and one that sells to both has two business models.
- The instructor says a company with no access to the end consumer is not selling to that consumer, and what gets called B2B2C is a channel.
- The instructor says capturing usage data from the people who use a product is telemetry and not selling to them.
- The instructor says a company selling to an employer whose employees are the users runs a B2B sales motion but must still solve for the end user.

### Booked and recognised revenue

- The instructor says large cloud companies report booked revenue alongside realised revenue to show confidence in the business.
- The instructor's example is a six year, 30 million dollar cloud deal that pays 5 million dollars a year: recognised revenue is 5 million each year and booked revenue is 30 million.
- The instructor says revenue cannot be shown on the books until the order is fulfilled, the goods are issued and the invoice is paid.

### Principles over tools

- The instructor says principles matter more than tools, because tools are specific to a business and keep changing.
- The instructor says the aim of the class is to lay out the full menu of principles and leave each founder to decide which apply to their product.

## Examples and cases used

- Wells Fargo: staff were measured on the number of accounts created per executive, so employees created fake accounts.
- Facebook: with engagement as the top metric, the company found that sad people spend longer on social media and showed them unhappy content, which the instructor calls an ethical problem.
- Airbnb: the retention metric is yearly booked nights per guest and weekly booked nights per host, the same action at different frequencies for two personas.
- Google search: the user pays in data and the value is captured in ads.
- Instagram: a view is a proxy metric based on time on screen, while a swipe or a like is a direct metric.
- Refrigerators and water filters with sensors: connected products let a maker know whether the product is in use.
- Maruti 800: in the past a car maker had no way to know what a buyer did with the car.
- A premium pilgrimage service: the senior citizens who travel are the users, and their children abroad are the paying customers and want frequent updates.

## Numbers given

- 500 interactions on a website in a day, the instructor's example of a magnitude metric.
- 30 days, the window in the instructor's heuristic of paying customers who were recently active.
- 30 million dollars over six years at 5 million dollars a year, the instructor's made-up cloud deal for booked against recognised revenue.
