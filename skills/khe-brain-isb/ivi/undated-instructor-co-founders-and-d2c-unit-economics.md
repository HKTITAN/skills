# Co-founder questions and D2C unit economics, investor bootcamp second day

An investor, not named in the recording, ran what he refers to as the second day of a session with the cohort. The readable parts are a question and answer stretch on co-founders and solo founders, and a line by line walkthrough of the unit economics of a direct to consumer product using a founder in the room's sheet. The file also carries a classmate's written notes, kept in their own section below. The founder's own figures are left out.

## What was said

### Co-founders and solo founders

- The speaker says investors do not treat a person holding a very small stake as a co-founder. A founder in the room put the line at under 10%.
- He says there is no settled research on solo founders, and that his fund debates in every deal whether the company can be run by one founder.
- He says an equity split should reflect unique contributions, skills and commitment, and that the plain question is what the other person brings to the table.
- He says distrust between founders is very expensive.

### Unit economics of a D2C product

- He starts the sheet from the listed price, the per unit price the customer would pay if no discount were given.
- He says every cost incurred for each unit, including labour, belongs in cost of goods sold.
- He takes logistics, the cost of sending the product to the customer's house, off gross margin to reach contribution margin 1.
- He says logistics "is a killer at every stage" in most D2C businesses because it does not get optimised quickly.
- He says gross margin has to be high for any business, not only D2C, because the costs below it will kill you. His other example is an HR tech business.
- He places two costs between contribution margin 1 and contribution margin 2: the platform fee paid to Shopify for enabling the sale, and the payment gateway fee paid to Razorpay.
- He says Shopify has three plans that charge three different percentages on a transaction.
- He says some companies also allocate brand spending per unit at this level, and that whether to do so is the founder's call.
- He says investors like to see unit economics with a clear path to margin expansion over the next year, built on simple levers.

### Selling through a marketplace

- He says the same sheet changes completely on a marketplace, where the platform fee goes up sharply because that is what the platform charges to onboard you easily.
- He says a marketplace charges under separate heads, such as a platform fee for being listed and a branding fee for showing up at the top.
- He says volume projections on a marketplace have to be worked out with the platform's category manager.

### Tracking by channel

- He says the economics should be laid out separately for each channel, such as your own site, your app and each marketplace.
- He says a founder should have this analysis done at least once a week to understand what is working.
- He says a founder should be on top of these numbers for the business itself and not just for investors.

### From the written class notes in the file

- The notes list five common mistakes with investors: telling them other VCs are chasing you, saying you do not need cash, using jargon, claiming no competition, and reaching out again after the fund has already passed.
- The notes record the advice to have difficult conversations with a co-founder as early as possible.
- The notes record that you need to know your co-founder well.
- The notes record the advice to prepare a guiding document between founders early on.

## Examples and cases used

- Shopify: the platform fee on an own-website sale, charged as a percentage that depends on the plan.
- Razorpay: the payment gateway fee on an own-website sale, which he says can be negotiated down as the business grows.

- 1.5% to 1.2%: what he says Razorpay's executives bring the fee down to, depending on how big the business is.
