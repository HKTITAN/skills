# Negotiation, dispute resolution and influence, course instructor with guest Amit Khanna

This was the last of six sessions of the negotiation course. It opened with a debrief of a dispute simulation the class had just played, then Amit Khanna, an ISB alumnus who negotiates deals for a living and invests in startups, joined by video call with a live auction case and took questions. After a break the instructor taught dispute resolution, threats, low power, multi-party negotiation, and the biases and influence principles she had left for the end. The instructor is not named in full in the recording.

## What was said

### Guest: preparing for an auction

- Amit Khanna says information and preparation give you the edge, and that the more data you gather before a negotiation starts, the better the outcome.
- Amit Khanna says the ratio of preparation time to negotiation time he practises is 3 to 1: for two hours in the room, spend at least six hours preparing.
- His live case was an auction for a restaurant space with one other known bidder. His team obtained the competitor's last two years of financials and its menus, and built a financial model of the competitor to estimate how high it could bid.
- Amit Khanna says his team always has an observer in a negotiation whose only job is to watch and take notes, including every question the other side asks.
- In the auction the observer also timed the gap between the other side's bids. The first bids came within about 10 seconds and later ones took three to four minutes, which his team read as the other side nearing its limit. He adds that this reading is not always true.
- Amit Khanna says the questions a counterparty asks tell you what matters to them, and what they do not ask about tells you what does not.
- His team did not meet the technical qualification for the auction. Instead of assuming rejection they asked the landlord whether they could bid anyway, and the answer was yes. His rule from this is "Don't assume. Ask."
- Amit Khanna says you must fix your walk-away price before an auction and have the discipline to leave at it, because in the room it is easy to say "one more bid". He compares it to setting a budget before entering a casino.
- He says he had assumed the auction was purely financial, but it was not. The space was tied to a 300-year-old monument, so his team built its branding and story around that.
- He says the landlord was a government body that had to follow procedure, so his team stayed available in case the winning bid could not go through.
- Amit Khanna says a negotiation does not end when the formal exchange ends. After the auction his team went back to the other bidder and proposed working together.
- He tells founders that when a venture capitalist says no, they should keep sending company updates.

### Guest: interests, respect and reputation

- Amit Khanna says the outcome he finds most satisfying is one where the parties together increase the size of the pie and share it, not one where a single side takes more.
- He says a negotiation is not a one-off transaction. The investor you negotiate with stays on your board afterwards, and the next time you raise capital they will be asked about you.
- Amit Khanna says the things to protect in any negotiation are reputation, trust and respect, ahead of the financial terms, because the investor ecosystem is small and people talk.
- He suggests treating the problem as a third entity sitting on the table between the two sides, so that both work on it together and stop being adversaries.
- Amit Khanna says preparation includes knowing who the counterpart is, including their country and culture. If you want something from a Japanese counterpart, his example, you learn how they negotiate.
- He says that if you feel you have reached a breaking point with the person across the table, ask for a different person or send someone else from your side, because sometimes it is only a personality clash.
- He says every time a negotiation fails you should sit down and reflect on what you learned from it.
- Asked how much is preparation and how much instinct, Amit Khanna says a lot of it is preparation. His team rehearses the exchange in advance: what the other side will ask, and what they will answer.

### Guest: dealing with investors

- Amit Khanna tells founders not to walk into a room assuming the investor holds all the power. He says good ideas are scarcer than capital.
- He explains that fund managers earn their fees only when they deploy capital and make money on it. If they do not invest in companies, they do not get paid and cannot raise their next fund. He tells founders to remember this when they think about leverage.
- He also says to treat investors with respect and not be arrogant.
- Amit Khanna warns against bluffing about a competing offer or a false deadline. If the bluff is called you cannot back it up, and it damages the reputation you are building.
- He says that when an investor questions your valuation, do not get defensive. Ask what their reservation is and answer it with data.
- On sharing information with a prospective investor before a term sheet, his rule is to share only what you would be willing to put on the internet publicly.
- On regular investor updates that could reach a competitor, he says to share only things that are not mission critical or proprietary.
- When someone calls a term "market standard", Amit Khanna asks what that means in practice, starting with what that party has agreed to in its own past deals.
- He advises early companies not to engage a lawyer on terms that reward the lawyer for spending more time on the matter.

### Guest: threats, difficult customers and value

- Amit Khanna says not to ignore a threat and not to react to it emotionally. Acknowledge it and stay calm.
- He reads a threat as a sign that the other person is getting desperate and close to their breaking point, which is useful information.
- He says people who make threats say things they later need an elegant way out of, and you should give them that way out.
- To a founder whose customers agree to anything and then do not pay, he says cash flow is what keeps a startup alive in its first months and years. He advises asking for a deposit, post-dated cheques or some advance, and declining business with a party you do not trust to pay.
- Amit Khanna says the person across the table and the business behind them can have different interests. A purchase manager's job is to buy as cheaply as possible, while the owner cares about more than price, including the relationship with the supplier.
- He says a founder selling something new should move the conversation from cost to value: what you bring that the next supplier does not.
- His closing point was that every call, contract and conversation builds your impression and reputation, and that lasts longer than the outcome of any one deal.

### Instructor: how disputes differ from deals

- The instructor says disputes differ from deal-making because emotions run higher and because the parties are already linked by a contract, so one side walking away hurts the other.
- She says emotion clouds judgment and makes disputes expensive. Her example is the Brad Pitt and Angelina Jolie divorce settlement, which she says took eight years.
- The instructor says people generally do not come to the table unless they want a deal, so a party who shows up is willing to listen even if they say they are not.
- She describes the fundamental attribution error: when something goes wrong we blame the person and ignore the situation they were in.
- She illustrates it with the Good Samaritan study. Trainee pastors told they were late rushed past a person calling for help, while those told they had time stopped. An onlooker without that context judges one group unkind and the other kind.
- The instructor says behaviour comes from an interaction between personality and situation, and accepting this is one way to manage your own anger in a dispute.
- She says a good tactic in dispute resolution is to let the other party vent before you respond.
- She says a dispute resolution must be future focused. Time spent going over what happened in the past does not help.

### Instructor: opening with an apology and interests

- The instructor says apologising for the situation at the start, without admitting you did something wrong, and then asking how to move forward disarms a party who planned to use anger.
- She names three approaches people use in a dispute: interests, rights and power. Interests means asking what you can do for the other side. Rights means pointing to the contract. Power means threats and anger.
- She says research shows parties who opened with interests or an apology reached better resolutions, and that the first few minutes of a dispute negotiation matter most.
- The instructor says opening with interests does not remove your rights. The contract stays in the background and both sides know it is there.
- She says rights and power can be used to bring a party to the table, or carefully near the end to improve the outcome, with the risk that the talks break down.
- She says even a party with no power can hurt you, because they have nothing to lose.
- In a dispute nobody gets everything they want, so she tells students to think about their worst alternative to a negotiated agreement, such as the cost and length of going to court, not their best one.
- Her template is: say what happened, then put an interest-based solution on the table immediately, because people listen when you bring solutions.
- She says changing the negotiator resets the game after threats have been exchanged, because backing down from your own threat is hard for the person who made it.

### Instructor: making and answering threats

- The instructor says never to start with a threat, even with strong alternatives and high power.
- She says a strong BATNA gives you the psychological power to ask for more. It is not for threatening the other side, because threatened people make very bad decisions.
- If you do make a threat, she gives four conditions: you must be willing to carry it out, it must target the other party's interests, it must leave them an exit, and it must be exact about what you will do, when and how.
- Her example of an exact threat is from a late home renovation: for every 24 hours past the contracted deadline, a stated percentage would be deducted from the final payment.
- She says credibility comes from reputation. Her example is Singapore's founding prime minister telling striking Singapore Airlines staff to end the strike or be fired, which was believed because of his record.
- The instructor says a leader who announces something one day and reverses it the next builds a reputation that makes later threats less credible. She uses Donald Trump as the example.
- On receiving a threat, she repeats the guest's advice: a threat signals desperation, so do not answer with a counter-threat, and let the other side exit gracefully.

### Instructor: irrational counterparts and your own emotions

- The instructor says a counterpart who looks irrational may simply have different information, so the first step is to ask questions and find out the basis for their position.
- She says everyone is irrational in some way. In the ultimatum game played in the first session, economic theory says any offer above zero should be accepted, but people rejected low offers.
- When a counterpart says nothing works for them, she asks for their own proposal and listens, because everyone likes to be heard.
- She says some people look irrational only because they are unprepared.
- The instructor tells students to prepare for emotion in advance: know which behaviours throw you off, decide how you want to feel when the negotiation is over, and have a way to regain balance such as a break or a joke.
- She says it is fine to ask for time to think during a negotiation.
- If a question catches you unprepared, she suggests saying it is an interesting question and you will come back on it.
- She says that if a setting makes you emotional you can change the setting. Her own example is asking to hold a difficult conversation with a senior colleague on a walk, not across a desk.
- She says the answer to "how do you want to feel afterwards" drives strategy, since most people do not want others to think badly of them.
- To catch your own bias she suggests distancing yourself: imagine you are negotiating on behalf of someone else and ask what outcome they would want.
- For a limited-time offer, her test is whether you would accept the same offer if there were no deadline.
- She says it is hard to say no to people we like or trust, so accept an offer only when it serves your interests.

### Instructor: negotiating from low power

- The instructor says your target price does not depend on your own power. It depends on what the other party can pay, which is their reservation price.
- She says that from a low power position you should not try to use the little power you have. First try to improve your power.
- She says both sides' alternatives matter. A party with a poor BATNA may be facing a counterpart who has no alternative either.
- She lists other sources of power when alternatives are weak: your network, your expertise, who you know and third parties.
- She says to negotiate the rules of the negotiation first, especially from a low power position.
- She says preparing more offers and more packages is a way to balance a power gap.
- For a first-time seller with no benchmark, she says she has no full answer beyond the guest's: focus on the value you bring that others do not.

### Instructor: multi-party negotiation

- The instructor says board meetings and discussions among co-founders are multi-party negotiations.
- She says who speaks first matters in a multi-party negotiation, because early speakers anchor everyone else. If you lead it, give your allies the floor first so others see support for your side.
- If you know the table will attack your proposal, she advises not opening yourself. Let a party who is on good terms with everyone speak first.
- She calls talking to parties one on one before the meeting coalition building, and says you do not need to be powerful, only allied with someone who is.
- She says coalitions break easily, so build them with people you depend on over the long term.
- On voting rules she says majority voting is acceptable for a one-time negotiation. Where the minority must keep working with the group, majority voting leaves them feeling shut out.
- She says unanimous voting gives every party equal power and is generally better, but makes a deal harder to reach.
- She says whether a vote is public or private changes the outcome, and that changing such process rules is itself a way to change decisions.
- She says you should not let two opponents of your proposal sit together.
- For multi-issue, multi-party cases she recommends an issue by party matrix in the planning document.

### Instructor: biases and influence

- The instructor says influencing someone means changing their beliefs about your offer, so you need to know how people decide. Biases are systematic, so they can be anticipated.
- She ran the 2, 4, 6 number sequence exercise to show confirmation bias. People propose arithmetic rules and overlook the simple rule of numbers in ascending order.
- She says negotiators walk in with assumptions that may be untrue, and that is confirmation bias at work.
- She cites a magazine claim that 94% of Fortune 500 CEOs had a childhood pet. She says the figure means nothing until you also count people with pets who did not become CEOs.
- She gives the Challenger launch decision as a case where the data was available but decision makers looked only at the data that confirmed what they already believed.
- On availability bias she says vivid, negative and recent information is easier to recall and so feels true. Her example is that people buy insurance right after a natural disaster.
- She describes the Milgram experiment to show obedience to authority. In a negotiation her advice is to look authoritative through information, expertise, dress and network.
- She says the Asch line experiment found subjects went along with a wrong group answer on 37% of critical trials, and on 5% when one partner gave the right answer.
- From Asch she draws two reasons for conforming: believing the group is right, and not wanting to look deviant. Letting people answer privately removes the second.
- She uses the principles from Robert Cialdini's book Influence: reciprocity, commitment and consistency, social proof, authority, scarcity and liking.
- On reciprocity she says to get something back for every concession, and to do small favours when they cost little.
- When thanked for a favour she suggests replying that you are sure the other person would do the same for you, not "don't mention it".
- On social proof she says to back a first offer with evidence such as a research study or what others are doing. Her examples are sitcom laugh tracks and hotel sites showing how many people booked.
- On scarcity she says people value what is limited, and that luxury brands such as Prada and Louis Vuitton do not discount for that reason.
- On liking she says people do things for those they like, and that you become likable by being trustworthy, polite and a good listener, and by finding similarities with the other person.
- She says to place a similar but inferior option beside your offer so that yours looks better.
- She describes argument dilution: people average arguments, so five strong arguments are weakened when you add three weak ones.

### Instructor: closing summary of the course

- The instructor says different negotiations need different preparation, and that preparation gives an edge because many parties do not prepare.
- She says the goal sets the strategy. In a distributive, one-shot, one-issue negotiation you make the first offer. In a dispute, opening with numbers while the other side is angry goes nowhere.
- She says differences in preferences between parties are useful, and tools such as contingent contracts let both sides gain from them.
- She says everyone can do the same homework on numbers, so how you deal with people is what sets negotiators apart.
- Her one book recommendation on negotiation is Negotiation Genius by Deepak Malhotra and Max Bazerman. She also recommends Daniel Kahneman's book on biases and the film 12 Angry Men for public voting.

## Examples and cases used

- Restaurant space auction (guest's own live case): modelling a competitor's finances, timing its bids, asking the landlord instead of assuming, and collaborating after the auction.
- Brad Pitt and Angelina Jolie divorce: emotion makes disputes long and costly.
- Good Samaritan study: situation, not character, explained who stopped to help.
- Singapore Airlines strike and Singapore's founding prime minister: a threat is credible when the reputation behind it is.
- Donald Trump: reversing statements erodes the credibility of threats.
- Ultimatum game: people reject offers that pure economics says they should accept.
- 2, 4, 6 sequence task and the Challenger launch: confirmation bias.
- Milgram experiment: people obey an authority figure.
- Asch conformity experiment and a television elevator experiment: people follow the group.
- Sitcom laugh tracks and hotel booking counters: social proof in everyday products.
- Prada and Louis Vuitton: scarcity kept by not discounting.
- Bar closing time study: ratings of attractiveness rise as closing time nears, which she ties to scarcity and to the comparison group.
