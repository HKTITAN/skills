# Building the MVP of a distributed cancer hospital, Sonali Srungaram

Sonali Srungaram, a former consultant who founded a cancer care company in Hyderabad, gave the first of three lean startup guest lectures. She ran it as a live case: she laid out the cancer care problem in India, asked the cohort what they would build, and then walked through the choices she made on people, infrastructure and process for an 18 month MVP. She drew general lessons for founders along the way and took questions.

## What was said

### The problem she started from

- Srungaram says India has about 300 hospitals that treat cancer, across government, trust and private, and only about 3,000 specialised oncologists.
- She says 90% of cancer treatment sits in ten cities, because oncologists finish training in their early thirties with loans to repay, and both the jobs and the expensive infrastructure are in metros.
- Her Telangana example: the cancer population is estimated at 2.5 lakh, about 90% of facilities are in Hyderabad, only 20,000 of those patients live there, and about 60,000 people travel to Hyderabad each year for treatment.
- She says the average district is two and a half hours from Hyderabad by road and the furthest is close to seven hours, so one treatment visit can cost a patient two days.
- She says a doctor assumes the patient's journey starts at the clinic door, when it began a day earlier with pulling resources together for the bus.
- She explains that cancer needs three kinds of specialist: medical oncologists for chemotherapy and other drug therapies, surgical oncologists, and radiation oncologists. No one doctor covers all three, and the average patient needs two of the three.
- She says seven out of ten cancer patients in India stop treatment midway because it is long, difficult and expensive.
- She gives 4 to 4.5 lakh rupees as what an average cancer patient pays over the treatment, and 8 to 10 lakh in extreme cases.
- She says only 17% of India has health insurance and most of that is state government cover, and that the average family policy is 2.5 lakh rupees for a family of five.
- She says only 20 to 25% of cancer patients reach a private hospital, so private hospitals and doctors compete hard for them.

### Choosing which problem to solve

- The cohort listed the problems: too few oncologists, access, affordability, support for carers, screening, infrastructure, operations and research. Srungaram says a small startup cannot take on all of them and has to pick one.
- She ruled out the shortage of oncologists because she had no teaching institution and a new batch takes years to graduate.
- She says the access versus affordability choice was an emotional internal debate. She chose access because much of what a patient spends is on travel, an overnight stay and an attendant, and bringing treatment closer removes that cost.
- She says the load on family carers is real: over three to nine months of treatment the hospital sees four or five different family members take turns bringing the patient.
- Her first venture offered allied services such as psychology, nutrition and genetics. Treating doctors would not refer patients to it, and sent only end of life cases, so it could not make money.
- She says screening is hard for a private company: the government budget is 500 rupees per person per year, tenders are won by players who subsidise them for other reasons, and the government pays late. She is still waiting for payment on a 2018 project.
- She now does screening for free as an add-on, because it helps her get into a district.

### Why she did not become an NGO

- Srungaram says people kept telling her to become an NGO because making money from cancer feels dirty. She was in that dilemma for her first three years.
- She decided against it because she had to pay market salaries to attract good doctors and build a credible institution. Her comparison: a good private sector CEO makes 5 lakh a month and an NGO CEO cannot make more than 1.5 lakh.
- She points out that trust hospitals also rely on paying patients to subsidise the poor, so someone has to make money.

### Pricing and choosing a segment

- She says price signals quality: the same person pays 30 rupees for tea at a stall and 500 rupees at a five star hotel, and is happy to do both.
- She says a founder should not decide on the customer's behalf what they can afford. The job is to put options in front of them and let them choose.
- She says you will not serve every segment, so you have to decide where you want to play.
- Her company targets middle class patients, slightly upper middle class in the city and lower in the districts, with two tier pricing that is lower in the district so patients do not travel to the city.
- She calls pricing "an absolute nightmare". With incumbents you either undercut them, which she does not always recommend, or create more value than they do.
- For a new category she says to find a lookalike business, copy its pricing position and test it. She says finding the right price takes three to nine months, depending on how fast the product is consumed.
- She prefers going out and talking to customers, including focus groups, over paying consultants for pricing models. She says the only real test is whether someone pays and then comes back.

### How hospital economics work

- Srungaram says the main metric for a hospital is revenue per occupied bed. Like a hotel, a hospital earns little from outpatient visits and makes its money when a patient stays the night.
- She gives 40,000 rupees as the revenue per occupied bed of a good multi-speciality hospital, and says Max Healthcare runs at 1 lakh.
- She says only three departments really make a hospital money today: cardiology, oncology and organ transplant.
- She says a basic multi-speciality hospital costs at least one crore rupees per bed to build, and civil work is 30 to 40% of that.
- She says in a surgery bill at least 30% goes to the surgeon, the anaesthetist gets 30% of the surgeon's fee, and about half the bill goes to people.
- She says 15% is a good EBITDA margin for a hospital, and an average hospital can survive only about three months if patients stop coming.
- She says cancer care has no repeat purchase, so all she can do is keep the patient with her for the whole treatment. A founder should know whether the business is repeat or one-time, and invest in the sales process if it has to sell constantly.

### The MVP: breaking up the cancer hospital

- She frames an MVP around three limits: the features you need, the time you have and the money you have. For her the features were people, infrastructure and processes.
- She had about 8 crore rupees and 18 months. Her build options were a medical oncology ward at 30 lakh and two months, an operating theatre at 3 crore and four months, and a radiation bunker at 30 crore and a year.
- She says 80% of cancer patients in India need medical oncology, 30% use surgery and 40% use radiation. Surgery is low because cancers are detected late.
- Her hypothesis was that medical and surgical oncology can be delivered 150 kilometres from the city centre. She picked 150 kilometres because a doctor can drive out, treat and return the same day.
- The MVP was not a full cancer hospital. Medical oncology was fully in-house, surgery used rented operating theatres, and radiation was outsourced entirely.
- She says every doctor told her the distributed model was mad and patients would die. She says hers is the first distributed cancer hospital in India.

### People: who she hired and how she paid them

- She says she could not afford star doctors. One star costs about 12 lakh rupees a month, and for that she could hire three or four young doctors.
- A senior doctor she had hired left two months after launch. She replaced him by recruiting on campus from a graduating batch whose offers had been frozen in the pandemic, which she says is not normally done in medicine.
- She hired only from government hospitals, where a doctor has seen 100 to 150 outpatients a day.
- Each doctor took on the district they came from, which she says gave them a personal reason to make the weekly trip.
- She chose full-time doctors over part-time ones, partly because part-time doctors work at several hospitals and would carry her protocols with them.
- She started with a part-time surgeon and switched to full-time, because the surgeon was never free when the rented theatre slot was.
- She says an average oncologist in India is given a sales target of seven times salary, and that this stops doctors referring patients to each other and hurts clinical outcomes.
- At her co-founder's push she put the surgeon and the medical oncologists on equal pay and made them one team, although the market pays a medical oncologist about twice what it pays a surgeon.
- She pays a fixed salary 10 to 15% above market, plus a performance incentive, plus equity. The doctors did not value the equity at first and preferred salary.
- She says a founder has to pitch to talent the way a company pitches at campus placements, with a pay structure and a career path, and that being professional up front matters most in a traditional sector.
- She says building a retention and career programme for her doctors took three years.
- She says it is good to have a partner who pushes back, and that people who always agree do not help you.

### Infrastructure and the patient experience

- She rented 2,000 square feet inside partner hospitals and fitted out a medical oncology ward for about 30 lakh rupees, because wards for middle class chemotherapy patients did not exist.
- She set the service up so the file and bed are ready on arrival and the patient sees a doctor within 20 minutes, with no names shouted across a waiting room.
- Her patients rejected the recliners that premium hospitals use. They had travelled for hours and wanted to lie down, so she replaced the recliners with beds at extra cost.
- Her nurses reported that patients did not want to be called sir and madam. They wanted aunty and uncle, so she changed the protocol.
- She says doctors want new equipment every year, and the company has had to keep upgrading infrastructure to keep them. It has gone from renting, to standalone clinics, to building its own hospital with a radiation bunker.

### Process, IP and build versus buy

- Srungaram says renting is fine when it gets a prototype to market faster and cheaper.
- She says outsource only what is commoditised and keep what is special and core in-house. Her example is a college admissions service: the coaching method is the IP, and the login and scheduling software can be outsourced.
- When you must outsource, she says to give each party only what it needs to know, so that no one outside holds the full picture.
- She says a legal right is of limited use in India because a case takes years. Her own dispute over a copied idea has been in arbitration for two years.
- Her core process is how chemotherapy is delivered safely far from the city: drug transport and mixing, a triple check, and how discharge summaries are written. She kept it in-house and says other hospitals have tried to obtain her forms through her nurses.
- She says trust has to be earned and does not come from signing an employment contract.
- She says to spend more time thinking than doing, because cracking the problem is the hard part and execution follows.

### Money, investors and persistence

- She says early investors ask how much of your own money is in the venture and do not accept sweat equity alone. She says she raised a million from angels and put in half of it herself.
- She says health tech investors rejected her for not being digital, and it took two to three years to find investors who understand hospitals. Her advice for a niche sector is to curate investors who know the space.
- Her first institutional investor came in during the pandemic, when the company was desperate, and got a cheap deal. She does not regret it.
- Healthcare investors told her a distributed model could not match the unit economics of a 100 bed cancer hospital in a metro. She has spent five years trying to reach the same metrics and expects to get there next year.
- The centres opened on 4 February 2020 and the pandemic began a month later. She kept paying her doctors for about a year with revenue at 10 to 15% of what it should have been, and could only start district work in November 2021.
- She says grants sound attractive but take up a lot of time and come with accountability for every rupee.
- She says founders must be hard on themselves about deadlines and money: a month and a half against a one month estimate means poor estimation or poor time management.
- She says successful founders are the ones who did well with limited resources. "It's what you need, not what you want."
- She says no problem is too complex if you apply yourself, and that persistence matters as much as brain power. There is also no shame in scrapping the work and starting again.

### On treatment decisions

- Asked whether treating an elderly patient is worth the cost to the family, she says it is not for the doctor or the hospital to decide. The doctor's duty is to put every clinically sound option in front of the patient.
- She says in India the family often decides for the patient and asks the hospital to hide the diagnosis. She is against this.
- She says management should not interfere in clinical decisions and doctors should not interfere in management.
- She says ignoring social realities does not work when you build a product. In district screening, many women were not allowed to attend by their families.

## Examples and cases used

- Max Healthcare: her benchmark for a high revenue per occupied bed, at 1 lakh rupees against 40,000 for a good multi-speciality hospital.
- L.V. Prasad Eye Institute, with Aravind and Sankara Nethralaya: paying patients fund treatment for poor patients, so even mission-led hospitals need revenue.
- Basavatarakam, a trust cancer hospital in Hyderabad: about 40% of its patients are below the poverty line and the rest increasingly pay.
- Varian and Accuray: specialist radiation machine makers. Equipment companies fund teaching hospitals so doctors train on their machines, and they buy up competitors.
- Pfizer, Merck and GlaxoSmithKline: big pharma now buys smaller outfits that do the R&D, which she says makes pharma easier to enter than heavy equipment.
- Proton beam therapy: her example of costly R&D with a small gain. She puts it at about 6.5 lakh rupees against 2.5 lakh for standard radiation, for fewer side effects.
- Accenture, where she worked for 12 years: the source of the career progression models she reused for doctors.
- Vincent DeVita: she cites his view that a cancer doctor's job is to keep the patient alive until the next treatment comes along.

## Numbers given

- About 300 cancer-treating hospitals and about 3,000 oncologists in India, her figures.
- Telangana: 2.5 lakh estimated cancer patients, 20,000 of them in Hyderabad, 60,000 travelling to Hyderabad each year.
- 7 in 10 cancer patients in India stop treatment midway.
- 4 to 4.5 lakh rupees paid by an average cancer patient over treatment.
- 17% of India insured, with an average family policy of 2.5 lakh rupees.
- 20 to 25% of cancer patients reach the private sector.
- Starting private salary for an oncologist: 2.5 to 3 lakh rupees a month. Government doctor: 50,000 to 75,000 a month.
- Build costs: 30 lakh for a medical oncology ward, 3 crore for an operating theatre, 30 crore for a radiation bunker.
- 80% of patients need medical oncology, 30% surgery, 40% radiation.
- In most cancer hospitals medical oncology drives 50% of revenue, and surgery and radiation 25% each.
- Hospital EBITDA of 15% as a good number.
- Sales target of 7 times salary for an average oncologist.
- Eight centres across Andhra Pradesh and Telangana at the time of the talk.
