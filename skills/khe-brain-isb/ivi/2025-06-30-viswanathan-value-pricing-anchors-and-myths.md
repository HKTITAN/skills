# Value pricing part 1, pricing anchors and myths, Madhu Viswanathan

Madhu Viswanathan taught the first half of a class on value pricing, up to the break. The speaker is not named in this recording and is identified by matching it to another recording of the same class. He opened with water and petrol to show that even commodities sell at different prices, went through three common pricing anchors and why each falls short, listed pricing myths, and then set out the pieces of maximum willingness to pay. The file also carries a classmate's written notes, used below only where marked.

## What was said

### Commodities still carry different prices

- Madhu Viswanathan defines a commodity, in the economist's sense, as a raw good made in mass, freely traded and without much differentiation.
- He says a 20 rupee water bottle is the market norm today, while two or three decades ago people would not have paid for water and would have asked for it at someone's house.
- He says restaurants charge for water and get away with it even where tap water is drinkable.
- He says petrol is as much a commodity as anything can be, and still there are substantial differences in price.
- He says Shell petrol is more expensive because its service setup is much better.
- He says the value has to go beyond the product itself, otherwise you cannot charge a premium.
- He says value based pricing requires you to understand how customers differ from one another.

### Where price sits

- He says price is the tip of the iceberg, and that most of the time people only see the list price.
- He says price comes after segmentation, targeting and positioning. His example is a pricing strategy that promotes experimentation when the target is early adopters.
- He says everybody understands price because it is a number everyone can see, so any salesperson starts by negotiating on price.
- He says companies with a process move the conversation from price to what they are saving the customer.
- He says that when you compete on price your game is a cost game, where you are a low cost provider who needs to sell volumes.
- He says a low cost player is super efficient on the supply side and squeezes its supplier network and employees in the quest for low cost.

### Anchor one, cost based pricing

- He says cost based pricing uses readily available accounting data and is the first method most people reach for: add up costs and put a margin on top.
- He calls this approach very incorrect and a finance and accounting led way of thinking.
- He says the first problem with cost plus is that people do not know their real cost. He asks about wages, marketing, the cost of space and loans.
- He says the second problem is that the margin added on top is a random number.
- He points to restaurants and pharma, where the cost is almost nothing compared with the price paid, and says branded pharma margins are usually 100% or more.
- He says a managerial accounting class is one really good class every founder should take.
- He tells the class "do not start with cost plus".

### Anchor two, competition based pricing

- He describes competition based pricing as doing exactly what the competitor is doing.
- He says you are not selling the same thing as your competitor, because if you were, customers would have no reason to come to you.
- He says whether you have the pricing power to charge for the difference is a separate question.
- He says there will always be a deal seeking segment, and the question is how big it is for you.
- He says the answer starts from your value proposition. A Walmart kind of seller has a super price sensitive segment, while Target does not.
- His illustration is that 70% of the market is there for you, and going after the other 30% means competing purely on price.

### Anchor three, sales orientation and discounts

- He describes the sales orientation as pricing that is about selling, and says new salespeople do this while good salespeople do not.
- He says discounts do not happen just because a customer came and asked.
- He says flash sales and factory outlets exist as ways to reduce stock holdings.
- He says discounts are a good strategy for trial, and the extent varies.
- He describes two ways to run a trial: full access for two weeks or however long the customer needs to see the value, or a 10% or 20% discount to start with a clear statement of the price after two or three months.
- He says a time limited trial is much better than freemium, though it depends on the category.
- He says a trial offer fits the entry into a new market segment.
- His example is a company selling banks a product that improves their risk insight on clients. It gave selected banks the full product free for two months, and is now rethinking the trial because enough customers ask for the product without it.
- He says a company can change consumer habit gradually over a year or two, or take the brash approach of throwing money at it with subsidised prices.
- He says an investor in such a company should know that the early numbers are not the actual numbers.
- He says all three anchors are used in some form but are not the best ones to have.

### Pricing myths

- He says the first myth is that cost is the price, when prices should reflect the value you bring to customers and not production costs.
- He says the second myth is that small price moves do not matter.
- He says price matters to all customers, but not to the extent people think.
- He says a player in a super price sensitive segment who is also a value player has to move on price.
- He says the belief that products cannot be differentiated comes from a lack of creativity and from being internally focused on the product in place of the customer.
- He says a business does not need to be unit positive all the time, but should set norms for where it will be in two years.
- He says a company should be careful about changing prices across channels because that creates channel conflict.
- He says the moment you price low, people wonder whether the product is as premium as claimed.
- He says "cheap and best" does not hold, because quality needs investment and a cheap price leaves no margin to keep providing it.

### Reference value and maximum willingness to pay

- He defines the reference value as the price of the customer's best alternative, adjusted so the units are comparable.
- He defines differentiation value as where your offer is better, the positive part, and where it is worse, the negative part.
- He says total customer value is the reference value plus positive differentiation minus negative differentiation, and that this is also the maximum willingness to pay.
- He says the reference value is not only the price paid for the alternative. Non monetary costs of the current way of doing things count as well.
- He says some value is very hard to quantify and will always be left on the table. His example is the network students form by sitting together.
- He says customers go by perceived value, which comes from marketing efforts, context, reference prices and competitors.
- He says the result is a pricing zone. You can price below the reference price, at it or above it.
- He says a premium brand picks its own reference: "BMW never says look at Maruti".
- He says price minus cost is the potential contribution margin.
- He says the framework does not tell you the exact price, only the zone you can price in.

### Raising prices and premium tiers

- He says a firm can take marginal features that cost little, package them as a premium tier for businesses and charge more, as Zoom does.
- He says raising price across a mass base is hard, and the chief executive has to decide whether losing customers is acceptable.
- He says "It is PQ that I care about, not just Q", meaning price times quantity.
- He says Netflix makes courageous calls against the grain, such as going digital when its mail business was doing well, and that it loses customers on price moves yet stays regularly profitable.
- He says a premium product cannot discount continuously.
- He says price hurts more when paid in cash and less with UPI, but the effect does not become zero.
- He says not to announce a premium price of 25 and bring it to 20, because people see through a product masquerading as premium. You decide what your price is.

### From the written class notes in the file

- The notes record that people pay for perception, emotion and brand, and that your positioning is your value.
- The notes record the figure that a 1% price increase can yield an 8 to 12% improvement in profit, with the advice to fight for every penny.
- The notes record the myth that customers are price obsessed, against which B2B buyers prioritise value, reliability and total cost of ownership.
- The notes record the myth that market share equals profits, against which volume without margin destroys enterprise value.
- The notes record the myth that managing price means changing price, against which effective pricing is a system and a process.
- The notes list three value based approaches: value in use pricing, which considers total cost of ownership, perceived value pricing and performance based pricing.
- The notes list three steps in selling value: communicate, calibrate and quantify.

## Examples and cases used

- Bottled water: once free, now 20 rupees as a norm, to show that reference prices change.
- Shell: charges more for petrol because of a better service setup.
- Bisleri: sells two bottles at 20 and 30 rupees with the same water and bottle, where only the positioning differs.
- Walmart and Target: the first serves a super price sensitive segment, the second does not.
- Restaurants and branded pharma: prices far above cost, to show cost does not set price.
- Zoom: packages low cost extras into a higher priced business tier.
- Netflix: moved from mail to digital while mail was doing well, and accepts losing customers when it raises prices.
- BMW and Maruti: a premium brand does not use a mass brand as its reference.

## Numbers given

- 100% or more: the margin he gives for branded pharma.
- 70% and 30%: his illustrative split between the market that is there for you and the deal seeking part you would have to win on price.
- 10% or 20%: the size of an introductory discount he describes for a trial.
- 1% and 8 to 12%: from the written notes, a 1% price increase and the profit improvement it can yield.
