# Paper Boat and Cult lessons on brand and focus, Parvesh Debuka

This is the fourth recorded part of Parvesh Debuka's marketing bootcamp. He told the story of Paper Boat's first campaign and its go-to-market mistake, described how Cult acquired and kept members, and answered founders' questions on when to add products and how to stand out in a commodity category. He spoke from his own time at Paper Boat, Cult and Tanishq. Advice aimed at individual ventures is left out.

## What was said

### Paper Boat: from tension to campaign

- Parvesh Debuka says that once Paper Boat had found the consumer tension, drinks like aam panna led straight to nostalgia.
- He says arriving at the line "drinks and memories" took a long time and many discarded options.
- He says there were internal debates about the line having no call to action, and the view that won was that it simply worked.
- He says the task then was to show that the drinks came straight out of a mother's kitchen, so the first campaign was set around home, family and relationships.
- He says the campaign mixed brand and product marketing. It showed the product as made at home and left out features, trusting people to infer the quality.
- He says Paper Boat's first print campaign was irreverent and took on the big mango drink brands directly, the way one takes on a bully.
- He gives 45% pulp in Paper Boat's aamras against 10% in the large mango drinks.

### Paper Boat: the go-to-market mistake

- He says the mistakes at Paper Boat were not in communication but in go-to-market.
- He says the team got over-excited and spent crores on television. The campaign created demand, but supply could not reach the outlets, and a lot of money was wasted.
- He says at that time companies spent under 5 to 6% of their annual marketing budget on digital, and 10% at the most, so everyone went to television and print.

### Packaging and price in Indian beverages

- He says the packaging question for Paper Boat was how to stand out on the shelf.
- He says choosing opaque packaging meant giving up a critical way of driving desire, which is letting people see the drink. The team accepted that because standing out gave it a shot at winning.
- He says Paper Boat made drinks like aamras available through the year, when earlier they were only had for about three months in summer.
- He says the limiting factor for Paper Boat was the price point and not the number of consumers. He gives 1,000 crore as the size of the market at 30 rupees.
- He says the 10 rupee segment is far bigger than the 20 rupee segment in single-serve drinks, noting that his data is about ten years old.
- He says a 10 rupee drink will always do better in India because it is a poor country, and the large group of buyers is people who work very hard, along with college students.
- He says reaching the few customers who pay a premium meant delivering to about 100 fragmented outlets in a city, 30 to 40 km apart, which was a distribution nightmare.
- He says quick commerce has now made those customers easier to reach.
- He says that distribution problem is where Swing came from.

### How Cult acquired and kept members

- He says Cult gave free trial classes and then followed up with the people who took them.
- He says members booked classes on the app, and Cult penalised those who booked and did not show up.
- He says the message of the penalty was that Cult cares about the member's fitness as much as the member does, or more.
- He says no-shows were bad for people who wanted to work out and could not get a slot, so Cult added a waitlist that worked like a train ticket.
- He says a member had to confirm one hour before the class. An unconfirmed slot went to the next person on the waitlist, and a member who did not feel like going could cancel so that the waitlisted person got in.
- He says the variety of formats and the group of people one met turned Cult into a community.
- He says Cult's centres were called training centres and not gyms.
- He says ideas like the waitlist came from a culture where anyone could suggest ideas, not only one department.

### When to add products

- He says the better route for new products is to understand the further problems of the same audience, not to think of a product and then find an audience for it.
- He says a brand's target group shifts every time it adds a category, and it becomes hard to cater to each one.
- He says a brand can stand for a broad idea and attack several problems under it, but should ask why it wants to if it can do justice to only one.
- He urges founders to stay with the first opportunity and not get distracted by easier or adjacent ones.
- He uses Titan to show expansion in stages: watches alone for more than a decade, then jewellery, then eyewear.
- He says a founder who picks a 300 to 400 crore market will run into the problem of needing more products. His comparison is choosing a 5,000 crore market in the first place.
- He says once customers trust a brand, it can launch more products for those same people, because it understands them and sees the problems they face.
- He says talking to the mother about what is better for her child and talking to the child with a free gift are two different target groups.
- He says he has not worked in B2B branding.

### Standing out in a commodity category

- He doubts that colour is a real tension in soft drinks, since so many brands use colour and customers do not care.
- He says to play to the strengths of the product, such as flavour.
- He says when a product is not differentiated enough for customers to pay for the difference, naming and branding are the only levers left.
- His funnel for such products is to drive trials with what he calls "ridiculous branding" and let the product do the heavy lifting on repeat purchase.
- He says striking packaging gets the product noticed on the shelf, and social media can back it with stories.
- He says terms a mass customer does not understand, such as botanical, should stay in the internal statement and out of the customer message.
- He says he would warn anyone against launching a beverage brand, and describes the jeera drink segment as crowded.

### Positioning a content platform

- He says a content platform spanning several genres needs one sharp thing it is about, and that a generic claim such as snackable content is not enough.
- His example of going sharper is telling stories nobody else is telling, such as those of lesser known characters.

### Ambition and market size

- He says Myntra has reported profits only now, after about 15 years.
- He says Minimalist, which was bought by HUL, is a 300 crore brand, in a category where women spend a lot of money.
- He says founders should ask themselves tough questions about which problem they are picking, how large they want to become and what their ambition is.

## Examples and cases used

- Paper Boat: the nostalgia positioning, the home-centred first campaign, the opaque pack, and the mistake of advertising on television ahead of distribution.
- Swing: the 10 rupee brand that followed from Paper Boat's price and distribution limits.
- Cult: free trials, no-show penalties, the waitlist and community as acquisition and retention tools.
- Titan: staged expansion from watches to jewellery to eyewear.
- Minimalist and Myntra: how hard it is to become large or profitable.

## Numbers given

- 45% against 10%: fruit pulp in Paper Boat's aamras against large mango drinks, as Parvesh Debuka states it.
- Under 5 to 6%, at most 10%: the share of marketing budgets companies spent on digital when Paper Boat launched.
- 1,000 crore: his figure for the market at a 30 rupee price point at that time.
- 100 outlets, 30 to 40 km apart: his picture of premium distribution in one city.
- 300 crore: his figure for Minimalist's size.
