# Verification, company building and leadership, Kirthiga Reddy

Kirthiga Reddy, the first employee of Facebook in India and now co-founder of a credential verification company the recording calls Verix, spoke in a fireside format. A faculty host interviewed her on identity and verification, then the cohort asked questions on going to market early, winning trust, co-founders, hiring and leadership. Questions that were about a student's own startup are left out.

## What was said

### What her company does

- Kirthiga Reddy says her venture began as Virtualness and now works on verifying credentials, so that a claim such as a degree or a speaking engagement can be checked instantly against the issuer.
- She says the focus is on areas where credentialing does not yet exist: awards, recognitions, events, communities, memberships and skills.
- She describes skills passports built with governments and states as one use, and digital recognition as a close cousin of verification.
- She puts the market for all of this at 24 billion dollars.
- She demonstrates a credential issued by a university that can be checked on the blockchain ledger, and says checking a claim by hand can take 45 minutes.
- She says data is stored hashed and the issuing organisation controls what is shared, whether it is searchable and whether sharing is enabled.
- She explains zero knowledge proofs as proving authenticity without revealing the content, such as confirming a transcript is genuine without showing the grades.
- She says the model puts control back in people's hands, with people choosing what information they want to receive.
- She says the infrastructure is improving fast. When the company first ran transactions it found a 63 percent success rate and had to build a lot of infrastructure.
- She says the need to verify what is real grows in the age of AI, because video and voice can be generated.

### Sell the benefit, not the technology

- She tells her team that if you ask a company whether it wants a blockchain solution the answer should be no, because the buyer does not care whether it is blockchain.
- She says customers buy the benefit, which in her case is instant global verifiability.
- Her Facebook parallel: carriers sold data plans as a way to connect with friends, not as getting on the internet.
- She says a verified claim stands out against unverified ones.

### Go out early

- Her general principle is to go to market early, test and iterate, and not to build in a room by yourself.
- She says the company only knew its success rate because it had gone to market, and that data point told it to go back and rebuild.
- She says to work with clients, find allies, and use today's AI tools to build the best product possible.
- Asked about the risk to reputation of going early in an industry where you get one chance, she says the answer is to identify innovators and thought leaders in those roles who will take a risk with a startup.

### Winning trust and first customers

- She says a new enterprise seller should build trust through networks it already has and through introductions from its institution.
- She suggests a mid sized company as a stepping stone, or the number two or number three player.
- Her example is Facebook India's early days, when the large carriers would not talk to the company and Aircel, a smaller and hungrier player, took the bet.
- She says a contact who is convinced in the meeting still has to get buy in inside their own company, and often cannot explain the so what and the why now without the founder present.
- Her fix is to give that contact something they can forward, such as a short recorded video, with the questions answered in the customer's language.

### Talent, co-founders and first hires

- She says the war for talent is always there, including at Meta. In Hyderabad she made closing calls to the parents of candidates.
- She says people choose a job for the mission of the company, its leadership and its culture.
- She says she had an offer to lead Google in India at the same time as the Facebook offer.
- She gives 65 percent as the share of startups that fail because of co-founder dynamics, and says to spend a lot of time on the choice.
- Her co-founder was someone she knew from her Facebook days, so they knew how to go through challenges together.
- She and her co-founder went through the what if scenarios in detail, and some took weeks to resolve.
- She says to look for complementary skills in a co-founder.
- Her first team came from another blockchain company that failed to raise a round: four people who had already worked together for years, which she says saves months or years.
- She used a talent platform to find the first engineers for the prototype and hired some of them, and suggests short term projects as a way to try people.
- She recalls a revolt at Facebook India when the first managers were hired from outside. She promised the team they would grow faster under those leaders.

### Leadership lessons

- Her mantra is that when businesses succeed, livelihoods flourish, and she says she does not see impact and business as a trade off.
- She says 33 percent of Facebook India's employees each year were hired by Facebook offices elsewhere in the world, which she counts as impact.
- Her first manager's advice was to focus on the success of your customers, your organisation and your team, and your own success follows as the by product.
- Advice she received on joining Facebook to build an operations office, having come from product and engineering, was to learn the literacy of that function so she could lead it from a high level.
- She says she feels she is on the steepest learning curve of her career now, and hopes to say the same in ten years.
- She says Facebook India spent almost its first six months on its vision, and set a goal it called 100-100-100 that stayed for the first four years.
- She says a big bold goal drives energy and makes a team think differently from how a small goal would.
- She closes with a story of workers doing the same task, one building a wall and one building a cathedral, to make the point that perspective matters.

### Influencers, ads and platform responsibility

- She is bullish on influencers and on AI helping them scale, and calls the way brands find influencers today a very inefficient market.
- She separates using AI to touch up a video from using AI to make a person say something they did not say.
- On running ads she says to study the new AI driven sales and marketing tools, and notes that some companies now run twelve channels at once where the old advice was to focus on one.
- She says a founder should keep someone focused on optimising ad spend, because it can be the biggest money drain.
- She says growth at Facebook came from decisions made every day on data, and that the product was viral because of the growth team.
- She says Facebook and the industry need to spend much more time asking what is the worst that could happen if a product falls into the wrong hands, and that this applies even more to AI.

## Examples and cases used

- Netflix: she says it credentialed video editors trained on its curriculum, and the verified certificate helped them get jobs faster.
- Aircel: the smaller carrier that partnered with Facebook India when the leaders would not.
- A lab grown diamond brand: a product whose origin can be scanned and checked.
- LinkedIn: the host's foil, a place where stated credentials are not verified.

## Numbers given

- 63 percent: the transaction success rate her company saw when it first started.
- 24 billion dollars: the market she gives for credentials and recognition.
- 65 percent: the share of startups she says fail because of co-founder dynamics.
- 33 percent: the share of Facebook India employees hired each year by Facebook offices abroad, in her account.
