# Marketing bootcamp day 1, brand building and campaigns, Chandan Mendiratta

Chandan Mendiratta, who led brand marketing at Zomato and now works at Zepto, ran the first day of a two-day marketing bootcamp. He walked through a printed deck, played Zomato and Zepto ad films and took them apart, and answered questions throughout. He closed by setting a team assignment for day 2. He said he is not a career marketer and that this was the first time he had taught the material as a course.

## What was said

### What marketing is for

- Mendiratta defines marketing, citing Kotler, as creating genuine customer value, which means making the customer's life better.
- He says a campaign improves a customer's life in one of two ways, functional or emotional, and a campaign that does neither is "just theatre".
- He separates three words: marketing is the activity, branding is the asset that builds over time, and advertising is the way the work is executed.
- He says the goal of marketing is behavioural change: a non-buyer starts buying, a buyer buys more, or a lapsed buyer comes back.
- He says to pick the problem before starting a campaign and to circle back afterwards to check whether the campaign addressed it.
- He warns against the brief "let's make something viral".

### Orders equal users times frequency

- He gives the growth equation as orders equal users multiplied by frequency, so orders grow only by adding users or raising frequency.
- He says a campaign should have one clearly defined primary user, either new, active or lapsed, because the message differs for each.
- He says reactivating lapsed users is the cheapest route, usually through coupons, but they have lower lifetime value and tend to lapse again.
- He says new users cost more per order but have the best lifetime value, and that a young business should focus on acquiring users for its first several years.
- He says raising the frequency of active users is the hardest of the three because it means changing a habit.
- He says frequency cannot be built forever, because rising frequency signals an attractive market and brings new competitors.

### Cool over functional

- He says the common failure is choosing "cool over functional": a script wins the team over and the original goal is forgotten.
- His example is a Zomato film with Hrithik Roshan and Katrina Kaif. The brief was to tell users that Zomato delivers on time, the on-time-or-free promise behind it was dropped late, and the finished film ended on an emotional line with no action a viewer could take.
- He says that film had no impact on the business and that it was distributed like a performance creative without performing like one.
- He says the same mistake happened at Zepto with a film of a horse on a sofa, which was dropped within days and replaced by a plain film listing lowest prices, no delivery fee and 10 minute delivery.
- He says creative people are more often right than wrong, but a left-brain check must always be applied to a right-brain creative.
- He says branding campaigns suit mature companies and festival moments, not a growing company in an ordinary month.
- He says festivals and big events are when customers adopt new brands or switch from competitors, so a brand should never miss them.

### Support the worldview, ask for a small leap

- Drawing on Seth Godin's All Marketers are Liars, he says behavioural change comes most easily from supporting what people already believe, and that people reject a creative that challenges their worldview.
- He says to ask how big a leap the campaign expects from the customer, because customers will not make a large one.
- His failed example is a Zomato campaign that gave each day of the week a food theme to push daily ordering. It ran against the belief that eating food from outside should be occasional.
- He adds that the same campaign split the budget across seven creatives, so no viewer saw any one of them often enough, and a survey showed people had not understood the message.
- His corrected example is Zepto Super Saver. The film first agrees that a month of online shopping feels expensive, then asks only that the viewer check the prices once.
- He says checking an app is a low-friction ask, and that belief follows once people see the prices themselves.
- He reports that after the Super Saver films the share of orders on Super Saver rose, message recall in the survey was high and few people attributed Super Saver to another brand.
- He says the products named in that film were everyday needs such as flour, dal and rice, not wants such as cold drinks and chips, because low prices on needs are what a monthly shopper checks.
- He says the choice of user and the message must match. The Super Saver film was written for existing convenience shoppers in the middle of the funnel, not for new users.

### Brand recall and distinctive assets

- He says a skippable ad must show and say the brand inside the first five seconds, so that a viewer who skips still gives a free impression.
- He says the Super Saver film opens on a Zepto bag in the first frame and says the product name at about four seconds, then repeats it several times.
- He says a brand must keep a strict and distinct colour, font and tone of voice, or its work will be credited to someone else.
- He says the test is whether people can recall the brand without the logo, from colour and font alone.
- He says Zepto uses one fixed hex code, #7C1CC0, and that everyone on his brand team knows it by heart.
- He says colour is first for recall, and that emotion is better carried by tone of voice than by the colour itself.
- His example of misattribution is a viral reel where delivery riders in purple shirts were taken for Zepto riders, though they belonged to another company.
- He says both brand recall and message recall have to be checked for every creative, and a creative that fails either has no point.

### Celebrities

- He says to choose a celebrity for the personality trait the audience already attaches to them, and to ask what the public knows of that star.
- He says Akshay Kumar was chosen for Super Saver because the script was comic and he comes across as trustworthy and careful about money.
- He says the film worked partly because it showed the star as a neighbour-next-door comic figure, which other brands had not done in recent years.
- He says stars know what their fans will accept, and tells of a star who refused a script because his fans would not like the way a character treated him.
- He says the Telugu version with a regional star outperformed the Hindi one in its own market.
- He says a standard celebrity contract is one day of shooting, meaning eight hours, plus one year of association, and that days and years can be negotiated.
- He says recreating a film character needs permission from the producer, who owns the rights, not from the star.

### Languages and regions

- He says most of north, west and east India can be reached with a Hindi creative, because those audiences understand Hindi even when they speak another language at home.
- He says Bangalore has no single language a brand can pick. He puts Kannada at about a quarter of the audience there.
- He says never to dub or translate a Hindi creative for Tamil Nadu. A plain hoarding is better than a translated film.
- His rule is "don't translate, adapt", and he names the film Pushpa as a case where the Hindi version was adapted and not translated.
- He says a qualitative study in Chennai found that the brand was seen as an aloof north Indian company, which led Zomato to shoot an original Tamil film full of local detail.
- He says that Tamil film lifted orders and new users, but one film is not enough and it has to be repeated.

### The funnel and measurement

- He names the funnel as top, middle and bottom, which he also reads as awareness, consideration and purchase, with loyalty after.
- He says every creative should be written for one part of the funnel as its primary goal, though any creative does some of both awareness and consideration.
- He says a new service has to create awareness first, by showing that it exists and why someone should install it, before it talks about discounts.
- He says digital campaigns can be read right away through click and view-through rates, while TV has to be measured through surveys.
- He says a quantitative study must be large enough to be statistically significant, and that such a survey costs his team roughly 6 to 10 lakh rupees.
- He says qualitative research gives a laundry list of what people feel, and quantitative research ranks those points by how many people hold them.
- He says the purpose of research is to find the insight, and that marketing should be built on insight and not only on intuition.
- He says a traditional campaign takes about three months: a month of research for the brief, a month or more with the agency, and about three weeks of production.
- He says his in-house team can write a film in two to three days and have it produced in about ten days.
- He says a creative is finished when people have understood the message and the business has moved. He gives one and a half to two months as the minimum run and six to nine months as more than enough before fatigue.

### Light buyers, reach and where he disagrees with Byron Sharp

- Citing Byron Sharp's How Brands Grow, he says not to overlook the "sometimes" customer.
- He says that on a platform about 30% of customers bring about 70% of orders, and that the larger growth lever is the other 70% of customers who bring only 30%.
- He says the heavy users should be retained through good support and refund practices, since there is little more to get from them.
- He says refund generosity can be set by a user score blended from refund requests, cancellations, cash-on-delivery use, fraud and coupon use.
- He says broad reach beats going deep into one user group, and that awareness among more people should be the goal.
- He describes mental availability as memory structures and physical availability as distribution: the brand should be easy to recall and easy to buy.
- He repeats the rule that share of voice drives share of market, and says Zepto has the largest share of voice in its category on a small brand budget.
- He disagrees with Sharp's view that brand loyalty is overstated. He says India is an emotional and aspirational market where loyalty is real.
- He defines loyalty as using one brand more often than its rivals, not using it alone.
- He says people get used to an app's interface and find it hard to switch, which he calls "UI marriage".
- His position is to reach more people more often, and also make a few people fall in love with the brand, because that compounds into a tribe.
- Citing Binet and Field, he says a brand has to balance long-term brand building with short-term sales activation.

### Building a brand

- He gives the three pillars of brand building as content, community and consistency.
- He says brand building is consistency multiplied by frequency, and names Amul as a brand whose creatives have looked the same for decades.
- He says a brand is working when people pick the pack without turning it over to check the price.
- He says to start with a functional benefit and build the emotional one on top, because a product with only an emotional appeal does not get used or bought again.
- He says a new entrant usually wins by offering a functional benefit that the incumbent has ignored. It then either gets acquired or becomes credible competition with a small share of a big market.
- He says most B2C companies have one hero product, which he puts at 40 to 50% of sales for a big company and about 90% for a small one.
- He says a company can keep adding products as long as one thread ties them together, and anything outside that thread needs a separate brand.
- He says a tribe is the extreme form of a strong brand, where customers feel personal pride in using it. His examples are Nike, Red Bull and The Whole Truth.
- Citing David Aaker, he says brand equity needs patience and vision.

### Content and earned media

- He says brands and marketers are now content creators and publishers, and should think the way a creator thinks about trends.
- He gives the 4E model: every piece of content should educate, entertain, empathise or enrage.
- Citing Seth Godin, he describes permission marketing as content that customers ask to receive, and gives Zomato's emails as his example.
- Citing Joe Pulizzi, he says marketing should not feel like marketing to the customer.
- He says content can be useful and only loosely tied to the product. His example is a Tata Steel site that gives home builders design ideas and lists contractors.
- He says that site gives value to the contractor as well as the home owner, and the contractor is the one who recommends the steel.
- He defines earned media as customers sharing a brand's creative on their own feeds and groups, which is free and more trusted.
- He teaches Jonah Berger's STEPPS from the book Contagious: social currency, triggers, emotion, public, practical value and stories. He asks for at least one in every creative, and two or three if possible.
- He says people share a clever creative because getting the joke earns them validation from friends.
- He says Zepto puts up a hoarding mainly to photograph it and take it to social media, since few people see the physical board.
- His other earned-media examples are city-specific copy for a city launch, two brands replying to each other, and copy on tissues, sugar sachets and delivery boxes.
- He describes halo marketing as naming the category's best-known product instead of the category. His example is a Zepto Diwali film about soan papdi instead of "sweets".
- He says the soan papdi film worked as an underdog story, which is why viewers did not skip it.
- He separates hard-working creatives, which state the product from the first second and nobody shares, from share-worthy ones. He says a company that is just starting needs only the first kind.
- He says frequency can be built by tying the brand to a frequent occasion. Zomato chose cravings, because cravings come several times a day and are not the same as hunger.

### In-house team, agencies and channels for a young company

- He says a young company's first goal is conversions: get early adopters, get feedback, improve the product, and only then build the brand.
- He says not to hand an agency money in the first days, and that the founder should run the brand's content personally until there is scale.
- He says an in-house creative team is cheaper and faster when the volume of work is high, and an agency is cheaper when the work is occasional.
- He says an in-house team also builds culture and brings ideas an outside agency never would.
- He advises hiring creative people and giving the marketing lead meaningful equity, because that person holds the brand's tone and consistency.
- He says content has to be kept up for years, so it must be built on a low-cost model or it will be stopped and the earlier spend wasted.
- He says agencies work for awards and their interests are not the brand's, and he advises against hiring marketers who lead with their awards.
- He says to make the agency repeat the brief back, and to re-read the brief before approving a script.
- He says short-form video has replaced long-form. He recommends YouTube Shorts for a tier 2 and tier 3 audience and Instagram for a metro and tier 1 audience.
- He says the same reel does not perform on both platforms because their recommendation systems differ.
- He says a company at cold start must get LinkedIn right, because early adopters, investors and future employees are all there.
- He says people buy from founders whose face they know. His example is Nikhil Kamath's podcast building trust in Zerodha without talking about Zerodha.
- He says a founder's face is a brand asset that costs nothing to build.

## Examples and cases used

- Zomato film with Hrithik Roshan and Katrina Kaif: an on-time delivery brief that turned into an emotional film with no business impact.
- Zomato themed-days campaign: asked for daily ordering against the belief that outside food is occasional, across seven creatives.
- Zepto Super Saver with Akshay Kumar: agrees with the viewer's belief, then asks for one small action.
- Amazon Fresh ad with Manoj Bajpayee: played as an earlier ad on the same price theme, which the class did not find convincing.
- Zepto horse film: a clever idea that was dropped for a plain functional film.
- Bisleri: a market leader that had to grow from an already leading position.
- Amul: decades of consistent topical creatives in a commodity category.
- Colgate: an incumbent that keeps adding functional variants.
- Tata Steel home-building content site: content for the end user that also wins over the contractor who recommends the product.
- Nike, Red Bull, The Whole Truth: brands that became tribes.
- Zerodha and Groww: trust built through a founder's podcast and through educational video.
- Zomato cravings campaign: one script produced in many city versions, with dish and restaurant changed by AI lip-sync.
- Zomato Tamil film: an original local film made after research showed the brand felt distant in Chennai.
- Pushpa: adapted for Hindi audiences and not translated.
- Zepto soan papdi Diwali film: halo marketing through an underdog story.
- Ariel Share the Load: his example of an emotional campaign people remember.

## Numbers given

- 3 to 4 crore rupees: his figure for producing the two Zomato celebrity films, including the stars' fees.
- 10 to 12 crore rupees: his figure for distributing those two films.
- 30 to 40 lakh rupees: what he says the dropped Zepto horse film cost, against 7 to 10 lakh for the functional film that replaced it.
- 2 to 4 crore rupees: the range he quotes for a film star on a one-day shoot with a one-year association.
- 6 to 10 lakh rupees: his cost for one statistically significant quantitative study.
- About 3 months: a traditional campaign from research to finished film, against 2 to 3 days of writing and about 10 days of production in-house.
- 30% of customers bringing 70% of orders: his description of a typical platform.
- 40 to 50% and about 90%: the share of sales he says one hero product brings for a large and a small B2C company.
