# Defining the customer and their tension, Parvesh Debuka

Parvesh Debuka opened a day-long marketing bootcamp with the first of several recorded parts. He introduced himself as someone who began in research, moved to sales and marketing, worked on Paper Boat, and now runs a pizzeria and a kids' area in Goa. After a round of introductions he defined go-to-market, gave a template for describing the customer, and had each founder write down the tension their customer feels. Most of the session was him coaching founders through that exercise. This note keeps only the general teaching.

## What was said

### What go-to-market is

- Parvesh Debuka says go-to-market should be seen as the whole customer journey and not only acquisition of the first customers.
- He says a common mistake is to confuse go-to-market with marketing.
- He describes go-to-market as the bridge between the product a founder creates and how the world discovers it, trusts it and keeps coming back to it.
- He says a good product that few people found and an average product that was everywhere both show a go-to-market gap or a go-to-market win.
- He says a founder should lose sleep over three questions: who the customers are, how they will discover the product, and why they will buy it.

### Defining the customer

- He says "if you're selling to everyone, then you're not selling to anyone".
- He says a sharp customer definition goes beyond demographics to mindset, tension and beliefs.
- His template has four parts: a type of person, who is feeling an emotion, because of a tension, and who holds a belief.
- His worked example is Mamaearth: a new mom who is feeling anxious about chemicals in baby products and believes that natural ingredients are safer.
- He says the belief has to sit close to the category, so that it fuels buying behaviour in that category.
- When a founder sells through an organisation, he says to look for the individual who actually has the problem, because the problem belongs to a person and not to the organisation.

### The three levels of tension

- He separates a generic want, a category tension and a sharp emotional tension.
- His example of a generic want is that people want to eat healthy. He says it is true but too big to build on.
- His example of the next level is that healthy food is usually boring or expensive.
- He says staying focused on product and features is good, but the real value is unlocked when the founder gets to the emotional tension, because having or not having something affects how people feel about their day.
- He says the question to ask is what is really upsetting people about the options available to them.
- He says an internal tension like this is where a space opens up for a brand to grow.
- He says the tension is not something a founder articulates at once. It takes many drafts.

### Writing it down and pinning it to one thing

- He asks founders to pick one tension and not list two or three, because any room left for ambiguity grows as the work moves on.
- He says writing the statement down matters because people can easily fool themselves when they are only talking.
- He says a founder should check that the tension is widespread, or they end up attacking something too small or too niche.
- He warns founders against being biased by what they are already selling when they describe what the customer wants.
- He says working backwards from a built product through this framework is a useful exercise. The founder either ends up fully sure of what they are doing or stops to reconsider.

### Where the insight comes from

- He says a hypothesis should ideally come from conversations with people. The other route is to start with a strong personal feeling and then test it with about 20 other people.
- He says a product has to come out of an insight, which can be a personal experience or come from talking to people.
- He tells founders never to conclude that a tension does not exist in a category. The better mindset is that it exists and has to be looked for.
- To a founder who argues that nobody asked for electric vehicles, he answers that they came from a cultural want to be a better citizen of the planet, and that a carmaker's CEO thinking about what customers will care about next would see it as a want.
- He says the notion that people do not know what they want is true, but holding only that view stops a founder from trying to understand what people want.
- He says the exercise is to take a high level want and boil it down to how the product fits in.

### When the tension is not enough

- He says Paper Boat ran into a huge problem of not having enough consumers willing to pay a premium for a better product.
- He says Paper Boat launched at 30 and 35 rupees, then had to pivot, which led to Swing, a 10 rupee brand.
- He says if the tension in a category is not large enough or is already being addressed, the founder's next question is how else to enter and create differentiation, which is a positioning question.
- He says if customers do not care about what the founder offers, educating them is possible but adds a gestation period of a year or two before uptake grows.

## Examples and cases used

- Mamaearth: his worked example of the customer template, a new mom anxious about chemicals who believes natural ingredients are safer.
- Cult: the tension was wanting to get fit but finding the gym boring and monotonous. Cult answered with a choice of classes such as boxing, dance and sports.
- Paper Boat: the tension was being away from home, tired of unhealthy drink choices and feeling bad every time one picked a carbonated drink. He says very few non-carbonated brands existed when it launched in 2013.
- Coaching classes for entrance exams: weekly tests get students used to the big day, an idea he and a founder in the room discuss as missing in sports training.
- His own pizzeria: he had stopped eating pizza, tried a Neapolitan pizza, asked people around him, found many had also stopped eating pizza for similar reasons, and launched on that insight.

## Numbers given

- 30 and 35 rupees: Paper Boat's launch price points, in Parvesh Debuka's telling.
- 10 rupees: the price of Swing, the brand that came out of Paper Boat's pivot.
- About 20 people: the number he suggests testing a strongly felt hypothesis with.

## Not covered

- How customers discover the product, conversion and pricing were listed as parts of go-to-market but not reached in this part.
- Meta ads and other tactical questions were set aside for one-on-one conversations.
- Belief statements and positioning statements were covered in later parts of the same day.
