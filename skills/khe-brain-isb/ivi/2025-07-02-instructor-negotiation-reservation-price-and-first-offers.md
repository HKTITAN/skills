# Negotiation, reservation price, target price and first offers

This is the first half of the second class in the negotiation course, taught by a faculty member who is not named in the recording. She debriefed the one issue coffee contract simulation from the previous class and used it to teach how to treat your alternative, your reservation price and your target, how to gather information, and how to make and resist first offers. The file also carries a classmate's written notes, used below only where marked.

## What was said

### Alternatives and reservation price

- The instructor says not to use your best alternative as a threat, and not to reveal it unless towards the end of the negotiation.
- She says the goal is not to beat your partner in the negotiation.
- She says the reservation price was handed to students in the simulation, while in real life you do the homework yourself, weighing the other bids you hold and their quality.
- She says not to ask the other party for its reservation price.
- She says the wrong mindset is to enter a negotiation aiming only to clear your reservation price.
- She says a negotiator needs another number in mind that keeps them motivated to ask for more even after the reservation price is met.
- She says the goal in a distributive negotiation is to get as much as you can.
- She sums up the preparation as: think about your alternative, know your reservation price and anchor yourself at your target price.
- She says that across six years of teaching negotiation, the tendency she sees in most students is to focus too much on just getting past the reservation price.

### When the first offer looks too good

- She says that where both sides expect negotiation, you still negotiate after a good first offer. You can say the offer looks good and that there are other concerns to talk about.
- She asks the class how they would feel if their offer were accepted immediately, to show why instant acceptance is a problem.
- She says an offer that good can also mean you did not do your homework.
- She says a business negotiation should always have multiple issues.

### Homework and information

- She says "Homework is the key", meaning talking to people and looking at data.
- She says people feel there is no data, but there is always some data.
- She says you learn what comparable deals look like by talking to people in your network and to people who have done it before.
- She says some information is public. Her example is a country where employers must post the salary for a role, which a job seeker who thought there was no benchmark could have used.
- She says it is also important to know the other party as people, and that useful information can come from someone similar to them.
- She says the preparation has to be organised in a planning document.
- Her example is a person choosing between job offers who, once made to write down what mattered, realised location was the most important issue.
- She says the more issues you can add to the table that at least one party cares about, the better.

### Making the first offer

- She says you should make the first offer if you are prepared.
- She cites an anchoring study from the book Negotiating Rationally, in which people's estimates stayed close to the numbers they had been shown.
- She says there is an extremely high correlation between the first offer and the final outcome.
- She says everyone has the midpoint tendency because it just feels fair, so the aim is to pull the midpoint towards your side.
- She says to keep bringing the other party back to your first offer, because the longer a number is talked about the stronger the anchor.

### Making an offer persuasive

- She says an aggressive offer is made legitimate by quantifying each element of it and breaking it down further if needed.
- She says you show your homework in place of saying "this is my best offer".
- She says coherent stories are very important, because a coherent story is believed.
- On being asked for a previous salary that is low, she says to talk about the other things first and to say the old figure does not matter in the present context.

### Concessions

- She says never to give a large concession, even when an aggressive first offer has left you plenty of room.

### Handling the other side's first offer

- She says one response to an aggressive offer is to ask the other side to justify it, which exposes an offer that has no legitimacy.
- She says another response is to make them an offer so good they cannot refuse, one that is not similar to their offer.
- She says ridiculous offers without legitimacy can backfire on the person making them.

### From the written class notes in the file

- The notes record that even if your alternative is weak, the other side's may be weak too.
- The notes list three assessments to prepare: of yourself, of the other party and of the situation.
- The notes record that a weak anchor needs a background given with it.
- The notes record that the way to neutralise a strong first offer is to focus on evidence inconsistent with it.
- The notes record that a no deal feels like a loss.

## Examples and cases used

- A coffee supply contract simulation: the one issue buyer and seller exercise whose results she debriefs.
- Negotiating Rationally: the book she cites for the anchoring study and recommends from the library.
- Salary negotiations: several stories of students and candidates, used to show benchmarks, anchors and how to handle a low previous salary.
