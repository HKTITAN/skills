# Metrics and go-to-market

A faculty instructor ran this session of the Design Thinking and Innovation course. It picked up after the MVP work from earlier sessions and covered two things: how to choose what to measure, and how to think about go-to-market. The instructor lectured, ran a short exercise where founders wrote goals and actions for one feature, and took questions throughout. The instructor said several times that the aim was to give principles, not tools or advice for any one startup.

## What was said

### Why the choice of metric matters

- The instructor says metrics are the instruments that drive a company towards success or failure, and that measuring the wrong thing does more harm than it gives value.
- The instructor says picking a metric can change the culture of a company completely, because people act on whatever they are measured on.
- The instructor says a company can chase a reasonable metric and still get into trouble if it ignores what the metric leaves out, such as customer service.

### What counts as a metric

- The instructor defines a metric as something collectible, quantifiable and measurable that tells you about the performance of the product or of one aspect of it, tracked over time.
- The instructor says data collection has to be built into the product, so that data is gathered while the usage is happening and not by a survey afterwards.
- The instructor says that if you only collect data after the event you can only measure lagging indicators, so you learn how you did when it is too late to change it.
- The instructor says revenue is always a lagging indicator because it comes after the sale, while how often people reach the checkout page is a leading indicator.
- The instructor says leading indicators tell you whether you will hit the lagging target, which lets you intervene with customers before the result is fixed.
- The instructor says a measure only counts as a metric when it relates to the performance of the product, and uses a doctor taking your temperature but not your height as the comparison.
- The instructor says granular measures beat aggregate ones, because an aggregate such as revenue cannot tell you which aspect of the product caused it.
- The instructor says performance over time is more useful than a point in time: 100 users today says little, 100 users consistently over a month is a good sign.

### The GAME framework

- The instructor teaches the GAME framework: Goals, Actions, Metrics, Evaluations.
- The instructor calls GAME a bottom-up, action-based framework that starts from the goal of the user, and says it suits a startup at the MVP stage.
- The instructor contrasts it with the North Star metric framework, which is top-down and starts from the goal of the business, then asks what to measure, then what activities move those measures. The two converge in the end.
- Goals are what the user is trying to get done with the product, which the instructor ties back to jobs to be done. The business also has strategic and tactical goals.
- Actions are what the user has to do in the product to reach the goal. The instructor says you measure these activities, not the goal itself.
- The instructor asks founders to picture the product as a box: the user puts in needs and effort, value comes out, and the actions in between are what you can measure, including how often, how deeply and how long it took to reach value.
- Evaluation means asking whether a measure truly represents the health of the business, including whether more of it is better or less of it is better.
- The instructor's example for evaluation is hours spent: a productivity tool wants fewer hours spent in it, a social platform wants more.
- For the exercise, the instructor tells founders to pick one product or one feature, not the whole company, because working at the aggregate level causes confusion.

### Actions: acquisition, activation, retention, monetisation

- The instructor groups user actions with the AARM framework: acquisition, activation, retention and engagement, and monetisation.
- Acquisition is the set of actions by which a user discovers the product and becomes a user.
- The instructor says activation is the step most people miss, and that a person arriving on the platform is not enough.
- The instructor defines an active user as someone who has derived value from the product at least once in a given time frame.
- The instructor says the definition of an active user must be specific to the product, because usage cycles differ. A quarterly reporting tool cannot be judged on daily use.
- With a pressure cooker as the example, the instructor says buying is acquisition and cooking with it for the first time is activation.
- The instructor says you cannot retain someone who is not an engaged, active user.
- The instructor says that when a product cannot be instrumented, you are left with after-the-fact measures, and that founders should measure during use whenever they can.
- The instructor says retention is hardest when the buyer and the user are different people, as in B2B or parents paying for children, because both must be kept happy.
- The instructor says a business software vendor has to satisfy the buyer on cost, security and certification and the employees on ease of use, or the contract is not renewed.
- The instructor says users can pay in forms other than money, such as time, data or referrals, and that Google search matters to Google because of the data it gives for ads.
- The instructor says a platform with several sides has to define actions and measures separately for each side, then aggregate at the platform level.

### Ways to measure an action

- The instructor says an action can be measured directly or by proxy. Page views, add to cart and purchases are direct. Whether someone watched a video is a proxy, inferred from how long the frame stayed on screen.
- The instructor says measures can be individual or aggregate, and can be magnitudes or ratios such as actions per user per day.
- The instructor separates intrinsic measures, which are read straight off a count, from heuristic measures, which combine several intrinsic measures by a formula.
- For monthly-subscription B2B SaaS, the instructor says the most useful heuristic is the number of paying customers who were active in the last 30 days, because it predicts renewals.
- The instructor says engagement is a heuristic built from several other metrics, and that heuristics are usually aggregates but not all aggregates are heuristics.

### Who you sell to, and when value is created

- The instructor rejects the label B2B2C. You sell either to a consumer or to an institution, and if you sell to both you have two business models.
- The instructor says capturing data from the people who use a product does not mean you sell to them. That is telemetry, not sales.
- The instructor says a company selling to institutions still has to solve for the end user, because if users do not use it the institution will not renew.
- The instructor argues that value is created when the customer decides to buy, not at the sale itself, which is why companies can take a booking amount at that point.
- The instructor explains booked revenue as contracted revenue not yet realised, which companies report to show confidence in the business.
- The instructor says you make money at the point of value realisation, and that this point may be the sale, the usage or the renewal depending on the product.

### Go-to-market basics

- The instructor defines go-to-market as the most efficient and effective way to get your product into the hands of your customers.
- The instructor says every go-to-market plan answers four questions: who to target, how to reach them, where and when to target them, and how much to charge.
- The instructor says the simplest way to choose a marketing channel is to ask where the target customer is hanging out.
- The instructor says the revenue model is part of go-to-market, and that you must also understand your cost structure.
- The instructor says it is acceptable to plan to make money 18 months from now, and not acceptable to say you will get users first and work out the money later.
- The instructor says a revenue model answers five questions: who is paying, what is being paid, what they are paying for, how they pay, and how much.
- The instructor says a community only counts as go-to-market if there is a mechanism that converts its members into users of the product, so that it works as an acquisition channel.
- The instructor says only two companies, Google and Meta, have worked out how to monetise users at scale, and that doing so needs about a billion users.
- The instructor says unit economics matter more than anything else in a business and tells founders not to chase valuation without net profit.
- The instructor says that for digital products the biggest cost heads are customer acquisition and distribution, along with people costs.
- The instructor calculates customer acquisition cost as salaries, overheads, paid marketing and tools divided by the number of customers.
- The instructor gives 3 to 1 as the minimum ratio of lifetime value to customer acquisition cost.
- The instructor declined to teach pricing, saying it is a question for marketing and pricing specialists, and only noted that it rests on your cost, the willingness to pay and the margin between them.

### Product-led growth

- The instructor describes product-led growth as growth with no sales team: people discover the product, use it, keep using it and spread it.
- The instructor says product-led growth needs a product that is simple, intuitive and valuable without explanation. If someone has to explain it, product-led growth will not work.
- The instructor says product-led growth works well for B2C products and depends on being data driven so you can nudge user behaviour.
- The instructor defines the virality coefficient as how many new users arrive because of one existing user, and compares it to the R0 value from the pandemic.
- The instructor says a virality coefficient above 1 gives exponential growth, below 1 gives decay, and 1 is the sustaining level.
- The instructor lists four ways to drive virality: direct network effects, word of mouth, casual contact such as a sign-up link in an email signature, and incentives such as referrals.

### Sales-led growth

- The instructor says sales-led growth is for high-touch products where someone has to explain the value, teach the product and help integrate it.
- The instructor calls these value-based purchases and says sales-led growth is mainly a B2B strategy where relationships with the buyer matter.
- The instructor says a B2B sale involves several kinds of people, such as the buyer, the influencer and the user, each with different goals.

### Product-led sales

- The instructor describes product-led sales as a hybrid: use the product's stickiness with individual users to find which accounts can become institutional sales.
- The instructor says it has grown because acquisition channels are crowded and costly, buyers hesitate over tools their staff do not use, and end users have more say inside companies.
- The instructor calls the target customer a prosumer, a mix of professional and consumer, and says the product must solve the problem at three levels: individual, team and enterprise.
- The instructor says the strategy only works when you hold usage data on your power users. Without data it does not work.
- The instructor contrasts the classic funnel of marketing qualified leads and sales qualified leads with product qualified accounts, which are found from usage and then passed to marketing and sales.
- The instructor says the approach lets one salesperson talk only to the right accounts, where a sales-led company would need many.
- The instructor names the strategy land and expand, and also the Trojan horse strategy: enter through hero customers, make them champions, then expand.
- The instructor gives two drawbacks. It relies on an influencer inside the company who may leave, and it takes years to pay off, where sales-led and B2C sales bring money immediately.
- The instructor says B2C has volume but not margins, and that many consumer tools end up selling to businesses because that is where the money is.

### The tree picture of growth

- The instructor compares growth to raising a tree. Seeds and saplings are unscalable activities: sharing with friends, cold outreach, finding early adopters, viral content, physical adverts, hosting events.
- The trunk is the stage where growth starts to sustain itself, through SEO, sales, some virality and ads.
- The roots need water and sun, which for a product are users, data and cash.
- Nutrients are added at intervals once the tree is growing: conversion incentives, retention work, brand awareness, customer success and after-sales support.
- Pruning and grafting, meaning channel partners, new geographies and new categories, come only after the trunk is strong.
- The instructor showed a chart of how well-known companies found their first users and says all of them began with unscalable things.

### Attitude to growth

- The instructor says winning does not mean a 95% market share. A profitable company with a 20% share of a fast-growing market can be worth a great deal.
- The instructor says getting users is very hard and getting paying users is harder, so a plan for a million users in three months does not work.
- The instructor says choosing to be an entrepreneur means choosing to enjoy the process and not the outcome.
- The instructor says there is no single go-to-market strategy. It has to keep changing as the product and the customers change.

## Examples and cases used

- Wells Fargo: staff were measured on the number of accounts created per person, so employees themselves opened fake accounts.
- An airline overbooking incident: the airline was optimising how full its flights were and neglected customer service.
- Facebook: with engagement as the number one metric, the company ran an experiment showing unhappy content to people who were already sad, which the instructor calls an ethical problem.
- Airbnb: its retention metric is yearly booked nights per guest and weekly booked nights per host, the same action measured at different frequencies for different personas.
- Instagram: a view is a proxy metric based on how long the frame stays on screen, while a swipe, a like or a subscribe is a direct metric.
- Connected fridges and water filters: sensors report back to the maker, which shows physical products can be instrumented too.
- A pilgrimage service for senior citizens paid for by their children abroad: the user and the payer want different things.
- Cloud companies: the instructor's illustration is a 30 million dollar deal over six years, with 5 million recognised this year and the rest reported as booked revenue.
- Dropbox: public folders in 2010 made recipients create an account to see shared files, and its referral scheme gave storage to both sides.
- WhatsApp and Skype: direct network effects, since you can only reach people who are on the same service.
- SAP: a sales-led product whose value is only realised once it is integrated, and which created the SAP consultant as a profession.
- Figma: started with individual designers, spread to their teams, then sales used usage data to tell executives how many teams already used it and offer an enterprise deal.
- Tableau: free download for analysts, a community and a championship event, then sales went only to companies with heavy usage and turned analysts into hero customers. The instructor says this love for the product is why Salesforce acquired it.
- Miro and Canva: named as following the same route from individual use to business sales.
- Excel: the instructor argues people buy Microsoft Office for Excel.

## Numbers given

- 3 to 1: the instructor's minimum ratio of lifetime value to customer acquisition cost.
- About a billion users: what the instructor says is needed to monetise users at scale.
- Virality coefficient of 1: the instructor's threshold between decaying and exponential growth.
- 20% market share in a rapidly growing market: the instructor's example of winning without dominance.

## Not covered

- The North Star metric framework in detail. The instructor offered to send notes on it.
- Analytics tools. The instructor chose to teach principles and leave tools out.
- Pricing methods. The instructor referred founders to marketing faculty.
