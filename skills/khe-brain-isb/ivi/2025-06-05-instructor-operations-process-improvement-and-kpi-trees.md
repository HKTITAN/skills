# Operations class on process improvement, value indicators and KPI trees

A class in the operations and supply chain course, taught by an ISB instructor who is not named in the recording and who earlier headed business process excellence at the World Bank. The class covered Kaizen and Kaikaku, a readiness check before any change, a group exercise on value indicators, and how to turn the voice of the customer into KPIs.

## What was said

### Differentiation and speed

- The instructor says a differentiator has to be consciously created, so that the customer sees only you among a hundred others.
- He gives two ways to create one. Bring a new product that never existed in the market, or enter an existing market and give the customer a new reason to come to you.
- He says that in both cases you must work to make the differentiator clear in the customer's mind, or you are lost in the crowd.
- He says that when many teams are building the same thing at once, such as legal LLMs or health apps, speed to market matters most.
- He says the advantage goes to the first to scale the market, not the first to enter it, and calls this a fast mover advantage.
- He says differentiation analysis matters a few months later, when the existing customers scatter across ten providers, and not on day one.

### Kaizen and Kaikaku

- The instructor says there are two ways to improve a process. Kaizen is continuous improvement and Kaikaku is breakthrough improvement.
- His example is an app where payment takes 30 seconds. Cutting it to 3 seconds is Kaikaku, and cutting it to 25 seconds is Kaizen.
- He says a chain of small Kaizen projects can also reach 3 seconds, but it takes time.
- He says Kaikaku makes change management hard, because employees whose comfort zone is broken agree in front of the boss and then show passive resistance.
- He says Kaizen meets much lower resistance, needs little effort and is less expensive, often just a small tweak in the process.
- He says Kaizen lets every employee run an improvement in turn, which gives a sense of participation and accomplishment and builds culture.
- He says Kaikaku gives visibility in the market and a significant gain that management can see.
- He says Kaikaku is hard to imitate, because by the time competitors copy one breakthrough you can deliver another.
- He says a young firm that can take risks, has capital for trial and error and wants to disrupt suits Kaikaku, and a firm investing over years in culture suits Kaizen.

### Readiness before change

- The instructor says to do a readiness assessment before either approach, and compares skipping it to lifting a 50 kilo dumbbell on the first day at the gym.
- He frames readiness as a triangle of people, process and technology, and adds asking whether you can afford the change.
- He says people readiness covers how you will communicate the change, how often, how you will know people are embracing it, and the motivation systems.
- He says process readiness asks whether current processes are compatible with the change. If they are not, improve the process before starting the digital journey.
- He says technology readiness asks whether the new technology integrates with and is compatible with what already exists.
- He says adding a generative AI layer on top of predictive AI systems without a compatibility check creates a new problem of data leaking from the company.
- He says acceptability testing is part of change management. Put the change in front of people and give them a reason to try it, because they have to taste the honey first.
- He says nothing done in management promises results. It only increases the likelihood of success.
- He says you can never make 100% of people ready, and most people being ready is what is needed.
- He says that if you launched without people readiness, you should accept that some people will leave, and start looking for new talent.
- He says to turn tacit knowledge into explicit knowledge before people leave, for example with a one to two week knowledge management hackathon where everyone documents how they work.
- For change that depends on outside parties he says to plan a communication strategy, such as workshops, mailers and competitions.

### Value indicators

- The instructor defines a value indicator as the reason customers in my market segment come to me and not to my competitors.
- He says a value indicator is defined from the customer's side. It is not a feature of the process, and it is not an outcome such as delight or experience.
- He says premium experience cannot be a value indicator, because it only says which market segment you are in.
- His exercise is to name two key competitors, name two value indicators, and decide what operations decisions would strengthen each indicator.
- He separates variety from customisation. Variety is a longer menu and can be standardised, while customisation is something created for me after understanding where I stand.
- He says to judge how realistic each operations decision is by its impact, for example whether it would bring another million customers.
- He tells founders not to defend their first answer in class, because defending closes the feedback loops, and more critique means you are more ready for the market.

### Why big players are hard to copy

- Asked why rivals do not simply copy a successful firm, the instructor describes what he calls a cannibalisation strategy.
- In his telling a giant gives the service away at a loss, funded by its other businesses, until rivals go bankrupt, and then raises the price or lowers quality when customers have no choice.
- He uses Jio's entry into telecom as the example, and says the smaller players saw it was about survival and not market share.
- He says a firm that owns the whole market then starts influencing policy and regulators.
- He says that if you lack a strong value indicator and have no time to create one, you can form a strategic alliance with a successful firm from another industry.

### Voice of the customer

- The instructor says customers will not simply tell you what they want when you ask through surveys, interviews, focus groups, observation, complaints and reviews.
- He says the voice of the customer is corrupted by how the customer understands the question and the product.
- He says customer needs are of two types. Explicit needs are ones the customer feels and can state without being asked.
- He says the usual research methods capture only explicit needs.
- He says hidden needs are ones the customer does not know of or cannot put into words.
- He defines a need as something that, once made into a product or service and put in front of you, you are ready to buy.
- He says design thinking helps find hidden needs, and that test marketing is used for them in large organisations.
- He says the camera phone came from empathising with customers' new curiosity about handling a camera as digital photography arrived.

### From vague needs to KPIs

- The instructor says the voice of the customer is generally vague, so it cannot be handed to production as a requirement.
- He says a customer need is not the words alone. It is also about ego, emotion, aspiration, timing and context.
- He says to find the intent behind what customers said, which he calls the drivers of the need.
- He says to keep breaking a need down until a worker on the floor can understand it without any subjectivity.
- He gives four properties that show the vagueness is gone. The instruction is clear to understand, not dependent on the person, measurable and comparable.
- He says a statement with these four properties is a key performance indicator, and the tool that gets you there is the KPI tree.
- He says the purpose of a KPI tree is to remove vagueness from the voice of the customer.
- He says to learn whether customers are happy you should first ask the process, by measuring the KPIs periodically, before asking the customer.
- He says not to assume KPIs. Uber should not assume safety is its KPI, and Uber's KPIs will differ from Ola's because their value indicators differ.
- He says that with wrong KPIs the dashboard shows green all the time while market share does not improve, and that with the right KPIs the dashboard would be full of red.
- He says choosing KPIs is not an operational problem but one with strategic impact, and that he teaches this tool to senior executives too.
- He says some KPIs can perform badly without affecting market share, so the next step is to prioritise KPIs.

## Examples and cases used

- World Bank: two large digital projects worked in silos, the monthly feed from HR to finance stopped, and no employee was paid. The fix was an interfacing technology that cost more than both projects.
- Aadhaar: his case of change management at the scale of a country, where every citizen was given a reason to join.
- Creamstone, Ibaco and Baskin Robbins: Creamstone's value indicators are signature seasonal real fruit ice creams and ice cream made just in time in front of the customer, Ibaco offers variety, and Baskin Robbins offers neither just in time nor customisation.
- Jio: free data at a loss until rivals could not survive.
- Citibank and Indian Oil: a co-branded card that borrowed a reason to buy from another industry.
- Samsung camera phone: asked 20 years ago, customers would have said they did not need a camera in a phone, yet they paid extra when it was sold.
- Hospital outpatient waiting: a 10 o'clock appointment that starts at 10.45 is an explicit need customers complain about.
- A car showroom: buyers ask for mileage, legroom and service, which production cannot act on as stated.
- A bakery: great donuts became fresh donuts and then a schedule of 25 donuts at 10am, 1pm, 5pm and 9pm in place of 100 in the morning.
- Swiggy: one service that meets different customers' needs for speed, discounts, quality and variety.
