# Japanese investors and India, Satoshi Kuriga

Satoshi Kuriga of Reazon, a Japanese group that builds its own ventures and invests in startups, spoke to the cohort about two things: bootstrapping versus external capital, and how Japanese investors look at India. He gave a short talk and then took questions from founders.

## What was said

### Who was speaking

- Satoshi Kuriga says Reazon Holdings does both venture building and investment.
- He says its own businesses include a food delivery platform, and that it now invests a lot in robotics and AI as its own business.
- He says the group has focused on Southeast Asian investment for the past two to three years and holds more than 10 portfolio companies there.
- He says he came to India to learn the market, talk to players in the ecosystem and discuss a new accelerator programme.

### Bootstrapping versus external capital

- He says fundraising is not mandatory. His group started with an agency business that needed no outside capital.
- He says the profit from the agency business funded a riskier move into mobile gaming, and the gaming business then produced large cash.
- He says the food delivery platform was different, because it needed huge capital to compete with players such as Uber Eats.
- For that business the group raised 50 to 60 million US dollars from KDDI, which he describes as the second largest telecom player in Japan.
- He says the purpose was to accelerate that business, and KDDI's large customer base gave a strong synergy.
- His lesson is to ask whether you need external capital at all, what the purpose of the money is, and what type of investor it should come from.

### Fundraising is marketing

- He says fundraising is a kind of marketing: you tell investors what they want to know, and you choose how to deliver the message.
- He says this means first understanding what kind of investor you want to raise from.
- He compares investors to consumers, each with a different focus or preference.
- He says some investors follow short-term trends, and names AI, blockchain and SaaS as examples.
- He advises founders to find the right investor who believes in their story.

### Why Japanese investors look at India

- He says Japan's population is shrinking and its GDP has had zero growth for 30 years.
- He says Japanese corporations and investors do not believe in Japan's future growth, so many are expanding outside Japan.
- He says their usual first step is Southeast Asia, because it is close and people there like Japanese culture.
- He says Southeast Asia is not one market. Thailand, Indonesia and Vietnam are totally different markets, and each is much smaller than India or Japan.
- He says China is very big but too difficult to enter because of regulation and government interference.
- He says many Japanese corporations tried China over 30 years with fewer than five success cases, and names Uniqlo and Muji.
- He says the mindset is therefore that India is the next big market, though he does not think India is easy either.

### How to pitch a Japanese investor

- He says to show a bigger potential and a bigger future within India, because Japanese investors interested in India are focused on the Indian market.
- He says a founder does not need to insist that the company can win globally.
- He says Japanese people are conservative and their decision-making takes a very long time.
- He gives six months to sometimes one year for a Japanese corporation to make a startup investment, and three or four months for Japanese VCs.
- He says founders should therefore start communication with Japanese players early.
- He says to show the defence side as well as the upside: that you care about the risks and have good compliance and governance.

### Focus on one product in the pitch

- A founder asked how to pitch an Indian business with many SKUs and use cases to investors who want one focused product.
- He says not to change the SKUs but to change the communication style.
- He says to pick the best product, argue its potential, and then say you will expand to other SKUs afterwards.
- He says a market-size claim built on a wide range of products is too difficult for a foreign investor to judge.
- He says that from a Japanese or US investor's view, "focus is always good".

### Balance sheet money and holding period

- He says his group invests from its own balance sheet and is not a fund, so it can invest for the long term.
- He says a normal VC fund has a 10 year restriction, with about 12 years as the maximum.
- He says 10 years is not enough for developing markets such as India or Southeast Asia.
- He says his group can hold shares for 20 or 30 years.
- He says he has a private equity background and believes in long-term, stable success.
- Asked whether investments must be strategic, he says no. He describes the group as closer to a family office whose main goal is pure financial return.
- He says portfolio companies can get help from the group's engineers, designers and marketers and its Web3 and AI technology, but that support is not mandatory.
- He says the group does not mind whether a company enters Japan or stays focused on India.

### Rejection

- He says to talk to as many investors as possible, because some will like you and some will not.
- He says famous VCs declined to invest in Google and Facebook, so a VC's rejection does not mean much.
- He says a founder may face 100 or 200 declines and should not give up. He cites a raise where about 200 investors were contacted and 199 declined.

### Other questions

- He says the planned accelerator programme focuses on AI but is open to non-AI companies.
- He says AI is normally an enabler or a tool, unless a company has strong models of its own.
- On manufacturing, he says some big Japanese players already have factories in India, and he has not recently heard of new players investing.
- Asked which Indian sector excites him most, he says he is still figuring that out.

## Examples and cases used

- Reazon's own path: an agency business funded mobile gaming, and external capital came only for capital-heavy food delivery.
- KDDI: a strategic investor chosen for its customer base as well as its money.
- Uniqlo and Muji: his examples of the few Japanese successes in China.
- Google and Facebook: declined by famous VCs.

## Numbers given

- 50 to 60 million US dollars: raised from KDDI for the group's food delivery business.
- More than 10: the group's portfolio companies in Southeast Asia.
- 2 million US dollars: the group's minimum first ticket, with follow-on investment after it.
- Longer than 10 years: the group's expected holding period, with 20 to 30 years possible.
- 10 years, about 12 at most: the life of a normal VC fund, in his telling.
- 6 months to 1 year: time a Japanese corporation takes over a startup investment.
- 3 to 4 months: time a Japanese VC takes.
- 30 years: the period over which he says Japan's GDP has not grown.
- About 200 investors contacted and 199 declines: his example of persistence in a raise.

## Not covered

- No named Indian sectors or investment thesis. He says he is still learning the market.
- Valuation, deal terms and the structure the group uses when investing.
- How a founder gets introduced to Japanese investors beyond contacting him directly.
- Details of the accelerator programme, such as dates, size or terms.
