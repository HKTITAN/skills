# Term sheet clause by clause, Amit Khanna

Amit Khanna, an angel investor who invests through a family office, walked the cohort through a draft term sheet he had handed out, stopping at the clauses he had highlighted. It was his second session with the group, following a role play on valuation the evening before. He said several times that he is not a lawyer or a tax expert and was giving his own understanding. The second half was open questions.

## What was said

### Why the term sheet matters

- Amit Khanna says the term sheet is the most important document in raising capital after the shareholders agreement between co-founders, because the definitive agreements emerge from it.
- He says it is very hard to negotiate anything contrary to the term sheet at the definitive agreement stage.
- He tells founders not to save money on this document. They should hire a good lawyer who understands their short, medium and long term needs, and not copy and paste a term sheet from someone else.
- He says a term sheet is not a guaranteed finish line, and he has seen people walk away because there was no agreement on it.
- He tells founders to settle the contentious clauses in the term sheet and not leave them for the definitive documents, because those are the clauses on which deals fall apart.
- He says anything the founder wants, such as an ESOP pool or a founder salary, should be written into the term sheet.

### Instruments

- He says a founder is raising equity but will rarely see the word equity in a term sheet. Investors bring instruments that give them more security.
- He describes a convertible structure as one where the two sides cannot agree a value, so the investor comes in now and converts at the next round at a discount. His examples are a 30% or 40% haircut on the next round's valuation.
- He says the downside of a convertible structure for the company is that it cannot show the term sheet to anyone as proof of a valuation. The benefit is less dilution if the next round is at a much higher valuation.
- He says any institutional investor today will ask for an instrument better than plain equity, with preferential rights.
- He says optionally convertible redeemable preference shares are better for the investor than CCPS, because the investor keeps the option to stay a lender if the company does badly and convert if it does well.
- He says funding rounds are still mostly done in CCPS because the Indian government wants foreign capital to come in as equity and not as debt, so investors with a foreign holding structure can only use CCPS.
- He says he prefers an outcome where everyone carries equity risk, because founders want investors in the same boat when the company is sinking as when it is floating.

### Use of funds and matching investors

- He says the use of funds clause becomes a deployment sheet that gets coded into the agreements, and founders should give it serious thought.
- He says founders should work out how much they need and then approach investors whose cheque size matches it. His example is that one does not go to Peak XV to ask for 100,000 dollars.

### Board seats and observers

- He says one board seat for an investor is the usual recommendation for a startup, and it can become proportional at later stages.
- He gives about 10% ownership as the point where board seat discussions start, and says above 20 to 25% it becomes a proportional discussion.
- He says if having a person on the board gives the company a benefit and visibility, the founder should give the seat whatever the amount invested.
- He says a company should have a board of two to three people, four at most, before the investor arrives, and a startup board should not go beyond five or six people.
- He says the Companies Act has no concept of an observer. It is a role created by investors, with no filing, and at most the observer is mentioned in the minutes.
- He says investors increasingly want the right to appoint a director without using it, because of liability. Directors get sued when the company is sued, and in India director insurance does not remove criminal liability.
- His tactic for founders: when an investor asks for two board seats, offer one board seat and one observer seat.
- He says an investor's veto comes from two places. As a shareholder it comes through reserved matters in the shareholders agreement. On the board it comes through quorum, where an investor director must be present at the meeting.

### Pro rata, anti-dilution and lock-in

- He says smart investors always ask for a pro rata right so they can invest in the next round and not be diluted.
- He says an existing investor putting in more money reassures the market, and from the second or third round new investors always ask whether the old investors are investing again. If they are not, it can be a red flag.
- He suggests asking a prospective investor whether their fund has a mandate to put money into the next round as well.
- He says a promoter lock-in typically starts at three years, and founders should not negotiate it hard because doing so signals that they are in the mood to exit.
- He says dilution happens automatically in every round unless there is an anti-dilution clause, and such clauses are typically asked for by investors, not employees.
- He says ESOPs also typically come with a vesting schedule and a lock-in.

### ROFO, ROFR, tag and drag

- He explains a right of first offer as an obligation to go to existing investors first, before there is any outside offer or price discovery. The obligation ends when they say no.
- He explains a right of first refusal as going to the market first, getting a term sheet, then showing it to the existing investor, who can match or better it.
- He says investors prefer a right of first refusal because the market does the price discovery for them. A startup should prefer a right of first offer, so that its fundraising process is not undone at the end by the current investor.
- He says at an early stage the investor will usually get a right of first refusal, and that is acceptable because it gives sight of the next round's capital.
- He says a tag along right means that if the promoter sells shares after the lock-in, the investor can sell alongside, because investors do not want to stay in a company the founder has left.
- He says a drag along right lets an exiting investor take the promoter's shares along in the sale. It matters when a private equity buyer wants a majority or a strategic buyer wants the whole company.
- He says a promoter facing a drag along clause can negotiate a right of first refusal before the investor sells.

### Information rights and exit obligations

- He says investors will in practice ask for more information than the term sheet lists, and the founder will not refuse.
- He says the quality of the company's MIS helps it raise future rounds, and founders should learn which metrics their investors ask for and improve the MIS every quarter.
- He tells founders not to commit to helping an investor exit at a pre-agreed valuation, and to stay away from as many exit obligations as possible.
- He says equity is risk shared by everyone, and the money can go to zero or multiply a hundred times.
- He says a good lawyer words an IPO timeline as a best effort. His example is making the best effort to do an IPO in seven years.

### Conditions precedent, standstill, governing law, exclusivity and costs

- He says conditions precedent are the things an investor finds in diligence and wants fixed before the money comes in, such as IP not being registered in the company's name. Founders should agree only to those they can fulfil.
- He says a standstill provision stops the company from doing anything dramatically different between signing and funding, such as starting a new business line or replacing key management.
- He tells India-based founders to ask for Indian jurisdiction, because Singapore is a very expensive place to fight a dispute.
- He says if an investor insists on Singapore as a matter of fund policy, the founder should ask for the seat of arbitration to be in the founder's own Indian city.
- He says the dispute clause should first have the parties try to resolve the dispute themselves and then go to arbitration.
- He says investors ask for exclusivity because they are about to spend time and money on diligence. They typically ask for six months, and he considers two to four months sufficient.
- He says exclusivity does not stop a founder from bringing in co-investors to fill the round, as long as the lead investor is kept informed.
- He says co-investors rely on the lead investor's diligence and negotiated documents and do not get all the lead's rights. The founder has more bargaining strength with them.
- He says diligence is an investor cost and founders should push back if asked to pay for it. The worst outcome he would accept is the cost being borne by the company.
- His negotiating line on diligence fees is to offer to pay only if the diligence finds something contrary to what the founder represented.
- He says confidentiality starts at the term sheet and survives its termination.

### Director loans, dilution and discounts

- He says smart investors ask founders to convert their loans to the company into equity before a round, because they do not want their money used to repay the founder.
- He says taking a founder loan out gets harder with every round, so it should be dealt with early and declared in the use of funds.
- He explains why founders accept dilution with an example: a founder who sells 30% at a 100 crore valuation holds 70% of 100 crore, which is worth more than 100% of the undefined number before.
- He says when founders are diluted to single digits, new investors worry about skin in the game, and he has seen investors offer such founders 5 to 10% in ESOPs tied to KPIs over five years.
- For early angel money he suggests not fixing a valuation at all. The founder offers a discount on the next round's valuation, which buys time to create value before diluting.
- His own formula as an angel is a discount of 10 to 20% per year until the next round, and he says the founder can cap the total at a figure such as 30, 35 or 40%.
- He says an investor who fights for upfront equity instead of a discount believes strongly in the idea, and the founder should read that as a sign they do not want to walk away.

### Angels, friends and family

- He says negotiations with angels are different from those with institutions. An angel investing a few lakh does not do diligence because it would cost more than the investment.
- He says with angels the company leads with a condensed version of the term sheet and usually offers a discount to a future round.
- He says angels pool money into a fund so that diligence becomes economical.
- For friends and family he recommends a stripped down term sheet with no board seats and no ROFR, ROFO or tag rights, and information rights limited to annual accounts.
- He tells founders to present the investment to friends and family as a high risk equity investment, and to tell them to put in only as much as they are willing to lose.
- He says the terms must be clear with friends and family whether or not they are written down.

### How to treat investors

- He says capital is a commodity and good ideas are few, so founders should negotiate hard. He calls the right posture "respectful aggression".
- He says an investor sitting across the table to negotiate already likes something about the founder and the company, so the founder does not need to be defensive.
- He says fundraising is a chain reaction. The founder has to create competition and find the right lead investor, and the others follow.
- He tells founders to raise capital before they need it, because there will be down cycles in the market and setbacks in the company.
- He tells founders to do diligence on their investors, angels included, by calling the companies they have invested in and asking how they behave on the board and at exit.
- He tells founders to ask every investor how they can help the company grow apart from capital.
- He encourages founders to email investors every quarter with the next quarter's target and specific requests, such as access to experts, help sourcing talent or an introduction to another portfolio company.
- He tells of founders who used a distress sale to wipe out their investors while keeping a stake themselves, and says such founders may never raise capital again. He tells the cohort a founder needs a strong reference when a future investor calls the earlier ones.
- For buying another company without spending cash, he suggests a share swap, where the acquired company's promoters take shares in the parent. He says the swap also sets a valuation the founder can show future investors.

## Examples and cases used

- Peak XV: a founder does not approach a fund of that size for 100,000 dollars. Match the ask to the investor's cheque size.
- Unilever as a strategic buyer: a strategic buyer wants the whole company, which is why investors ask for drag along rights.
- An alumni angel fund at ISB that he invests in: angels pool capital and trust the fund's managers to find and check companies.

## Numbers given

- About 10% ownership: where Amit Khanna says board seat discussions start. Above 20 to 25% it becomes proportional.
- Five to six people: his maximum for a startup board.
- Three years: the typical starting point for promoter lock-in.
- Two to four months: the exclusivity he considers sufficient, against the six months investors typically ask for.
- 30 to 40%: his examples of the haircut on next round valuation in a convertible structure.
- 10 to 20% per year: his own discount formula as an angel investor.
