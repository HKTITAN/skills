# Design thinking class on early adopters and solution brainstorming

The design thinking instructor, who is not named in the recording, opened by asking founders what they learned from the customer interviews set as homework. He then moved from problem discovery to solutions: the ideation, definition and validation method, which customers to validate with, and a brainstorming exercise. The recording ends as the exercise begins.

## What was said

### Understand why the customer has the problem

- The instructor says knowing that people buy drills, nails and hammers is not enough. You have to know why, because a carpenter and a person hanging a photo frame need different solutions.
- He says understanding the reason behind a purchase is what lets you build a differentiated solution.
- He says a question that offers fixed choices, such as nice to have, good to have or need to have, is leading, and being slightly more open-ended gives you more.
- He approves of running the interview without showing the solution and showing a prototype only at the end.
- He says founders often think they are the customer because they have the problem themselves, and the real question is whether the market has the problem.
- He quotes a former head of Xbox as saying great product builders build what their customers love even when they do not like it themselves.
- He says if you build something because you like it, you only increase the chance that it may or may not work, and you want to maximise the chance of success.
- He says everything he teaches reduces the chance of failure and does not assure success.
- He describes the truth curve: "Till such time as the customer uses it, everything is fantasy."
- He says the goal is therefore to take the product out to the market as much as possible.
- He reminds the class that confirmation bias and anchoring bias show up in customer interviews.

### Social goals in a marketplace

- He says the different sides of a marketplace are solving for different goals, and sometimes a social goal matters to users as much as a financial one.
- He says user interviews and personas should look for goals beyond money.

### Ideation, definition, validation

- He presents a method he attributes to the Stanford design school and IDEO: ideation, definition and validation.
- He says ideation draws on what you already know from qualitative research, quantitative data, market size and target market.
- He calls generative AI the best brainstorming partner now available, and says to use it to augment your thinking and not as the answer.
- He says he will show several ways to build and test a solution, and founders should choose by their stage and by whether they build software, hardware or a service.

### Which customers to validate with

- He lists five customer categories: innovators, early adopters, early majority, late majority and laggards.
- He says innovators are about 2% of a market. They are enthusiasts who try any new product, have a high tolerance for financial risk, and do not mind whether it works.
- He says innovators are easy to find and easy to get data from, but their feedback does not help much.
- He says innovators are not loyal and leave when the next new thing arrives, because they want to be first and do not care about your problem.
- He says early adopters believe in the problem you are solving and may already be solving it with a makeshift fix.
- He says early adopters give meaningful feedback because they want to shape the product and influence your roadmap.
- He says research puts early adopters at about 10 to 12% of a market.
- He says in B2B the first customers who are willing to use the product and give input are the early adopters.
- He calls early adopters lighthouse customers, who later stand on stage with you and help sell the product.
- He says to incentivise early adopters, but not with money, because paid people tell you what you want to hear.
- He says validating with the wrong people gives you wrong signals.
- His advice to a founder with both groups is to go after early adopters only and not worry about innovators.
- He says the same holds in a luxury segment, which has its own early adopters.

### The chasm and the early majority

- He says there is a gap between early adopters and the early majority called the chasm, and most companies die in it.
- He says the real money is in the early majority and the late majority.
- He says the early majority joins only when early adopters give credible reviews and when other early-majority customers already use the product.
- His example is a bank, whose first question to a vendor is which other bank it has worked with.
- He says the early majority is sceptical and wants to see data on the value delivered so far, but once it sees value it is sticky.
- He says the early majority asks whether it gets 100 rupees of value for 100 rupees, while an early adopter also wants to give value back as a market maker.
- He says early adopters usually have a superset of the early majority's requirements, so you tone the list down and prioritise.

### Finding early adopters

- He says to work out who experiences the problem and where they gather, and then go there.
- He says for a B2B product you can look for companies whose policies, infrastructure or past public statements show that they already believe in the problem.

### The brainstorming exercise

- He asks each founder to pick two customer pains and write seven solution ideas for each, alone, before any discussion.
- He says four or five ideas come easily and people get stuck after that.
- He then has groups of at most three people brainstorm each member's problem in turn.
- He says done properly this gives at least 30 ideas for every problem.
- He says the point of the exercise is to build the skill, not to produce great ideas that day.
- His tip is to form groups with people you disagree with, because they think about the problem differently and teach you more than friends do.

## Examples and cases used

- Ashok Leyland: mechanics on highways were hard to bring onto its service platform until it offered training and an Ashok Leyland certified board, which built strong loyalty.
- Tesla: buyers of its first car, which he describes as built like a Lotus two-door sports car, were innovators because nobody knew whether it would work.
- Dropbox: it decided its early adopters were developers, found them on technical sites such as Digg, showed a video of the product before giving access, and asked them to sign up as early users.
- AWS and Dropbox: Dropbox was a lighthouse customer for AWS when both were new.
- Clubhouse: backed loudly by Andreessen Horowitz, it peaked and then fizzled, his warning against building on innovators.

## Numbers given

- About 2% of a market are innovators, as the instructor gives it.
- About 10 to 12% of a market are early adopters, which he attributes to research.
- Seven ideas per person per pain and at least 30 ideas per problem as the targets of his exercise.
