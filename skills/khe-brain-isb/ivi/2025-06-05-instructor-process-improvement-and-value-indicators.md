# Process improvement, value indicators and voice of the customer

An operations management class for the cohort, taught by an ISB faculty member who says he earlier worked at the World Bank. He is not named in the recording. The session covered two ways of improving a process, a readiness check to run before either, a group exercise on value indicators using well-known companies, and the start of a unit on turning the voice of the customer into measures. The recording begins partway through the class.

## What was said

### Differentiation and speed

- The instructor says a differentiator has to be created consciously, so that among a hundred similar offers the customer sees only yours. He says that is what brand building is about.
- The instructor says a new product that did not exist before is itself a differentiator, but the founder still has to work to make the difference clear.
- The instructor says that in a crowded field speed to market matters first, more than analysis.
- The instructor says being first to enter a market gives no advantage by itself. The advantage goes to whoever is first to scale.

### Kaizen and kaikaku

- The instructor says there are two ways to improve a process. Kaizen means continuous improvement and kaikaku means breakthrough improvement, both Japanese terms from the quality movement.
- His example is a checkout where payment takes 30 seconds. Cutting it to 3 seconds in one go is kaikaku and needs a page redesign, more server capacity and costlier technology.
- He says the kaizen route cuts about 5 seconds per project. It is cheap and reaches the same place after many projects, and its disadvantage is the time it takes.
- The instructor says he recommends neither over the other. Both are proven in research and practice and both have pros and cons.
- The instructor says a breakthrough change makes change management hard, because the people affected know how to spoil the system.
- The instructor says a small change meets much less resistance, takes less effort and costs less.
- The instructor says kaizen gives staff a sense of participation and accomplishment, which builds culture.
- The instructor says kaikaku is revolutionary, the gain is large enough for management to see, and it is hard to imitate, because by the time competitors copy it you can deliver the next breakthrough.
- The instructor says the choice is a trade-off to be assessed at each stage of the firm.

### The readiness check

- The instructor says a readiness assessment must come before either route. It has three parts, people, process and technology, which he calls a triangle.
- The instructor says people readiness is change management: whether people know about the change, whether they will embrace it, what rewards or penalties are needed, and how it will be communicated.
- The instructor says process readiness asks whether current processes can take the new technology.
- The instructor says technology readiness asks whether the new technology is compatible with what is already in place.
- He says predictive AI systems and generative AI must be checked for compatibility, or the firm creates a data leakage problem.
- The instructor says a firm that has already jumped and is facing resistance scored low on people readiness. It should accept that some talented people will leave.
- For that situation he advises documenting everyone's working knowledge at once, which he says takes one to two weeks, and starting to hire replacements right away.
- The instructor says workshops, mailers and competitions to build awareness are part of the change management homework.
- The instructor says nothing in management promises results. It only raises the likelihood of success, and what is needed is most people being ready, not all.

### Value indicators

- The instructor defines a value indicator as the reason customers in my market segment come to me and not to my competitors.
- The class exercise was to take a named company, pick its two closest rivals, state two value indicators, and then say what operating decisions would strengthen them.
- The instructor says a premium experience is not a value indicator for a premium brand, because rivals in the premium segment offer it too.
- The instructor says quality is too broad to be a value indicator, since safety, on-time delivery and taste are all quality. It has to be specific.
- The instructor says to separate what you offer from how you deliver it. Making to order is the how, and convenience or customisation is the value.
- The instructor says variety and customisation differ. A long standard menu is variety. Building something for one customer from where they stand today is customisation.
- The instructor says whether a rival could do the same thing is not the question. The question is whether they are doing it.
- The instructor says strengthening variety is not just lengthening the menu. Five items that all sell may serve better than many that rarely do.
- The instructor says founders should not defend their first answer in class and should have teammates play devil's advocate. "More critique means more ready you are to jump into market."
- The instructor says a firm with no strong value indicator and no time to build one can ally with a successful player in another industry and borrow its pull.

### Voice of the customer

- The instructor opens the unit on measures with the well-known cartoon of a project that each team understood differently, and says the honest reading is that the firm did not understand the customer.
- The class listed ways to capture the voice of the customer: focus groups, surveys, interviews, field observation, experiments, complaints and reviews.
- The instructor says asking customers what they want does not mean they will tell you.
- His demonstration was to ask the class, as buyers of twenty years ago, how they would like a camera in their phone. He says the answers depend on what the customer then understood a camera and a phone to be, so they cannot be trusted.
- The instructor says customer needs are of two types. Explicit needs are ones the customer knows and will state. Latent needs are hidden and the customer cannot voice them.
- His example of an explicit need is the hospital outpatient who has a 10 o'clock appointment and sees the doctor at 10.45, and complains about it.
- The instructor says explicit needs can be found by the usual methods, while latent needs call for a prototype and test marketing to see how the customer really responds.
- The instructor says the voice of the customer is usually vague, and the firm has to work out the intent behind a phrase such as great donuts before it can measure anything.
- The instructor says that in digital markets a firm's competitor is not only its own kind. A customer who gets groceries delivered in the morning starts expecting healthcare to come home too.
- The instructor says this is learnt by living a day in the life of the customer and seeing what they do and in what order, and that competitor analysis alone is no longer enough.

## Examples and cases used

- World Bank HR and payroll systems: two celebrated technology upgrades did not exchange data, and staff salaries were not paid the next month. Fixing it needed a third interfacing system that cost more than both upgrades. His lesson is that "pockets of excellence" built in silos are dangerous.
- Aadhaar: change management across a whole country with conflicting interests, offered as a case study to read.
- Cream Stone, Ibaco and Baskin Robbins: Cream Stone mixes signature seasonal real-fruit ice creams to order from a menu, Ibaco lets the customer pick from about 40 displayed flavours and charges by weight, and Baskin Robbins sells standard tubs. He names Cream Stone's value indicators as signature seasonal fruit ice cream and made just in time.
- Citibank and Indian Oil: a co-branded card aimed at car-owning customers, as an alliance that borrows another industry's reason to buy.
- Camera phones: a product for a latent need that customers could not have asked for.
- LLMs for lawyers: a senior lawyer getting a case summary and talking points from a model, as an example of a crowded new product field.

## Numbers given

- 30 seconds to 3 seconds: his checkout example of a breakthrough improvement.
- 0.5 million dollars and then 0.2 million dollars: what he says a consultant charged the World Bank to find and then fix the payroll failure.
