# Positioning, product categories and brand trust

A marketing class in a multi-session series, taught by an instructor who is not named in the recording and who says he worked at Johnson & Johnson before teaching. He covered perceptual maps, how consumers sort products into categories, three brand crisis cases, and three kinds of disruptive positioning. He taught through cold-call questions, putting the class in the chief executive's seat for each crisis.

## What was said

### Perceptual maps

- The instructor describes a perceptual map as a visual picture of how consumers in a segment perceive the brands in a category at one point in time.
- He says a position on the map is a strategic summary of the product's benefits: how you are differentiated and why people should believe you.
- The axes are all or some of the key factors consumers weigh when choosing in the category. His example is a pain reliever map with effectiveness on one axis and gentleness on the other.
- He says a factor that every brand delivers equally, for technical or legal reasons, does not appear on the map, because the map shows how brands differ.
- He says the map only shows perception. Where to enter is decided by preference analysis, such as conjoint analysis and concept testing, which estimates how many people would choose you at each spot.
- He says corporate strategy also limits the choice of position. His example is Johnson & Johnson, which he says leaves a business where it cannot be among the top few.
- He says measuring something like gentleness, loyalty or satisfaction is a research problem with published, validated scales, collected in handbooks of marketing scales, and that you adapt the wording to your context.

### How consumers categorise

- He says people group things into categories in their minds based on experience and learning, and a brand has to sit in the right category before it enters the set of options the buyer considers.
- His analogy: the category is the city, the consideration set is the neighbourhood, and the position is the apartment.
- He says firms steer consumers toward the category they want through product design, channel, marketing communication and pricing.
- He says a brand that lands in the wrong category has to spend heavily on communication to explain itself, because people work from a few cues and fill in the rest.
- He says a segment should be alike not only in needs and behaviour but in how its members categorise brands, or one strategy will not work across it.
- He says a category does not have to be real to exist in the mind. People expect a robot to be perfect, or dangerous, from films, without ever having owned one.

### Memory, associations and brand crises

- He describes memory as a network of nodes. The more emotion and repetition link two pieces of information, the stronger the link, which he says is the principle behind repeat advertising.
- He says when one node is triggered the closely linked ones light up with it. A crisis repeated in the media welds the bad association to the brand.
- He says food is a more emotional category than shoes or laptops, so a bad association does more damage there.
- He says the class's first answers to a crisis were all about saving the business, and that good businesses start from the customer. "The best things come out of simplicity, honesty, empathy, compassion."
- He says every decision, by a customer or a company, is based on perceived value, so the way to change a decision is to change the value equation the other side faces.
- He says trust takes decades to build and one incident to break, and that a brand may recover sales after a betrayal but the relationship is not the same.
- He says a company is in the business of solving a problem for someone, not of selling a product, and a chief executive who does not understand why the customer buys cannot handle a crisis.
- He says managers drift away from customers as they rise and start defending armchair strategy. At Johnson & Johnson, he says, even the most senior executives were required to visit customers in the field.

### Categories change

- He says categories are dynamic: they can evolve slowly or change abruptly, with new clusters and hierarchies forming.
- He says categories sometimes proliferate because of product-centric thinking. His example is sports shoes splitting into running, walking, cross-training and basketball shoes, leaving no shoe for a buyer who wants one pair for everything.
- He names two characteristics of a category. Structure is how competition is organised, in tiers and clusters. Identity is what consumers think of the category, made up of its conventions and its image.
- His example of a convention: everyone knows what a hotel room in their usual segment contains, and a new hotel has to provide all of it or guests are unhappy.
- His example of category image: people bargain with a car salesperson and defer to a doctor, whatever the individual in front of them is like.

### The limits of traditional positioning

- He describes traditional positioning as comparing yourself with the other brands in the category and claiming to be better on some factor.
- He says its problem is that every differentiator gets copied. A hotel adds free Wi-Fi, then airport pickup, competitors match it, and it becomes something customers expect.
- He says the result is that brands grow more alike over time, expected value keeps rising, and price competition intensifies.
- He says traditional market research does not help escape this, because customers have been trained to ask for more of what the category already offers.
- He says if you can do significant innovation you should prefer it, because done intelligently it gives a distinct position and lasting market power.

### Disruptive positioning

- He calls disruptive positioning a pure positioning play, with no real product innovation, that works against the whole category by exploiting its conventions and image.
- He says you still run preference analysis on a disruptive option to check that it resonates.
- Reverse positioning: keep only the elements that solve the basic need, strip out the rest of what the category has taught customers to expect, and add something unexpected.
- Breakaway positioning: the product stays what it is, but you associate it with a different category.
- Stealth positioning: you place the product in a category where buyers do not see what it really is, and are happy to buy it.
- He says categories with a well-defined structure, few competitive clusters, and stable, predictable players are easier to disrupt, and easier still when the category's conventions and image are negative.
- Before trying it he says to check three things: the category's current conventions and image, whether consumers are dissatisfied, and whether you have the capabilities to deliver.
- He says every element of the marketing mix has to point at the planned position, and a tactic copied without the position behind it fails.
- He says positioning has to be planned carefully before entry, and that an ad hoc approach costs years and money.
- He says how you frame a product changes the stance people take toward it, and that positioning is deliberate framing.

### Positioning when the company no longer controls it

- He says the purchase journey used to have one or two steps the company controlled. Now buyers check social media at every stage and trust it more than the company.
- He says positioning is therefore no longer under the company's control.
- He says burying a negative story under positive pages in search results worked until about two years ago, and AI chat tools have ended that.
- His advice: do not play fast and loose. Pick a genuine problem, solve it honestly, communicate transparently, and over time a base of customers who trust you will defend the brand when you make a mistake.

## Examples and cases used

- Wendy's finger-in-chili claim: a customer said she found a finger in her chili. It was a fraud, but weeks of coverage tied the image to the product. He gives 2 to 3 million dollars as direct lost sales and 21 to 30 million as estimates of indirect losses.
- His proposed response to Wendy's: offer a large settlement paid only after an investigation clears the claim, or a small sum now, so that a false claimant takes the small sum.
- Taco Bell beef lawsuit: faced with a class action over its beef content, the company ran a new advertising campaign presenting its data. He says the suit was later withdrawn and the company counted the campaign a success.
- Maggi lead scare: he says Nestle India answered for the first week or two with a statement that everything was fine, and the chief executive was later replaced with an Indian executive. His reading is that the company showed no empathy for mothers who had fed Maggi to small children.
- What he says Maggi should have done: go on television in the first day or two, state that the product is fine, stop sales anyway because any doubt about a child's health is unacceptable, and relaunch after a public investigation.
- Tylenol tampering: someone put cyanide in bottles of the leading painkiller in the United States. He says Johnson & Johnson's stance was that injury to a single customer is unacceptable whatever the financial loss, and that this is why the brand is trusted.
- Tata Nano: talked about worldwide as the cheap car, it carried that label by launch and nobody wanted to buy a cheap car. He says Ratan Tata later called it primarily a positioning failure.
- Red Bull: used a tall can and its own look to create an energy drink category and avoid being judged as a cola against Coke and Pepsi.
- Gatorade: described as liquid fuel so that people would not file it under juice.
- Swatch: moved the watch from heirloom or tool to fashion accessory through design, fashion-style channels and a price low enough to own several. He cites it as breakaway positioning with no real innovation.
- Heinz coloured ketchup: tied ketchup to children's creativity. He says the category grew about 2% a year and this product peaked at 14 to 15%.
- IKEA: removed delivery, assembly, guarantees and sales pressure, and added a pleasant day-out store with food and childcare. His example of reverse positioning.
- Sarafem: he says the maker of Prozac gave the same drug a new shape, colour and name for a premenstrual disorder, so patients who would refuse Prozac accepted it. His example of stealth positioning.
- Sony AIBO: an early, buggy home robot sold as a companion pet at a high price, so owners read its faults as the pet's character and shared them in an owner community, giving Sony feedback. He contrasts it with Honda developing ASIMO fully in-house.
- Arvind Kejriwal's early campaign: he reads it as breakaway positioning, common man against politicians, with dress and manner all consistent, and says it weakened once his actions matched the category he had set himself against.
- Chai pe Charcha: he says it worked because it fitted the candidate's story, and a copy of the format for another party failed because the marketing mix did not fit the position.
- The Seven Habits of Highly Effective People: the story of the father whose child is disturbing everyone, used to show how a new frame changes the reaction to the same facts.

## Numbers given

- Wendy's: 2 to 3 million dollars of direct lost sales, and 21 to 30 million dollars of indirect losses, as estimates he cites.
- Heinz coloured ketchup: about 2% regular category growth against a 14 to 15% peak for the product, his figures.
