# Design thinking house building workshop

The operations management instructor ran a hands on design thinking exercise in the last class of the course. Five teams had to build a prototype house from limited craft material for seven classmates playing masked customers, after 15 minutes of customer interviews and 30 minutes of building. The debrief covered personas, customer journey mapping and nudging, and closed with a public health case from Tamil Nadu.

## What was said

### The design thinking framework

- The instructor presents the Stanford d.school framework for putting a new product, service or ecosystem into the market: empathise, define, ideate, prototype, test.
- Empathy means getting into the customer's shoes and not only listening, because customers often do not know what they need or how to say it.
- The instructor lists ways to empathise: talk to customers, observe them, shadow them, and seek to understand their drivers, feelings and emotions.
- The instructor says you can never understand a customer 100 percent, only to a great extent.
- Define is paperwork with the team: converging the many things customers said into the problem you are solving, the personas, the objectives to meet, and the pain points to address.
- Ideate is choosing how to do it. Prototype is making the idea real, putting it in front of the customer and asking for feedback.
- The instructor sums up prototyping as "make fast, fail fast, learn fast".
- Testing with a wider audience comes after a couple of prototype iterations, and the wider audience may still not like what the prototype group liked.
- The instructor says the framework overlaps a lot with the lean startup and points the class to a summary of Eric Ries's book.
- The instructor says these steps look obvious on a slide, and the difficulty appears only under competition, when they must be done correctly and with speed.

### How the exercise was set up

- Each team was the product owner of a house, and the instructor says in most businesses the founder is the product owner.
- Teams had 15 minutes to interview customers and define requirements, then 30 minutes to build a three dimensional prototype.
- Material was limited and could not be topped up, to simulate resource constraints.
- The winner was the prototype liked by the most customers, with every customer weighted equally.
- Customers wore masks. The instructor says this was to block emotional cues, so teams would observe intent and not go only by what customers said.
- The instructor says the exercise was prototyping and not a sales game.

### Customer contact is continuous

- The instructor says customer contact has to be continuous, because as the product evolves the customer understands hidden needs and starts saying more.
- The instructor calls the process iterative with many loops, not linear.
- The advice to teams was to co-create value: the more you involve the customer, the more successful the product is in the market.
- In the debrief, teams reported that customer needs kept changing. The instructor says that is how customers and the world are.
- The instructor says customers change what they want after seeing other products and come back thinking more creatively.

### Personas and where to start

- The instructor defines a persona as a set of customers who show similar characteristics, not a single customer.
- The instructor had built five personas into the seven customers of the game, among them a responsible family oriented parent, an artistic customer and a vintage enthusiast.
- The instructor says every organisation is constrained by people, material and sometimes time, so the best start is to pick the personas that readily suit your resources.
- The instructor says looking for the overlap among all customers' requirements is good thinking but is not the first step.
- The recommended strategy for a new business is to build the base product for one or two important personas and then serve the remaining personas through enhancements.

### Customer journey mapping

- The instructor calls customer journey mapping a very good tool for empathy.
- The journey starts when the thought of buying first enters the customer's mind, before any action.
- The instructor gives three steps: capture the stages of the customer journey, identify the customer's expectations and pain points at each stage, and capture the emotion each stage triggers.
- In the house example, a family oriented buyer first weighs buying a house against alternatives such as mutual funds, then compares builders, then locations.
- The instructor says browsing behaviour differs at each stage, and each stage is a chance to put your product in the customer's mind.
- The instructor says not to treat the moment the customer comes to you as the starting point of selling.
- The instructor notes the buyer comparing a house with a mutual fund finds no single place that makes that comparison, and counts that as a pain point to capture.
- The instructor says the map captures behaviour and not only voice.

### Nudging

- The instructor says pattern finding is easy in the digital world, because every click on an app is consumer behaviour data.
- A nudge should differ by customer. The instructor's example is a morning message about fresh leafy vegetables for the customer who buys them and not for the one who buys something else.

## Examples and cases used

- Cataract screening in Tamil Nadu: the instructor describes an article of his in Management and Business Review on a state government project that combined AI with design thinking.
- In that project a photo of the eye is uploaded to an app, and an image classification model sorts it within seconds into high risk, medium risk or no cataract.
- The instructor says the app is only the front end. Behind it are the hospital scheduling system, a human diagnosis to confirm the AI result, transport to and from the hospital through an NGO, 15 days of post care, and a follow up visit.
- The instructor says detection alone changes nothing for the patient, which is why he calls the design an ecosystem and not an app.
- The instructor says the first phase eradicated cataract from two districts of Tamil Nadu and the project has scaled to two other states.
- The instructor's message is that if a government body can build this, a private firm has far more potential to do the same.
- The instructor says most blindness in India comes from untreated cataract.

## Numbers given

- 15 days of post care in the cataract project, in the instructor's account.
