# Market definition, growth hacking and value, Professor Siddharth

The second session of the Marketing and Market Readiness course, taught by the same ISB marketing professor as the day before, although the recording carries the label Lecture 1. He recaps the marketing process, then works through cases on market definition, a long class debate on what counts as growth hacking, market sizing, the targeting decision, and value, ending with a case from his own time selling surgical products for Johnson and Johnson.

## What was said

### The process is a loop, not a line

- Professor Siddharth says every concept test gives new information, so a founder should go back and revise the segmentation and everything after it, not treat each step as finished.
- He says good marketing today is customer-centric and follows a design thinking approach, starting from the need.
- He says need and heterogeneity are the concepts that never change, and only the way heterogeneity is handled changes.
- He says positioning and value creation are two sides of the same coin.
- He says a founder can choose a position first and design value to reach it, or test value propositions and accept whatever position the most preferred one leads to.
- He says in a corporation the corporate strategy always dominates the product strategy, while a startup founder is free to choose a position.
- He says consumers care only about perceived value.
- He says most people are instinctively product-centric, starting from what others sell and tweaking it, because society trains people to conform to what is around them.
- He says a founder should keep the sequence of need, heterogeneity, segmentation and targeting, and bring in competing solutions and their positions only after that.

### Latent needs

- He says latent needs can be found by observation, or in a focus group by asking why again and again and working out what is going on in people's minds.
- He says customers say no when asked about a latent need, and say wow once they see the solution.
- He says a latent need becomes an existing need once people have used the solution.
- He says a founder in a consumer goods business can cut research cost by trying formulas on family and friends first, narrowing to four or five, and only then testing formally with a sample from the segment.

### Market definition changes everything

- He shows two charts with identical sales data for one product and says the owner would celebrate in one and change strategy in the other, depending only on which market the product is placed in.
- He says in a stable or declining market, gaining sales and market share means you are winning and should keep the strategy.
- He says the founder decides which market they are in, sitting in their own office, and nobody tells them.
- He says how you define the market changes how you judge success and failure and who your competitors are.
- He says competitors sit in rings, with close ones to focus on and more distant ones to keep an eye on, because looking at all of them at once is too much information.
- He says a company that names its product when asked what business it is in is showing a red flag.
- He says it is never about technology but about the problem being solved, and technology is one element of the marketing mix.
- He says product markets are dynamic, so a segmentation done once will not stay valid.

### What digital technology changed

- He says a physical shop with limited space and a limited catchment had to stock only the most popular items, which left the demand for unusual items unserved.
- He says digital technology opened up that long tail, because a niche can be made viable by adding up demand from across the world.
- He says this is why advice on building a YouTube channel almost always says to find a niche.
- He says streaming companies spread a budget across many programmes, release them, and shift the budget toward what people watch, which is a different method from the old film business.

### Growth hacking

- He defines growth hacking as conscious rapid experimentation for growth, after launch, with the elements of the marketing mix that create value for consumers.
- He sets communication aside when using the term, because companies have always experimented with communication.
- He says growth hacking came about to replace slow corporate processes in which ideas waited months for a committee to decide which to test.
- He says a product that has been fully developed and launched is normally left unchanged until a version 2.0, while growth hacking keeps evolving it throughout the year.
- He says launching a new variant because you feel it should exist is a new product launch, not an experiment.
- He says an experiment needs a control group equivalent to the experimental group, and without one it is trial and error.
- He gives three conditions for saying X causes Y: X is correlated with Y, X comes before Y, and every other factor that could cause Y is ruled out.
- He says the third condition is the most important, and even then the conclusion is accepted only for now.
- He says software now exists to run automated experiments at scale.
- He says to choose for experimentation the elements with low or manageable development risk.
- He says a healthcare product is a case where common sense says do not experiment on customers.
- He says in digital media a company must experiment with its communication, because nobody can say in advance what will work.
- He says the idea is not limited to websites and can be applied to physical products and to content.
- He says the definition is not yet settled in marketing research and he is writing a paper on it.
- He tells the class not to worry about the label, and to understand the concept well enough to use it to win.

### Sizing the market

- He says secondary data is collected by someone else for another purpose and primary data is collected for your own.
- He says most secondary data is of little use to someone building something new, because research firms have to define existing categories to produce reports.
- He says market potential is what is possible and a forecast is what is expected, and both hold only under stated assumptions about what you, competitors, customers and the context will do, over a given time.
- He says to use several methods to reach a ballpark range and not to trust one.
- He says "if you get an accurate answer, you are wrong".
- He says market size should be estimated in units first, not rupees, because price is an element of the marketing mix that is decided only after targeting.
- He says investors know a founder's limits and look for reasonableness and thoroughness, not perfection.
- He says a founder should show investors the sources used and the good-faith effort made, and offer to revise if they know better.

### The targeting decision

- He says the first step in targeting is to judge which segments are attractive on your own criteria.
- He says the second step is to rank the attractive segments.
- He says the third step is to ask, starting with the most attractive, what it would take for any company to win in that segment.
- He says what it takes to win is specific to the segment and the same for every company that enters it.
- He says the founder then matches their own strengths and capabilities against that list, and enters where the match is best among the most attractive segments.
- He says each segment can be scored on attractiveness and on business strength using weighted criteria and plotted on a grid, a tool he says is based on the GE and McKinsey method.
- He says a weak business strength score can be raised by investing in the weakest factors.
- He says the attractiveness criteria are fixed, so the way to change that score is to redefine the segment.
- He says a startup that lacks what it takes to win should not enter yet and should prepare first.
- He says "battle well prepared is battle already half won".

### Value

- He defines value as the perceived worth, in monetary units, of the benefits a customer receives in exchange for the price paid, taking competing offerings into account.
- He shortens this to perceived benefits minus costs, given competition.
- He names need, market definition, value and heterogeneity as timeless concepts.
- He says every decision by every buyer is made on perceived value.
- He says value has to be perceived as superior, and in a way that leads to the desired position.
- He says costs include the cost of searching for information, so putting information online creates value even for a company that does not sell online.
- He says the focus of value creation has moved from benefits toward costs and competition over the last two or three decades, and from the transaction to the whole customer journey.
- He says a founder has to understand what the customer needs and what their emotional state is at each touchpoint in that journey.
- He says a good journey also creates a hook, such as a loyalty programme or a familiar interface, that brings the customer back.
- He says all value comes down to two things: better, easier, faster, and emotionally satisfying.
- He says value is relative, so one extra click matters because a competitor offers one click fewer, not because of the time it takes.
- He says if the end consumer does not see the value the company is dead, whatever its internal systems are, so internal systems and functions must be reorganised around delivering it.
- He says perceived value works in B2B as much as in B2C, and tells the class to forget the distinction and look at each situation.
- He says expert buyers pay less for perception, but perception still plays a part somewhere in their choice.

### Stakeholders and diagnosis

- He says when a purchase has several stakeholders, the company must explain the value to each one in their own language and according to their own concerns.
- He says understanding B2B customers means understanding the power relations, how demand is generated, how purchases happen and whose interest each step serves.
- He says a founder should choose a target by whether it can make money, then focus on getting the solution right, because money is a result.
- He says a founder or chief executive who puts money first ends up losing it.
- He says when something goes wrong, check strategic marketing first, meaning market definition, segmentation and position, and only then examine the marketing mix elements one by one.

## Examples and cases used

- A coffee-flavoured cola from Coca-Cola that he bought in Houston: his example of a latent need found by seeing that people take caffeine from two categories. He says it was a test market and was withdrawn.
- Johnson and Johnson's tray of sutures: procedure-specific, pre-sterilised trays with compartments and colour coding that saved nurses' preparation time, made a missing item visible at a glance, and shut competitors' sutures out of the procedure.
- Nintendo Wii: he says Nintendo defined its market as the need for game-based entertainment, asked consumers what they wanted, and chose low price, family-friendly games, no high definition and a motion stick, which served an existing need.
- Xbox and PlayStation: he says Microsoft and Sony were building a home entertainment hub, a latent need, and sold it first as a high-end game console to gamers, the segment most willing to tolerate early flaws, which he calls staged adoption.
- PlayStation and Samsung's Blu-ray player: he says Sony priced a better-rated machine below a plain player because it was fighting to lock in households, not to profit on the console.
- A maker of VoIP devices that asked his advice in 2011: it defined its business as making the devices and named only other device makers as competitors.
- MagicJack: he says it defined its market as the need for inexpensive long distance communication, so it moved from a hardware device to an app when smartphones spread.
- Horses: anyone who had defined the market as the need to get to the office would have sold horses then and motorcycles or cars now.
- Netflix and Amazon Prime: his examples of new entrants that became some of the largest media companies within 10 to 15 years.
- Indian television serials: he says a multimedia story cannot be concept tested in the traditional way, so broadcasters change characters and plots as ratings move, which he calls growth hacking on a full product and not on an MVP.
- McDonald's: he says it runs new items as specials in test stores against control stores and moves those that pass a sales threshold onto the main menu.
- A perfume company selling very small sample sizes: experimentation only if the results are used to change the product range.
- Craigslist and similar listing platforms: he says strong network effects leave them little incentive to improve.
- Lexus: its website offered a table comparing its car with rivals, which he reads as cutting the buyer's cost of searching for information.
- A car dealer who arranged a priest and a puja at delivery: value created at one touchpoint of the journey.
- Yebhi.com: he says it advertised try before you buy for shoes but its systems could not deliver that, and it went out of business.
- Eggs: regular, grain-fed and free range eggs sold at very different prices, which he calls primarily a mind game.
- A tile adhesive brand: he says it sells the same product as rivals at a higher margin through heavy advertising in a fragmented market, because the adhesive is a small part of a tiling job and a failure can never be traced back to it.
- Children's toothbrushes: parents are told about health, bristles and price on a news channel, and children are shown superheroes on a cartoon channel, because both must want it.
- SGPGI, a research and referral hospital in Lucknow: his own account from Johnson and Johnson of losing the annual contract in a reverse auction and being asked back within months.

## Numbers given

- He says Samsung's plain Blu-ray player was priced almost 25% above the PlayStation that included one.
- He says Sony and Microsoft were locking in each household for the next 10 to 15 years.
- He gives egg prices of 80 rupees a dozen for regular, 250 for grain-fed and 325 for free range.
- He says the tile adhesive brand has earned a margin 20 to 25% higher than rivals for years.
- He says Johnson and Johnson held 60 to 70% of the market in sutures, meshes and endoscopy equipment worldwide and in India, yet had one professional account in Uttar Pradesh, a state of 160 million people at the time.
- He says Johnson and Johnson's products were 20 to 40% more expensive than the local competitors in the auction.
