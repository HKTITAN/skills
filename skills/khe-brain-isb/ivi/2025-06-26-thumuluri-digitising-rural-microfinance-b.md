# Moving microfinance from field visits to digital, Sasidhar Thumuluri

Sasidhar Thumuluri of SubK spoke to the cohort about how a microfinance business that ran on field visits and cash moved its repayments, risk assessment and customer engagement to digital channels. He talked through slides and took questions throughout, then answered open questions on regulation, rural customers and group lending.

## What was said

### The business

- Sasidhar Thumuluri says the company started in 2010, when few people had smartphones, on the bet that phones would be the future of banking.
- He says the company does not lend its own money and works in an enabler role on behalf of banks.
- He describes two businesses: lending on behalf of banks, and basic banking such as deposits and withdrawals through an agent network.
- He says most founders in the room, like him, have not invented a new product and are delivering an existing one in a different way, to a different segment or at a different price point.
- He says lending is one of the oldest businesses, and his aim is to make it accessible, affordable and available to a large number of people.

### The real obstacle was the people who deliver

- He says the team first assumed that customer behaviour was the obstacle to going digital, and then found it was the behaviour of the people who deliver the service.
- He says people used to working one way do not accept that the customer has changed.
- He says they tested whether customers were ready with a simple study of how many had smartphones at home, how many used WhatsApp and how many used UPI.
- He says banks did not believe a rural woman could use a phone, and investors asked why he would challenge something that was already working.
- He says that if you do not make the change you will die, even though every factor pushes you back.
- He says a startup hires experienced people from the old way of working and then has to invest in making them understand the new one.
- His example is the collection officer whose job was to collect cash and who is now told to get customers to download an app and pay online. When that person changes, his customers slowly adapt.
- He says the channel is what you have to work on, not only the end customer.

### Choosing what to digitise

- He says the first step was a vision, put everything in the phone, which then had to be broken down.
- He says they broke their own process into basic steps such as sourcing, enrolment, appraisal, credit assessment and documentation.
- He says they were not changing the process, only the way the process is done.
- He says each step was broken into sub activities, such as using data for sourcing in place of going village to village, an app or WhatsApp link, and e-KYC in place of paper.
- He says they then identified the blocks with the highest return on investment, because "you can digitize everything in the world" without it being a good investment.
- He says they picked three interventions: repayments, risk assessment and continuous customer engagement.
- He says the interventions were done one after another and not all at once, doubling down on one or two important things.

### Repayments

- He says repayments were the most valuable intervention because most customers paid physical cash every month.
- He describes the old method: an officer travels to each village, counts the cash, bundles it, carries it in a bag and moves to the next village.
- He names soiled notes and fake notes among the risks of collecting cash.

### Risk assessment

- He says managing risk is the most important thing in a lending business.
- He says the old assessment meant a trained person visiting the house, looking at living conditions and stock and asking around, which he calls costly, informal and not scientific.
- He says informal checks fail because a customer who really needs money will not disclose everything.
- He says a risk tool sorts customers into green, amber and red. Green customers proceed with digital interaction only, amber customers get a second check by a person, and red customers are not considered.
- He says the tool does not make the decision. It tells the company where to spend its energy.
- He says a customer can show her house, shop and assets on a recorded video call in place of a visit.
- He says the recording lets an auditor, a trainer or a supervisor review the call later, and that the call captures location and does face matching.
- He says embedding video in an app that works in a village on low bandwidth was a huge challenge, and that they ran trials with several providers.
- He says Zoom worked best on basic phones but was the hardest to integrate, because it was not built for thousands of small retail calls a day.

### Results

- He says customers who pay digitally default at less than half the rate of customers who pay in cash, on both 30 day and 90 day measures.
- He explains this by visibility. Cash is invisible, while a digital payer builds a record and has an incentive to perform well.
- He says staff who moved their customers to digital payment manage more accounts and earn more.
- He says accounts managed per loan officer is the real unit cost in the business.
- He says you start with a hypothesis of the outcomes you want, such as productivity and turnaround time, and keep checking those parameters, because results show only over time.
- He says a startup with no baseline of its own can compare itself with an industry metric.

### AI on the recorded videos

- He says the company is part of a Google accelerator and now runs AI on the recorded calls.
- He lists what the system extracts: an audio transcript, objects seen, house quality, business quality, sentiment and tone, and whether a third party is present or prompting.
- He says the output is reduced to a single score.
- He says his job is to send the bank the best possible case so that it is not rejected, because a rejected case means all the cost is lost.
- He says nothing about the business changed, the same interactions, customers and loan officers, and the change was to capture the interaction as data.
- His wider point is that a founder does not have to radically change how a business is run and can keep the way it is done while getting more out of it with available technology.
- He says building blocks such as OCR and e-signatures are commodities, so a founder should not spend innovation effort there.

### How the ideas were generated

- He says they formed four or five cross functional teams, one for each workflow, with people from business, marketing and finance.
- He says a customer facing person was always on the team so that ideas were validated.
- He says they took behavioural scientists to talk to customers.
- He says the technology team then proposed tools and a budget, and management chose what to do first by low cost and high yield.
- He says the talk shows a success story and that they started and stopped many things, including individual loans, which they dropped after two years.

### External shocks and regulation

- He says customer risk in this business is small and the larger risk is external, mainly the political environment in a state.
- He says a state law against microfinance in 2010 was a risk they had never thought would happen.
- He says the hardest part afterwards was convincing customers to repay after political leaders had promised to take care of the loans, and that rebuilding took years.
- He says they left that market in recovery mode, did fresh business elsewhere and diversified because they had been too concentrated in one geography.
- He says Karnataka has brought in a microfinance law that restricts the hours at which companies can visit borrowers' homes.
- He says a regulator always follows the market and is never ahead of it.
- He tells founders not to just run their own business and to keep watch on the industry and be part of it.
- He says he wants his competitors to survive too, because the industry surrounds the business.
- He describes two kinds of regulation under the RBI, direct regulation and self regulation, where an industry body is given powers to check its own members.
- He says self regulation is used when a segment is fragmented and its customers run into millions, and compares it to community policing.

### Customers and lending rules

- He says the way to understand customers is to live in their environment, and that someone who does not know rural India should spend a couple of months there.
- He says the company lends only to women, in groups, so that members watch over each other.
- He says the company does not form the groups. Women in a village bring their own group, checks are run and only those who qualify go forward.
- He says they prefer to lend to people who are already in business and not to someone who plans to start after training, which he calls very risky.
- He says loans are the most revenue generating and the most difficult product, because money is easy to give and takes long to get back.
- He says the most common use of loans is kirana shops.
- He says older borrowers repay relatively well, and that once the system is set there is no large difference in default between generations.
- He describes the agent network as shopkeepers equipped with technology linked to a bank, where a villager can withdraw a government transfer using Aadhaar without an ATM.

## Examples and cases used

- Zoom: the video provider that worked best on basic phones in villages, though it was the hardest to integrate.
- Razorpay: his example of a large platform that was suddenly restricted after a regulator acted.
- An NGO working with Anganwadi workers: recorded video assessments of children scored by a system against set parameters, as another use of the same idea.
- Personal loan apps: he applied on several and was told he was not eligible, which he uses to show the state of credit access.

## Numbers given

- 10 million: customers served so far, by his count.
- 15 years: the age of the company.
- About 2 to 3 percent: the default rate he gives for his business.
- 24.5%: the interest rate he says customers pay, which he calls expensive.
- 5, 6, 7 percent: where he says industry defaults have moved in the current stretched market.
- 80%: the share of his customers he says fall in the green category and get digital interaction only.
- 95%: the face match figure he quotes from the AI output.
- About one dollar: what he says they pay per video for the AI processing.

## Not covered

- He did not explain the company's commercial terms with banks or how revenue is shared.
- He did not describe the scoring model behind the green, amber and red categories.
- He said the company is not yet offering its platform to other kinds of lenders.
