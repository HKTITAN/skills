# Digital marketing communication, paid owned and earned media, Madhu Viswanathan

Madhu Viswanathan taught this class on the communication part of marketing strategy, with a focus on digital. He ran it as a lecture with many questions from the room, one ad screening and a worked class exercise on a headphone brand. He said it was his last class with the cohort and he ran short of time, so the content framework at the end is only sketched.

## What was said

### Digital and traditional media

- Madhu Viswanathan says only about 40% of total advertising spend in India goes to digital and the rest is traditional. He adds that the share depends on whose numbers you read, but it has not crossed half.
- He says a digital tool working for someone else does not mean it will work for you. SEO and influencers are tools, and the choice follows from your objective.
- He tells founders not to fixate on platforms, because platforms keep changing. Go where your customers are, and if you are not big enough to build a platform-specific skill, hire an agency.
- He says communication has only two objectives: awareness and persuasion. Persuasion can be toward any action, such as visiting a site or a store, not only buying.
- He says traditional media still has the most reach. A hoarding gets more eyeballs than a targeted digital campaign.
- He says what digital does better is targeted reach. He uses two words for this: effective means getting to your customers, and efficient means doing it at a lower cost.
- He says the total cost of advertising ends up about the same in traditional and digital. Traditional has high upfront fixed cost and low variable cost, and digital is the reverse.
- He says digital will not save money. It helps you spend money better.
- He says digital is flexible: it lets you learn and correct as the campaign unfolds, but only if you listen to the feedback and act on it.
- He says traditional media is very hard to measure and digital clearly wins on measurement.
- He says new AI tools make content cheap to create without an agency, and the content can also go out through traditional media such as posters and pamphlets.
- He says Google's AI-based search is upending website rankings, so past SEO work loses its value, and each founder must ask why the business needs to be online at all.
- He lists the challenges of digital marketing as regulatory compliance in sectors such as healthcare and finance, the data protection law's burden on data security, content accuracy problems such as fake reviews and deepfakes, and professional boundaries.

### Paid, owned and earned media

- He defines paid media as any advertising, including web, social, influencer and retargeting. Owned media is your own property, such as a mobile app, blog, website or retail store. Earned media is word of mouth and referrals.
- He says the biggest impact of digital comes through earned media, because it is media you do not pay for.
- He says using digital only as advertising is using it the way you would use TV, and such campaigns are not very effective.
- He says paid and owned media must be integrated today, and the reason to boost content is to get to earned media that brings people back to you.
- He says advertising should boost organic brand building. Without a good organic base, paid media only gets more expensive.
- He says campaigns are no longer spray and pray. Each channel in the plan should have clear KPIs and an expected metric.
- He classes influencer seeding as paid media, with the goal that it leads to earned media.
- He says email marketing is still worth doing because its customer acquisition cost is very low, as long as it does not become spam. He says Mailchimp is free for lists of 1,000 or fewer.
- He says Instagram is a difficult platform for marketers because it lacks good click-to-action routes, and platforms that monetise through ads are filling up with ads that viewers scroll past.

### How much advertising returns

- He says marketers regularly overspend on advertising, because it is the easiest thing for them to push and the hardest to measure.
- He says responding to advertising that is not working with more advertising is a bad strategy. Often the cause is that the audience is already saturated with the ads.
- He says for a well-established brand, advertising is a volumes game that pushes sales at the edges. For a new product or a new market it has considerable value.
- He says digital advertising elasticities are very similar to traditional ones and are not very good.
- He says people get used to advertising and its effect fades. His example is that most people in the room have switched off or stopped reacting to Zomato notifications.
- He says advertising is what everybody sees first but "it is the thing that does the least".
- He says advertising builds the brand, and the brand is the asset, not the advertising. Once the brand is built you advertise less often, only to stay in mind.

### Customer acquisition cost and direct to consumer

- He says using paid media to acquire customers is possible but very expensive, and rising CAC in a company's published reports shows it is buying customers with advertising.
- He says a low-cost value proposition cannot sit with high-cost acquisition channels. When he sees high CAC with a low-cost proposition he expects the business to fail.
- He says high CAC is acceptable for a niche premium brand, because customers pay more once acquired.
- He says direct to consumer works only with loyalty and repeat customers. It suits low-involvement products where people stay once they switch, and premium niche products where search costs are high.
- He says it is hard to name a D2C brand that is still only D2C, because the economics of holding customers on your own channel do not work without very high brand loyalty, and that loyalty does not come overnight.
- He says D2C is useful at the start to prove there are customers, which marketplaces and retailers want to see. It is not a long-term strategy on its own.
- He says D2C also helps with loyal customers who would otherwise buy through a marketplace that takes about 30%.
- He says guerrilla marketing works through surprise and shock, so it helps in the short term and cannot be repeated for the same product.
- He says not to copy a competitor's marketing strategy. Use it as input, but your resources, capabilities, customer segment and value proposition differ.

### Product-led and sales-led growth

- For B2B he draws two dimensions. Touch is how many touches a customer needs before buying, or the ease of selling. Friction is how hard it is for the customer to understand the product.
- He says low touch with low friction is product-led growth, sold online. High friction with high touch is sales-led growth, dominated by a sales force.
- He says friction is high when the product is complex or must be used by many people in an organisation.
- He says the other two quadrants are hybrids. Low friction with high touch starts product-led and needs a sales force to finish the sale.
- He says product-led growth still needs a lot of marketing, and sales-led growth needs its own sales frameworks, such as incentives and lead generation.

### Personas

- He asks four questions to understand a customer: who is the customer, who is the product for, what is different about this customer, and how are they expected to use it.
- He says not everyone in a broad group is your customer, so the key question is what makes your customers different from the rest of that group.
- He says a persona holds demographics, psychographics, behaviour and attitudes. During product development it helps shape the product. Near launch it has two jobs: what to communicate and where to communicate.
- He says most people make up personas out of thin air, which misdirects targeting and campaigns.
- He names three kinds of data for personas. First-party data from your own customers is the most valuable, and new founders do not have it. Second-party data is market research, which is aggregate and noisy. The third is data held by the large platforms, which they do not share.
- His advice to a new founder is to accept that the first persona is a guess, use market research as far as possible, then run ads on the platforms both to win customers and to collect data that recalibrates the persona.
- He says to give customers an incentive, such as a discount or a birthday gift, to share more information about themselves.
- He explains lookalike matching: you give a platform your customers' phone numbers, and it finds similar people and shows them your ads without giving you their details.
- He says purchased contact lists vary with price. The cheaper the list, the noisier it is.
- He says a persona shown to investors must lead to actions and rest on data you collected, so that it shows you know your customer well enough to target them.
- He says a persona should end in a jobs-to-be-done statement, which is the value proposition, and in digital behaviour, which tells you where to place the communication.
- He says a persona should turn into decisions on channel mix, features or bundles, pricing strategy and how much to invest in the customer.
- He says registering as a seller on Amazon gives access to a lot of customer data, and is a way to gauge interest in a category even if you will not sell there.

### Tribes

- He says personas are good for targeting and for the point of sale, and tribes are for brand building and creating the value proposition.
- He defines a tribe as a network of shared interests and needs, like a community with leaders and followers. It rests on community affiliation, not on an individual's observed behaviour.
- He says tribes show up in review text, group associations and social engagement, and that each tribe has its own symbols, values and language.
- He says a person belongs to only one persona but to many tribes at once.
- He says the persona view suits advertising, and the tribe view suits engagement.
- He says the posts that get engagement are the ones close to the margin, where the topic could tip into negative territory, because shared interest is strong there.
- He says a tribe does not need an opposing tribe. Outsiders often do not care.
- He says tribes matter most in low-involvement categories where anyone can source the same product.

### Touchpoints

- He says a customer journey map must be built on touchpoints, because a brand cannot measure or change a thought, only the points where the customer meets it.
- The stages he names include trigger, search, purchase and onboarding. Advertising creates the trigger touchpoint. At the search stage the touchpoints are review videos and comparison sites, which a brand can influence through reviewers or ads placed there.

### The COMPASS campaign framework

- He presents COMPASS as his own campaign planning framework: context, objectives, methods, plan, action, surveillance, scrutiny.
- Context is the environment: the core persona, your capabilities, a SWOT and what competitors are doing.
- Objectives are set with OKRs for the high-level goals and SMART for each specific target, down to the KPIs.
- Methods are segmentation, targeting and positioning.
- Plan is where to advertise and what, by funnel stage, with the budget allocation.
- He says action is the easiest part, since it is implementation and can be given to people inside or outside the firm.
- Surveillance is monitoring KPIs during the campaign to correct course. He compares KPIs to valves in a plant that show where things are stuck.
- Scrutiny is the review after the campaign that feeds the next one, for example checking whether the people who came match the persona and which channels did not work.
- He says campaigns today are continuous and build on each other, unlike past one-shot campaigns.
- He says most frameworks are 80 to 90% identical, so pick one and stick with it.

### Content and the budget split

- He names his content framework CREATE: conceptualise, research, engineer, activate, track, evaluate.
- He says the target audience for content may not be your customers. A B2C company posts on LinkedIn for goodwill or hiring, not for sales.
- He says targeting everyone with content is a bad strategy. Use a particular channel for a particular audience.
- He says regularity matters more than frequency in posting.
- He says a new business starts with about 70 to 80% of spend on performance marketing and 20 to 30% on brand, and should move toward 50-50 or more toward brand.
- His reason is that performance marketing gives the most return in the initial phase and fades, by which time the brand should be strong enough that performance only nudges.
- He says PR is now close to paid media and less effective than before, and matters most when there is a backlash, including online.
- He says listening to customers online is not enough. It has to turn into action, such as trial offers, advertising, seeding customers and influencers, and soliciting reviews.
- He says inbound marketing through search is the better route because people arrive with intent. Firm-initiated outbound advertising, the focus of traditional marketing, is now a small part of the whole.

## Examples and cases used

- Cadbury's Diwali ad with Shah Rukh Khan: the ad named local shops by the viewer's location, and used an artificially generated version of the actor, with his permission, so the store names matched his face.
- Groww during the IPL: reach as a branding exercise. Everyone came to know the name though not everyone would invest.
- Fintech and brokerage firms, Zomato, Razorpay, Google Pay and PhonePe: heavy users of messages, notifications, communities and affiliate offers.
- Sugar Cosmetics: sold on Myntra for a long time, then built its own channel, then had to go into offline retail because D2C alone did not work out.
- Zomato: he says it added platform fees because it could not extract more from restaurants, that its core capability is micro-delivery and restaurant aggregation, and that it will expand beyond food because food cannot grow further.
- Amazon: he calls distribution its core capability, as distinct from its value proposition.
- ChatGPT, Salesforce and Zoho: his examples of product-led, sales-led and hybrid growth.
- A US grocery retailer he worked with: store layouts were changed to help men who shop. He adds that men who like to cook shop differently from men who cook because they have no other option.
- Royal Enfield, Harley-Davidson and Sugar Cosmetics: a study of social data found Royal Enfield users see themselves as foodies, Harley-Davidson users describe themselves as family oriented, and Sugar users write in Gen Z language.
- Under Armour signing Gisele Bundchen: the replies to the announcement split into distinct tribes.
- Dove's Real Women campaign: it gets engagement because some people stand by its ideals and others reject them.
- Netflix: he says its recommendations work from the community a viewer belongs to.
- boAt headphones: the class exercise. The persona is a 22-year-old male college student in a tier 2 city, budget conscious, listening about 4 hours a day. The tribe is a music and street culture that tags the brand in unboxing stories. Personas drive campaign targeting, bundles and regional influencers, and the tribe drives brand building.
- Coca-Cola and Royal Enfield: association with soldiers is brand positioning, not a tribe.
- Tata Motors: brand as the asset. He says truckers in smaller towns need no advertising to trust it.
- Content creators such as MrBeast: he says they monetise by building their own brand and selling something off it, because advertising alone is a weak model for them.

## Numbers given

- About 40% of India's advertising spend is digital, by the figures he cites.
- His advertising elasticity figure for a well-established brand: 14 to 17 rupees of immediate sales for every 100 rupees spent, sometimes as low as 1.
- About 70% of grocery shopping in the US is done by women, by his figure.
- About 30%: the cut he says Amazon or Myntra takes from a brand's sale.
- 30 to 40%: what he says Zomato charges restaurants.
- 70-30 or 80-20: his starting split of performance to brand spend, moving toward 50-50.
- 80 to 90%: how alike he says most marketing frameworks are.
