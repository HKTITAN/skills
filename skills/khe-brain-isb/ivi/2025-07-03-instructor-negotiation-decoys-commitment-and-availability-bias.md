# Negotiation, decoys, commitment and availability bias

This is the third class in the negotiation course, taught by a faculty member who is not named in the recording. She went through influence techniques a negotiator can use or must defend against: decoy options, commitment and consistency, the foot in the door and door in the face requests, and availability bias. She taught mostly through her own stories and quick classroom experiments, and the class then moved into a team negotiation on a real case. The file also carries a classmate's written notes, used below only where marked.

## What was said

### Using the techniques together

- The instructor says these effects exist in everyone, and a negotiator can use one or more of them together to influence the other party.
- She says knowing the biases is not enough, and that applying them in your own context takes practice.

### Decoy options

- She explains a decoy as an option added only to make another option look better.
- She says a decoy has to be similar to the target option but inferior to it.
- Her main example is The Economist's three subscription options, where she quotes 59 dollars for the online subscription and 120 dollars for print.
- She says decoys are used in telecom packages, car variants, Starbucks coffee sizes and software plan tables that show ticks and crosses for each tier.
- She says she once helped a consulting partner redesign three project options, because the client was not being given the right comparison for the package he wanted chosen.
- She says the decoy is a very effective technique that not many people actually use.
- She says a decoy can be used when designing your own packages.

### Commitment and consistency

- She says commitment and consistency is used a lot in sales, and that the foot in the door name comes from door to door selling.
- Her example of sunk cost is continuing to invest in a stock that never picked up, in the belief that it will do well at some point.
- She says escalation of commitment and sunk cost are similar, and both come from the sunk cost bias.
- She cites the book Negotiating Rationally for a case where the chief executives of two large companies kept escalating after a public statement and both ended up losing.

### Foot in the door

- She describes foot in the door as starting with a smaller request and then asking for something larger but similar.
- She says the larger request cannot be very different from the first one.
- Her first story is agreeing to be a backup teacher for a course months ahead and ending up teaching the whole course.
- Her second story is students who asked to film in her home for half an hour, stayed for hours, used every room and then got her to act in the film.
- She says once you have said yes it is hard to then say no, and that this is a very common human tendency.

### Door in the face

- She describes door in the face as starting with a big request that is refused, then making a smaller one, which works because most people find it hard to keep saying no.
- She says the guidance in the book is to use foot in the door when you have a working relationship, and door in the face when you have none, as in a one shot negotiation.
- She says she has no case of her own where door in the face worked, and that she thinks foot in the door works in most cases.

### Using commitment in a negotiation

- She says it is a good idea to include some easy to resolve issues, where both parties are aligned.
- She says once people have agreed on some issues they are more likely to move forward on the difficult ones.
- She says an early deal on easy issues means that walking away later feels like losing something.
- She says it is a good idea to give something up very early if it matters little to you and a lot to the other party.
- She says once the other side has received something, most people find it very hard not to reciprocate, and a no deal will feel like a loss to them.
- She says a negotiator should think about the issues that matter to the other party so they can be included in the mix.
- She says if people trust you they will build business with you, and that trust takes time but lasts.

### Defending against these techniques

- She says the first defence is to stay focused on your goal, and not to accept a deal just to honour what you did earlier.
- She says to ask a neutral party to check your judgment.
- She says to avoid public commitments unless you are certain.
- She says to ask what you would do in the absence of the current deal.

### Availability bias

- She describes an exercise where half the class estimates how many seven letter words on a book page end in ing, and the other half estimates a broader letter pattern that includes those words. People give a higher number for the ing words even though they are a subset.
- She explains that what the brain can recall easily is judged to be more common, and calls this availability bias.
- She says the use in negotiation is to make people familiar with an issue beforehand, by bringing it up casually so that nobody is shocked.
- Her example is a guest speaker who, before a board meeting with a serious issue, meets each board member one by one first.
- She says people respond more positively to familiar information than to new information, so a difficult issue should be raised a few times before it is negotiated.
- She says to be present in general, in meetings and at networking events, because people who remember your face are likely to like you more.
- From teaching on Zoom in 2021, she says she remembers only the students who kept their video on.

### What comes next

- She says confirmation bias is very strong, and that the next class will cover it.
- She says information needs to be presented in a way that conforms to the other person's beliefs, and that people then trust you more.

### From the written class notes in the file

- The notes record that commitments are most effective when they are active, public and effortful.
- The notes record the rule to use foot in the door with people you know and door in the face with people you do not know.
- The notes record argument dilution, with the advice to use only your three strongest arguments.
- The notes record that agreement on some issues makes people less likely to walk away from the deal.

## Examples and cases used

- The Economist subscription page: three options where one exists to make another look better.
- Starbucks sizes, car variants, telecom packages and software plan tables: everyday uses of a decoy.
- Negotiating Rationally: the book she cites for escalation of commitment between two chief executives.
- A board meeting: raising a serious issue with each member privately first, as a use of familiarity.

## Numbers given

- 59 dollars and 120 dollars: the prices she quotes for The Economist's online and print subscription options.
