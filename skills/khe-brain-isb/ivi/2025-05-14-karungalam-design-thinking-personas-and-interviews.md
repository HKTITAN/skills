# Design thinking, personas and customer interviews, Vishal Karungalam

A design thinking workshop taught by Vishal Karungalam. The first half argued for understanding the problem and the user before the solution and introduced personas. The second half, recorded only in typed notes, covered how to run a discovery interview, the biases that distort research and the value proposition canvas.

## What was said

### Do not design for yourself

- Vishal Karungalam calls it self-referential design when you reference yourself as the customer while designing a product.
- He says you do not have to be happy with your design, the customers have to be.
- He says founders are very different from their users and need to build for them and not for themselves.
- His example is Google Glass, a fantastic product whose makers could not say who it was for and had built it for themselves.

### Personas

- He defines a persona as a model, not a real person, that stands for a group of users with similar goals, motivations, pain points and behaviours.
- He says personas are expressions of users' goals, and that it is very difficult to get into people's behaviours and mental models.
- He says understanding a persona helps you decide what to do and also what not to do.
- He says understanding a persona also helps you judge commercial viability.
- He says personas are not built by sitting in a conference room. They come from research on the ground.
- He says you start with a segment and the personas emerge once you start talking to users.
- He says each interview or observation gives data, not a persona. Trends appear when you look across all the interviews, using affinity analysis.
- He says that because personas differ you have to build for different people, and that the same user can behave differently in a different situation.

### Buyers and users

- He asks whether you build for the buyer or the user, and uses Darwinbox as the case.
- He says Darwinbox first targeted the HR buyer, who wants compliance and integrations and wants to make HR's own life easy, not the most user-friendly product.
- He says employees are a captive audience for an HR system, because when HR says use it, they use it.
- He says migrating data into an HR system takes months, which makes customers slow to leave.
- He says the second problem is renewal. HR looks at user feedback and usage metrics when renewing, so after solving for the buyer you must solve for the user.
- He says Darwinbox then asked its customers for access to employees and built the user persona little by little.
- He says you cannot solve for everyone at once.

### Problem before solution

- He says customers do not know the solution they want, and the founder's job is to understand the problem and extract the insight.
- He says that if you think about the solution first and then the problem, you are like anybody else.
- He says Steve Jobs's genius was in finding an innovative solution to a problem that had always existed, and that the iPod solved a problem that existed in the Walkman era and before.
- He says BlackBerry users were happy and did not know the phone could be improved, but saw the value once somebody improved it.
- He says the idea of one person doing it all is a myth. Jobs decided, but many people gave him input.
- He says your job is not to fight the user and convince them that there is a problem.
- He says most founders do not end with the idea they started with.
- He says titles such as product manager or program manager do not matter.

### Using AI in research

- He says preparing interview questions used to take two to three hours and ChatGPT now does it in seconds, so use it for that.
- He says not to give ChatGPT the persona and ask it what that customer wants, because it is not there yet.
- He suggests giving interview transcripts to an AI tool and asking it to summarise the pains and gains for the value proposition canvas.

### Running a discovery interview

- He says not to tell people the solution you plan to offer and then ask questions around it. First understand their problems.
- He says an interview is not question and answer but a conversation between my views and your views.
- He says observation is as powerful for user research as asking questions.
- He says not to ask what will you do, and to ask what did you do, probing past experience.
- He says never to suggest answers or ask leading questions in a discovery interview.
- He says that when the interviewee says they do not understand the question, you are asking a long, complex question.
- He describes three levels of listening: hearing, listening in order to respond, and listening carefully. A sign of the second is cutting people off or responding too quickly.
- He says the best practice is not to take notes but to record the conversation, ask open-ended follow-up questions and look for non-verbal cues.
- The class listed what goes wrong in a poor interview: no small talk, no context or agenda set, never asking about the customer's problem, and the customer not understanding why the interview is happening.
- He says a founder should be able to tell an investor in three sentences what they are building, why, and what is in it for the listener.

### Biases in research

- He describes anchoring bias as going around validating what the first person told you.
- He describes authority bias as giving greater accuracy to the opinion of an authority figure, who may not understand what you are trying to do.
- He says participants in a focus group want to please the moderator and tell you what you want to hear, and that groupthink also plays out.
- He says that with only quantitative research you understand what is happening but not why.
- He describes zero risk bias as wanting 100 more interviews when the pattern is already clear.
- He says about 10 interviews start to show the trends and the big-picture problems, and beyond 20 interviews you have 90% confidence.
- He describes survivor bias as leaving out the data points that no longer exist, with air force aircraft as the example.
- He describes availability bias as depending too much on the people and data that are easy to reach.
- He says the best way to do a quantitative assessment is to put a product out in the market and get feedback.

### Value proposition canvas

- He says to ask your real persona three types of questions about the problem, covering pains, gains and expectations.
- His grocery store example treats discounts as a gain, quality as a must-have and waiting in line as a pain.
- His validation routine is to conduct the interviews, use the cheat sheets, map a value proposition sheet for each persona and identify pains, gains and expectations.

## Examples and cases used

- Google Glass: a strong product built for its makers, with no clear user.
- MakeMyTrip: leisure travellers enjoy exploring and are price sensitive, business travellers do not care about price, and corporate users get an uncluttered site that flags what is in or out of company policy.
- A truck maker that sold poorly in Kerala and southern Tamil Nadu: the cause was a design problem felt only by drivers who wear lungis, which fixing marketing and positioning had not solved.
- Darwinbox: solve for the HR buyer first, then for the employee user to win renewals.
- iPod and iPhone: new solutions to problems that had long existed.

## Numbers given

- His interview counts: about 10 interviews for the first trends, beyond 20 for 90% confidence.
- His MakeMyTrip figure: the leisure site is built so that a visitor stays at least 12 minutes.
