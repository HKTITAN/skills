# Startup legal essentials, Anil Advani

Anil Advani, a startup lawyer at Inventus Law who works with Indian and US founders, joined the cohort by video. The recording starts after his introduction. The session ran as questions and answers on vesting, founder agreements, entity choice, angel rounds and trademarks, with a short walk through the first stages of a startup's life cycle.

## What was said

### Vesting

- Advani says vesting protects the company: if a founder leaves, the company can buy back the shares that have not vested.
- He says vesting also protects the founders from each other, so the ones who stay do not keep working for the equity of the one who left.
- He says a departed founder holding fully vested shares leaves the company with dead equity.
- He says no venture investor will invest in a company with 10% or more of dead equity.
- He says founders should buy their shares outright on day one, when the value is tiny, so that tax arises only when they sell.
- He calls this reverse vesting: the founder owns the shares from the start, and agrees the company may buy back the unvested part at the original price on leaving.
- His example uses the standard four year vesting: a founder with 50% who leaves after two years keeps 25%, and the company can buy back the other 25%.
- He says the same vesting concept applies to an Indian company, with different tax treatment and terminology, and is now standard for Indian startups.
- He says vesting applies to employees and advisors as well as founders.

### Founder mistakes

- He says starting with fully vested shares because the co-founders are old friends is a common mistake.
- He says that in his experience three founders splitting equity equally always ends in a conflict of two against one.
- He says many things can be fixed later, such as a missed filing, but a founder dispute that was not structured for on day one is very expensive or impossible to fix.
- He says founders avoid deciding who is CEO and require all three to agree on everything, and that this does not work.
- His rule is that all founders hear the same information, and one person, the CEO, is the face and the decision maker for things like investors and legal matters.
- "Good fences make good neighbors."
- He says an unwritten understanding with a friend who helped early is a recipe for a later claim to a large share of the company.
- He says no investor will invest in a company where a possible co-founder claims to own half.
- He says nobody should do actual work for the company, including advising, building, hiring or investing, without a written agreement on scope and compensation.
- He says anyone who works for the business is owed something, and without a written agreement they can claim whatever they want, even an intern.
- He says the difference between a claim that costs months of legal trouble and no claim is one piece of paper.
- He says if co-founders cannot agree on day one, when the shares are worth little, they will not agree later when the company is valuable, and they should walk away and stay friends.
- His sequence for bringing someone in: check personal fit, discuss roles and time, check for conflicts with their employer, agree equity and vesting, have a lawyer write it up, and only then let them start work.
- He warns that if someone builds for you while employed elsewhere, the employer could claim what they built.

### Disputes between founders

- He says disputes are handled through how equity, board seats and decision rights are structured at the start.
- He says a majority founder who holds the board seat controls the decision, and the company can remove a co-founder who is not working out.
- He says if that co-founder's equity is subject to vesting, the company buys back the unvested part and both company and remaining founder are protected.

### Option pool

- He says the initial 100% is divided between the founder pool and the option pool, and the founders should work backwards from the hires and advisors they will need.
- He says a typical option pool is 10%, within a usual range of 10 to 20%.
- He says the pool should be larger if a senior hire such as a CTO is still to come, and he says such a hire may need 10% alone.
- He says the pool should cover all hiring from the start up to the Series A.

### Entity choice

- He says investors will not invest in an LLP, so a business that plans to raise outside money will have to restructure.
- He says a private limited company shields the founder from personal liability for the business, except in a case of fraud.
- He says an online business should weigh whether an LLP protects personal assets enough if, for example, customer data is stolen.
- He says founders in India should not assume they must be an Indian company in legal structure.
- He says the choice between an Indian company and a Delaware holding company depends on where customers, growth and investors will come from.
- He says a founder leaving a failed business for a new idea should close the old company and start a clean new one, because running the new idea through the old entity complicates it.
- He says an investor in the old business took an uncapped upside and so cannot expect a capped downside, and the founder is not legally required to give them anything.
- He says the founder may choose to give that investor some equity in the new company.

### Angel rounds

- He says an angel round has no legal definition and is the first money from friends, family and people who know you.
- He says to raise only enough to reach reference customers that let you approach institutional investors, because early money dilutes the most.
- He says plain equity shares with few extra rights are the simplest instrument for friends and family, since the value is fixed today.
- He says CCDs are also simple to document for angels, and that institutional investors will almost always require preference shares.
- He says the angels who put in the least money cause the most problems, such as lawsuits or forcing a buyout at the next round.
- He says to limit the first round to about three to five angels.
- He says angels should get basic information rights and no veto or other special rights.
- He says a business built for the Indian consumer market should raise from investors and angel groups who know that market, and he questions seeking foreign investors in the hope of a higher valuation.

### Trademarks and names

- He says a founder who invests in a name should secure the domain and file for trademark protection early.
- He says founders often run for two years under a name and then receive a trademark complaint when raising a Series A, and the forced rebrand is expensive and damages the brand.
- He says trademark registration is both defensive and offensive: it tells you now if you infringe someone else, and it lets you stop others later.
- He says a trademark can be filed in a founder's personal name, but the company should own it because investors will require that.
- His advice to a founder holding an Indian trademark personally is to transfer it to the Indian company and have the company file in the US.

### Legal tech

- He says legal tech is a crowded market and he has not seen a product his firm wanted to buy.
- He says the winners so far are large legal data providers adding AI tools on top of data they already own, and firms sign multi-year contracts with them.
- He says the opportunity is in narrow problems named by lawyers themselves, and gives billing for boutique firms of under 100 lawyers as one.
- He says some law firms are hiring developers to build their own tools.

## Examples and cases used

- Reliance: he contrasts family run businesses, where a buy back right over the promoter's shares would make no sense, with startups that start from scratch.
- Facebook: he says the Harvard students who believed the idea was theirs never had Mark Zuckerberg sign a paper saying he was an engineer working for them.
- Google: an employee building a product on the side, used to show the risk of an employer's claim on the work.
- LexisNexis and Thomson Reuters: legal data owners he sees winning with AI tools.
- QuickBooks: the kind of simple tool he would pay for if it were tailored to how a small law firm bills.

## Numbers given

- Standard vesting in his example: four years.
- Dead equity that stops a venture investment: 10% or more.
- Option pool: 10% typical, 10 to 20% range.
- Angel round size: he says 100,000 dollars was a good angel round twenty years ago and 5 million dollars is called an angel round in Silicon Valley now.
- Angels to avoid in number: those putting in 5,000 to 10,000 dollars. Angels he calls friendly: those putting in 250,000 to 300,000 dollars.
