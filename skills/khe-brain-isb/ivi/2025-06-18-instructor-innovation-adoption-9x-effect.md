# Innovation adoption and the 9x effect

The same marketing instructor as the behavioural pricing class continued with how consumers adopt new products. The class covered the gap between how firms and consumers value an innovation, a two by two of behaviour change against product change, and ways to reduce or manage consumer resistance. It ended with an exercise in which founders placed their own products on the grid. That peer discussion is left out.

## What was said

### How consumers really behave

- The instructor says founders carry their own picture of how customers think, while real consumers are emotional and lazy.
- The instructor attributes to Clayton Christensen the argument that most new products fail, and gives one reason as a mismatch between what the firm thinks is going on and what consumers think is going on.

### The 9x effect

- The instructor says consumers are sceptical about a new product, cannot see the need for it, are satisfied with what they have, and treat what they already own as the status quo.
- The endowment effect example: a pen that cost 1,000 rupees is valued by its new owner at 1,500 or 2,000 the moment it is theirs. People overvalue what they have.
- The instructor says this is why people keep a poor service they have already chosen, and that it contributes about three times to the gap.
- Firms are convinced their innovation works, readily see the value of the change, and treat their own innovation as the benchmark. The instructor says this inflates their perception of the product.
- The instructor calls the resulting gap between what the firm sees and what the consumer sees the 9x effect, and says this gap is what a founder has to bridge.
- The instructor says frameworks like this are guideposts and the exact multiple does not matter.

### The behaviour change and product change grid

- The grid has two axes: the degree of behaviour change asked of the consumer, and the degree of product change, which the instructor defines from the supply side as what it takes the industry to make and distribute the product.
- Low behaviour change and low product change is an easy sell. The instructor's example is a new soda, where manufacturing, production and distribution are like every other soda.
- High behaviour change with low product change is the quadrant the recording calls a short failure: the consumer is asked to change without being shown value or differentiation.
- High behaviour change and high product change is a long haul: it takes time for people to change.
- Low behaviour change and high product change is a smash hit.
- The instructor says innovations sit across the whole grid and are not all long hauls. A ground up change such as horses to cars is a long haul, and an iteration on an existing product is not.
- The instructor says packaging counts as behaviour change only when it adds steps for the consumer.
- The instructor places electric vehicles in the middle on product change, because much of the manufacturing is the same.

### Making innovations work

- The first approach is to minimise consumer resistance by making behaviourally compatible products. The instructor's key idea is that whatever the product, try to avoid behaviour change.
- The class notes add two more ways to minimise resistance: target new consumers who are not entrenched, and find believers.
- The second approach is to manage resistance. Where there is heavy product and behaviour change, brace for long innovation cycles.
- Managing a long haul means awareness campaigns, getting people to see the benefits and working at it over time.
- The instructor says building for the long haul means spending a lot of money, as Uber did, and needing investors willing to fund that.
- The class notes add giving ten times better value as a way to manage resistance.
- The instructor says most people have a status quo mentality even when the new option is cheaper.

### The exercise

- The instructor asks each founder to pick the feature that differentiates the product and write down the benefit they see.
- The founder then tells two other people the feature only, not the benefit, and asks what they see in it and what problems they would have if they bought it.
- The instructor says to do this because a founder writing about their own product writes only favourable things.
- The questions to place a product are how much of the customer's routine has to change, and how much of a difference the product change makes.

## Examples and cases used

- Soda flavours: the easy sell, since supply side factors are set.
- A washing machine that uses about a cup of water: high behaviour change, because people assume that without water clothes are not clean.
- Mobile phones: used for calling 15 to 20 years ago, now for many things. The instructor says they were not sold on all of those uses at the start.
- Uber: a long haul that needed heavy spending.
- Hybrid cars such as the Toyota Prius: substantial product change while the driver has nothing new to worry about. The instructor says India's neglect of hybrids is bad policy.
- iPod: the instructor calls it one of the big smash hits.
- 5G and the move to fibre optic cable: high product change that the end consumer does not have to adapt to.
- Telemedicine: it existed before COVID, asks for a huge behaviour change, and was a long haul.
- Diet Coke: the instructor sees it as targeting a small segment that does not want sugar and as not high on product change, and calls Coke Zero closer to an easy sell.
- Iodised salt and Himalayan salt: the instructor says fortification through salt was accepted because behaviour change is low, and that Himalayan salt is no different from other salt but has marketing built around it.

## Numbers given

- 9x: the instructor's name for the gap between the firm's and the consumer's view of an innovation.
- 1,000 rupees paid and 1,500 to 2,000 asked: the instructor's endowment effect illustration.
