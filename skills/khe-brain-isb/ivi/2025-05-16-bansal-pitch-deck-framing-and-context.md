# Pitch deck bootcamp, framing, context and narrative, Mohit Bansal

A later stretch of the same pitch deck bootcamp with Mohit Bansal of DeckRooster. He argues that the facts of a business are fixed but its framing is not, separates framing from context and narrative from founding story, and walks through reframings his firm did for client companies. Parts of the session are his answers to founders about their own pitches, which are left out here.

## What was said

### Framing

- Mohit Bansal says a deck should not tell investors everything there is. It has an objective, and only material that helps make the founder's points belongs in it.
- He separates two layers. The hard truths of a business, what it makes, who it sells to, its numbers and its geography, are static.
- On top sits a layer he calls framing, which is fluid and changes how outsiders perceive the same facts. In his words, "perception is all that matters".
- He says everything has a default framing: every business, every target group, every product.
- If the founder does nothing about framing, the default is assumed to be accurate, the listener attaches many assumptions to it at once, and it is then very hard to move them.
- He says most founders treat the business as fixed and put all their attention on the pitch, while his firm starts by fixing the framing.
- He says a listener's conviction forms before the pitch, from simply hearing the target group, the need and the solution. The pitch sits on top of that.
- He says one business can be positioned in five different ways that are all true, and each needs reasons and data on customer behaviour behind it.
- He treats the USP as a derivative of the frame. The USP is for the customer, the reason they choose you over someone else.
- He says reframing can change how large the market looks, the revenue per user, who the likely winner is, and the margins and scale.
- He says framing is not faking, and that he wants to find the truth of the business.

### Context

- He separates framing from context: framing is how you frame the thing, and context is information you add around it.
- He defines context as something the founder understands and the investor does not, or something they believe differently.
- He says adding context at that point changes what people believe.
- He says that if founders do not build their arguments, listeners build their own.
- He says the argument always comes first, and data only supports it.

### Narrative is not the founding story

- He says founders asked for a story start with how they began the company, and that story is not very helpful from a business point of view.
- His narrative is built from what you do, who you do it for, what need you serve, the world those customers live in, the need gap, how the world is shifting and how the business is placed in that shift.
- He compares this communication layer to design: a layer added to something to make it better.

### Where the reframing comes from

- He believes there is usually more in a business than the founder has managed to articulate.
- His firm treats a founder's strong feeling about the business as the starting point, and keeps asking questions to find where it comes from.
- He qualifies this: it holds for a reasonable founder who is unbiased with themselves.
- He warns founders not to borrow a fashionable label because it worked for someone else. They have to work out whether it is true of their own business.

## Examples and cases used

- A cafe, his hypothetical: framed as a meeting place and not a coffee shop, citing CCD. He then narrows it to working professionals avoiding the office after COVID, calls the cafe an alternate work environment, and says he is redefining the market itself.
- The cafe, on context: he adds that this group spends 3 to 5 hours a day in cafes, not the usual 30 minutes, and that if the market looks small he would add context on the growth of flexible work and co-working.
- Stage, a regional language streaming company his firm worked with: the audience was reframed from "Bharat" to people with a regional mother tongue. He says Haryanvi content is watched by Haryanvi speakers outside Haryana and abroad, which answered the worry that revenue per user would be low and that it was a poor man's product.
- A company that called itself a social media platform: he says that label invited comparison with ShareChat, heavily funded and reliant on advertising. Reframing it as a local network let the company explain revenue sources it already had.
- Excitel, a home broadband company: framed as serving unstructured urban areas while Airtel and Jio focus on structured urban areas of gated communities and high rises. He says the context was that wires are open in such areas and get stolen or broken, that the large operators are not organised to work there, and that Excitel partners with local cable operators.
- Excitel, on whether homes still need a wired connection as mobile internet gets faster: he says the answer was context about homes moving from one device to many, with smart TVs and connected devices that mobile data cannot serve.
- A short video learning app: he says it had traction but investors asked whether it would keep running. The deck defined casual learning as something everyone already does, and presented the company as a media app and not edtech, because edtech was not getting investment at the time.
- A learning app, his illustration: calling it entertainment first and not edtech, with retention figures offered as the proof that it is different.
