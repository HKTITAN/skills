# Term sheets and fundraising terms, Kaushik Rajan

Kaushik Rajan, a venture capital and acquisitions lawyer who also invests, took the cohort through the terms a founder meets in a funding round. He had slides on the legal clauses but ran most of the session as questions and answers, and he said several of his rules of thumb were not on the slides. The session followed an earlier bootcamp that had covered the basics of term sheets, shareholders agreements and due diligence.

## What was said

### The documents and the process

- The term sheet is a two-page summary of what the shareholders agreement is going to contain.
- The term sheet is not binding. It is replaced by the definitive agreements, which are the share subscription agreement and the shareholders agreement.
- The share subscription agreement covers the terms of the investment itself and the representations the company gives about its legal and financial health, along with the disclosures made against those representations.
- The shareholders agreement governs the relationship from the point of investment until the point of exit, so he calls it an ongoing partnership agreement.
- Closing means the point at which the money is received.
- He says deals in 2021 were closing in three to four weeks, and that the process slowed once the funding winter arrived.
- The funding winter hit the funds as much as the startups, because funds also have to raise money and are finding that difficult.
- He compares due diligence to a root canal examination and says the team gets smoother at it with each round.
- Receiving the money is not the end of the process for the founder, because the founder carries that investor for the rest of the journey.
- The shareholders agreement is restated at every round, so an investor's rights at seed are replaced by different rights at later stages.
- He describes investors as playing musical chairs among themselves over who holds which right and who sits on the board, while the founder stays at the table throughout.

### Investors and what they want

- He lists four kinds of investor: big VCs, micro VCs, angel networks and pure angels. Each operates in its own way, and angels sometimes ask for things only a fund would ask for.
- Before taking money from an investor, speak to other founders that investor has funded. Ask what they agreed to, what they refused, and what the experience has been after the investment.
- Most investors say they want to exit in four or five years. He says most of them are in fact hoping to stay in until the IPO, because there is no reason to leave a company that keeps going up.
- He describes the investor's frame as a relay. The first investor enters at 100 and wants the founder to keep running until 500, the next takes it from 5 to 25, and the next from 25 to 75.
- Check whether a fund has already invested in a competitor at the same stage as you. A portfolio company at a different stage is less of a threat and can be an opportunity, because the fund may suggest a merger.
- He says a family office helps when it comes from your sector or has scaled a brand of its own. Otherwise it behaves exactly as a VC does and uses the same advisers.
- He says the venture capital ecosystem in the United States is founder friendly and that in India it is not.

### Advisers, fees and angel networks

- A founder needs three advisers for a round: a lawyer, a chartered accountant and a company secretary.
- Once the work is handed to advisers, let them do their job and stay out of the way.
- He says two valuation certificates used to be required, one from a merchant banker and one from a registered valuer, and that now only the registered valuer's certificate is required.
- A VC will push its diligence fees down to the startup. He tells founders to agree to pay those fees only at closing.
- If the diligence cost cannot be pushed to closing, he advises getting the upfront share as low as possible, to 20 or 25 per cent.
- Angel networks pool many angels into a single entry on the cap table and take a cut for doing so.
- He says that cut used to be paid only at closing, and that some networks now ask for money upfront before the deal closes.
- He has seen founders who paid an advance go on to accept bad terms because they had already paid. He tells such founders to walk away instead.
- His advice against a bad angel network deal is to keep the option of speaking to several investors and to research the networks.
- The investor who leads the round does the diligence and sends the external diligence report to the co-investors.
- Negotiate with the investor directly and not with the investor's lawyer, because the lawyer only works from instructions.

### Loans and debt on the books

- Investors will want founder loans to the company converted into equity, because they will not let founders repay themselves with the investors' money.
- He says that as an investor he would stay away from companies carrying term loans, because banks get their money out first if the company fails.
- The order of priority he gives is secured lenders, then unsecured creditors, then holders of CCPS or preference shares.
- An investor who sees bank debt will ask the company to discharge it or to show a plan for repaying it, and will look at the debt to equity ratio.
- A small, short working capital facility is less of a problem than a term loan.

### The founder's priorities in a term sheet

- He ranks the founder's priorities as liquidity, then securities, then reverse vesting, then governance powers.
- Keep about 5 per cent of the founder's own shares outside the lock-in so the founder can sell when personal needs arise. His example is a founder who owns 70 per cent of the company, for whom this is 3.5 per cent of the company.
- He says that 5 per cent may shrink to about 2.5 per cent of the founder's holding after further fundraises, because the number of shares increases.
- The free shares are not easy to sell. The market is not liquid and the founder cannot sell to a competitor.
- Under reverse vesting, the founder's shares go into lock-in and vest back to the founder over time.
- A founder on a second or third round can ask for part of the shares to vest at closing, as credit for the years already spent on the company.
- Watch the accelerated vesting terms, which tie into termination for cause.
- The definition of cause is critical because the consequence is the loss of shares. He lists sexual harassment, a public controversy that brings the company into disrepute, siphoning of money, and transferring shares when that was not allowed.
- He says founders cannot navigate the definition of cause alone and need help with it.
- He says most founders agree to whatever is put in front of them at the first term sheet, and he sees the result when they come to him at the next round.
- In the final negotiation, keep one point in hand to concede. He calls it a penny point, and says that once every point is given away the founder has nothing left to trade.

### Dilution, salaries and runway

- His rule of thumb is that 10 per cent dilution for a round is a great deal and should be taken.
- He says 20 per cent is about as high as the whole round should go, and that above 25 per cent the founder should consider walking away.
- Dilution is always calculated on the post-money valuation.
- Investors look at founder salaries as a percentage of the amount being raised.
- Investors expect the money to last about 24 months. He says the expectation varies between 21 and 27 months, because that is the time needed to raise the next round.

### Lead investors

- He calls finding a lead investor a game of who bells the cat.
- In his example of a 4 crore round, most angel networks say they will put in 1 to 1.5 crore. The one who issues the term sheet is the lead and drives the diligence.
- The lead often puts in less than the co-investors, who then ask for more rights because they are putting in more.
- When two co-leads each ask for a board seat, tell them they have one seat between them and can decide who sits or rotate it.
- He says there are two meanings of lead in the ecosystem: the one who puts the most cash on the table, and the one who first validates the company and issues a term sheet.
- If someone is willing to put in 1 crore of a 4 crore round, engage with them, because once there is a lead the co-investors are easier to find.
- Syndicating the rest of the round takes time, which is another reason to push the lead's fee to later.

### Priced rounds and convertible notes

- A priced round fixes a valuation and a stake for the money. It can come in through CCPS or equity, and he says all investors ask for CCPS.
- In a convertible round the valuation is not set today. The money converts later at a discount to the next round.
- The cap protects the investor from a very high valuation at conversion. The floor protects the founder.
- Investors require a qualified financing, meaning a priced round, within a set period. He says the period ranges from 18 to 36 months and is usually about two years.
- He advises keeping that period at two years or more so the founder is not under pressure to raise.
- He says a discount of 15 to 20 per cent on an annualised basis is where the market is. He calls 15 per cent founder friendly and 20 per cent investor friendly.
- On an annualised basis, a round closed after two years at 20 per cent means a 40 per cent discount. His other example is 15 per cent multiplied by 1.75 for a round closed at 21 months.
- He says many angels ask for a 50 per cent discount, and that founders who do not know the market rate slip into agreeing.
- He suggests setting the discount by year, one figure for the first year and one for the second, so the founder is not pressed to close by a particular month.
- The note converts at the discounted price when that is lower than the cap, and at the cap when the discounted price is higher.
- He says many founders set the floor too high, and that this is a mistake.
- A high floor does not let the founder escape. His example is a floor of 200 crore followed by a down round at 150 crore, where the note holders have to be brought to 150 crore by issuing additional shares.
- Set the floor at a level you are confident you will never raise below.
- He tells founders not to fret over whether the cap makes the stake 7 per cent or 7.5 per cent.
- He says valuation is an art and not a science, and that convertibles exist to avoid the pain of that discussion.
- If the investor does not ask for a cap, it is fine to go without one. If the investor asks for a cap, the founder should think about a floor.
- A note with no cap and no floor is a pure discount. He calls it simply the time value of money and not a bad arrangement.
- For an accelerator taking 1 or 2 per cent, he says it is fine to accept terms without a floor.
- A note can be extended for later investors at a lower discount. Earlier investors will argue they deserve a higher discount than people entering six months later.
- He says he has not seen two successive convertible note rounds.

### Down rounds, liquidation preference and exit rights

- He says a down round should be avoided, and compares it to watching "your baby undergo open heart surgery".
- In a down round the company has to make good the investors who came in earlier at a higher valuation, and that becomes messy.
- Anti-dilution can cascade through several earlier rounds, because the extra shares issued to one investor can breach the protection of another.
- Anti-dilution is harder to work out when the earlier money came in on a convertible, because it is unclear whether to use the conversion value, the floor or the cap.
- Liquidation preference applies on a liquidity event, whether an upside sale or a distress sale, and works last in, first out.
- He says a 1x liquidation preference is the norm, that 1.5x should be negotiated, and that anything beyond is not market.
- A pre-emptive right is the shareholder's ability to maintain its stake in later rounds.
- On tag-along, he says the investor's right becomes a full tag once the founder sells down past 50 per cent, meaning the investor's whole stake is sold along with the founder's.
- If the buyer cannot afford both stakes, the investor's stake has to be fitted into the buyer's budget first.
- Investors agree not to sell their shares to a competitor. The competitor list is refreshed every year, and he advises negotiating for a list of ten names.
- The investor's counter is that if the exit period passes without an exit, it can sell to anyone. He says that is market.
- A founder buyback clause means the founder has to buy the investor out, which the founder may not have the money for. He says to negotiate out of it and that asking for it is not market.

### Indemnities

- Specific indemnities cover known problems found in diligence that cannot be fixed by going back in time.
- Limitation of liability means the investor cannot claim for small matters. His example is no claims below 1 lakh.
- Small claims are collected in a basket, and there is an overall cap so that liability does not exceed the amount invested.
- He says investors do not normally raise indemnity claims while the relationship is good. They keep them as a weapon for when a dispute arises.
- His answer to founders who find these clauses one-sided is that the risk moves to the investor the moment the money is invested.
- He says it is better to disclose more information than less in the disclosure process.

### ESOPs

- An ESOP has three stages: grant, vesting and exercise.
- Options cannot be granted until the company has an ESOP pool and an ESOP scheme, so earlier promises of options to employees are not valid until then.
- Most investors ask for an ESOP pool of 10 per cent on a post-money basis.
- His arithmetic is that when the investor takes 10 per cent, the pool has to be 11.11 per cent before the round to end at 10 per cent after it. When the investor takes 20 per cent, the pool has to be 12.5 per cent.
- There is a minimum of one year between grant and the first vesting.
- Vesting is 25 per cent at the end of each of four years.
- He explains ESOPs through a swimming school, where children spend a year in the small pool, learn four strokes over four years, and move to the main pool on exercise.
- Exercise converts the option into an equity share, and that is when actual dilution happens for everyone on the cap table.
- Until options are vested and exercised, the investor holds more than its stated percentage. The company's filings show actual cap table percentages and not fully diluted ones, so founders should not be alarmed by the higher figure.
- Employees are taxed twice on ESOPs. The first time is at exercise, on the difference between the fair value of the share and the price paid, even though they receive no cash.
- The second tax is at sale, on the difference between the sale value and the value at exercise.
- An employee can delay exercise until just before the company is sold, so the tax is paid from the sale money. The cost is paying short-term instead of long-term capital gains tax.
- A cash plus ESOP offer cannot be valued for an employee until the company has a valuation, which normally comes only with a fundraise.
- Management stock options for founders are possible at Series A or Series B, after heavy dilution. They need the investor's agreement and are hard to negotiate at an early stage.
- He says he has seen management stock options more often in D2C companies than in software companies.

### Whether to raise at all

- He advises bootstrapping as far as possible.
- His suggested order is a strategic angel from your sector who has been through the cycle, then friends and family, then outside funding once revenue is at a respectable level.
- Going out for funding too early exposes the founder to people who will take advantage.
- A single strategic angel taking 5 to 10 per cent is worth it, and he says it saves the founder a year.
- He says fundraising is a tool and not the definition of success, and asks founders to be clear on their own definition.
- He says, "Operational break-even is the holy grail."
- He says investors lose their usefulness to the founder once the company passes the stage they invest in.
- He says even VCs do not know as much as they claim, because if they did they would not need to invest in new startups.
- He knows bootstrapped founders who reached 1 to 2.5 million in ARR with a remote team of eight or nine and who will never raise, because they value independence.
- Take money from an investor who shares your vision for the company when it will get you there faster.
- He says building alone is a marathon you can pace, and that entering the venture capital race means sprinting every leg of it.

## Examples and cases used

- Indian angel networks: he uses the newer networks that charge founders upfront fees to show how far angel investing has moved from what it was.
- Bootstrapped software founders he knows: 1 to 2.5 million in ARR with eight or nine remote staff, used to show that a company can be built without venture money.

## Numbers given

- 3 to 4 weeks: how fast deals closed in 2021, in his telling.
- 5 per cent of the founder's own shares: what he advises keeping outside lock-in.
- 10, 20 and 25 per cent: his thresholds for round dilution, as great, about the ceiling, and walk away.
- 24 months, within a range of 21 to 27: the runway investors expect from a round.
- 15 to 20 per cent annualised: his market range for a convertible note discount.
- 18 to 36 months, usually about two years: the period for reaching a qualified financing.
- 1x: the liquidation preference he calls the norm.
- 10 per cent post-money: the ESOP pool most investors ask for.
- 25 per cent a year for four years, after a one year minimum: the ESOP vesting schedule he describes.
- 4 crore round with a 1 to 1.5 crore lead cheque: his example of how angel network rounds are put together.
