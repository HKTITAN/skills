# Oncology venture from MVP to scale, Sonali Srungaram

Sonali Srungaram, founder and CEO of a distributed cancer care company in Hyderabad, gave the second of her guest lectures on lean startup. She walked through the numbers of her own MVP, put its go or no-go decision to the class, and then told what happened with staff, partners and investors as the company grew. The session ran as a talk with frequent questions from the room.

## What was said

### How the MVP was designed

- Sonali Srungaram says the MVP covered three kinds of cancer treatment with different levels of ownership. Medical oncology had full-time doctors, full-time staff and wards the company built itself. Surgery had a full-time surgeon in a rented operating theatre. Radiation used part-time doctors and outsourced staff and equipment.
- She says the company kept its process design proprietary where it owned the work, and did not try to innovate on radiation because it did not own it.
- She says the service ran at three levels. Level 1 centres in the city ran full days six days a week. Level 2 centres about 20 km out ran half days. Level 3 sites in districts had no resident staff and a team travelled there.
- She says the centres sat inside existing hospitals that had no oncology programme, on a revenue share and with no rent, and the company fitted out the space itself to show commitment.
- She says the aim was that a cancer patient in a district gets the same quality of service as a patient in the city, so the outcome was fixed and each part of the supply chain was designed to deliver it.
- She says early investors suggested a structure with an operating company for staff and processes and a separate doctors' entity, because doctors do not want to report to consultants. Her doctors sit in an LLP that the operating company hires and that gets a profit share.
- She says an MVP will never look like the final product, and it is enough if it reflects roughly 80% of what you think you can do.
- She says she could not do all three treatments fully, so she did medical oncology completely because it was the more profitable, did half of surgery and left radiation.
- She says resources usually decide how much an MVP can cover, and where there are no advisers who have done it before the founder has to trust instinct and take the call.
- She says being a new category means nobody is like you, and people will compare you to the nearest thing they know, so you will struggle to define yourself.

### Runway and the unit economics of the MVP

- She says the founders put in 4 crore rupees of their own savings first, sized by what they had after setting aside two years of family expenses.
- She says equity between the two founders was not split 50-50 just because they were married, and they discussed the worst case before starting.
- Her capex for the MVP was 25 lakh for each of two Level 1 centres, 12 lakh for each of two Level 2 centres and nothing for the district sites, about 75 lakh in total.
- She says fit-out money cannot be recovered. You can sell a bed or a monitor but not the paint job.
- Her monthly operating cost was 30 lakh: 12 lakh for four oncologists, 8 lakh for four support teams, 2 lakh for travel and 8 lakh for marketing and admin.
- She says 325 lakh left after capex divided by 30 lakh a month gave about ten months of runway with zero revenue.
- She says every founder must do this runway sum, and if you are halfway through the runway without results you must stop and re-evaluate.
- She says she paid oncologists 3 lakh a month against a market rate of 2.5 lakh, and paid the surgeon the same as the medical oncologist so that the two would work as a team in remote sites.
- She says the partner hospital kept 15% of the bill on chemotherapy and 55% on surgery, and overall the company kept about half of what the patient paid.
- At six months the centres did 18 chemotherapies, six surgeries and five radiations a month. Patients paid about 26 lakh and the company's revenue was 13 lakh.
- She says the company launched on 4 February 2020, COVID arrived in March, and her one star doctor left in April. Travel limits meant the district MVP could only start in January 2022, and about 1 crore went on keeping the team in place while waiting.
- She says that after a year growth was steady and not doubling, and the company still gave staff a 10% increment because losing a doctor or nurse would end the business.
- She says that at 18 months revenue was still rising slowly and the company was still making a loss.
- She says most MVPs do not give a clear-cut go or no-go answer, and founders face a choice between two rights or two wrongs.
- She says the reason revenue was slow was high stickiness in healthcare, and the same stickiness makes it hard for a later entrant, so the early grind becomes a deterrent to others.

### Measuring the process and the customer

- She says a process designer should not expect a process to work for more than a year. Her standard is to test processes every six months, or once a year if you are busy.
- She says processes are also reviewed when one fails, and customer feedback is used to tell whether a problem belongs to one centre or to the process itself.
- She says staff sent to a remote centre will always report that everything is good, so data has to be collected. Each chemotherapy has ten steps, vitals are recorded every 30 minutes and the paperwork is uploaded the same day.
- She says the company tracks each patient in Salesforce the way a project is tracked, so a patient can take the next chemotherapy at any centre, like a bank branch.
- She says the data showed most patients take 21 days to move from an outpatient visit to treatment, and they spend those days shopping around, so the team calls after 5 days and after 10 days.
- She gives the outpatient visit as 800 rupees and the treatment ticket as about 3.5 lakh, which is why she works hard on that conversion.
- She says cancer decisions are made and paid for by the family, so the payer is different from the patient, and the company began counselling carers as well.
- She says founders do not need a fancy system. Put every transaction into Excel, do it yourself for the first few weeks, and look at what the data says.
- She says a founder should have access to financial data, operational metrics, employee data and payroll, and go back to the data the moment something feels off.
- She warns against analysis paralysis. You will never have enough data, investors notice when you have not moved in six months, and you have to take a call.

### How patients behave

- She says district patients did not walk into the district centre. They came to Hyderabad for opinions first, and the company had to convince them there to go back and take chemotherapy near home.
- She says nobody arrives saying they have cancer. They reach an oncologist through another specialist, and existing hospitals had already divided up those referring doctors, so breaking in was hard.
- She says direct to consumer marketing was not very helpful at first for a super speciality.
- She says patients do not switch provider mid-treatment, so the only chance is to catch them early when the cancer is being discovered.
- She says surgery in a tier 2 town costs less because the anaesthetist and the bed cost about half the city rate, and a 25% cut in the bill with the same doctor makes patients think about staying near home.
- She says she accepts not being the first choice now and aims to be the second or third choice until word spreads.
- She says insurers would not empanel a day care chemotherapy centre without a radiation bunker, so claims went through the partner hospital, which paid her six months later.

### Raising money

- She says the company raised a million dollars in two halves from two groups, and the work done inside Hyderabad during COVID helped raise it.
- She says the first investor backed the numbers and the second, a Singapore fund, backed the impact of taking cancer care outside the metros.
- She says a founder should research everything about a fund before the meeting, learn its ethos and tailor the story a little, without making anything up.
- She says big funds send a junior analyst first, and that analyst should be treated with respect.
- She says investors helped with introductions to founders, funds and hospitals but not with strategy.
- She says the company spends on a plan only after the money is in the bank.
- She says after a 7.5 million dollar Series A the company has outgrown venture ticket sizes and must go to private equity. She says venture funds will come in before a company makes a profit, while private equity looks only at profitability. Her target is to break even by December 2026.

### People, pay and culture

- She says large hospitals expect a doctor to bring in about seven times their salary in revenue each month, and her offer to doctors was that they do clinical work while the company does sales and management.
- She says doctors began asking to join once that message spread among a graduating cohort.
- She says a competitor then hired away a star doctor at 5 lakh a month against her 3 lakh, and he signed before telling her.
- She says you cannot keep an employee who wants to leave and it is their right to go, but she insisted on the notice terms because letting it pass would set a precedent for everyone watching.
- She says employment contracts are hard to enforce in India. She settled for one month of notice and one month of salary as compensation, and that clause is now in every doctor's contract.
- She says medical oncologists graduate at fixed times, so there are only three hiring windows in a year and a departure can cost months of revenue.
- She says a leader has to act as the CEO of the company and not as the non-confrontational person they may be, and has to judge when to be charitable and when to stand firm.
- She says culture matters most in an experiential service because the founder cannot control how a patient feels with a nurse at a distant centre.
- She recommends one to two months of classroom training and two months of field training before staff are sent out, with grooming and conduct treated as part of the job.
- She says the company gives nutrition, psycho-oncology and genetic counselling free to every inpatient and calls families with condolences when a patient dies.
- She says staff are paid enough that they do not need tips, and after a cash theft by a low-paid employee the company raised pay for that whole grade so that take-home was 18,000 rupees.
- She says about 60% of her nurses are working mothers, drawn by no night shifts and Sundays off, and the company runs a five and a half day week with two Saturdays off a month.
- She says the company trains doctors to build their own personal brand even though they may leave, and gives part-time doctors the same training as full-time ones.
- She says founders will have to fire people who do not improve after warnings, and keeping them on does the company no favours.
- She says a founder must keep time for strategic work, such as a day a week or three days a month, and must stay reachable by junior staff.
- She says a founder cannot show favourites and must admonish a liked employee the same way as any other.

### Partners and competition

- She says one partner hospital saw the revenue, decided to run oncology itself and gave her 15 days to leave, 18 months into a contract with a two-year lock-in.
- She says her lawyer advised keeping a person on the premises every day so the company could show it had not vacated. Patients were moved to other centres within two days and the dispute went to arbitration.
- She says partners also began asking for more than 50% once they saw the money.
- She says non-compete clauses did not help in practice, and legal support became essential because the brand carried no weight yet.
- She says the company began collaborative and had to accept that its market is competitive and that it cannot look soft.

### Scaling

- She says the company doubled down, moving from two areas to four in Hyderabad with four Level 1 and four Level 2 centres, and is building its own hospital with a linear accelerator.
- She says the hospital was not planned from the start. Without radiation the company could not offer complete treatment or capture the full ticket, and patients did not see a company without a big building as a big player.
- She describes the shift as asset light to asset medium.
- She says the investors' ask changed from building the first distributed model to capturing 10% of oncology revenue in Hyderabad.
- She says business to consumer is brutal month to month. Her sector has three weak months a year, so a team that misses a monthly target must still meet the quarterly one.
- She says a founder's view of what they can do changes over five years, and you become what the job requires within ethical and legal bounds.

## Examples and cases used

- Her own company: the whole session is its MVP, its numbers and what followed.
- Maaza: a seasonal product, mango, made available all year, as a model for businesses with seasonal demand.
- NephroPlus: she says a founder friend there had a partner lock a ward and demand payment, to show that partner disputes are common in healthcare.
- Tier 2 hotels: bigger rooms at half the city rate, as the parallel for cheaper surgery outside the metro.
- Accenture: she stayed over twelve years because the firm invested in her early, which is her argument for investing in staff.
- Large Hyderabad hospitals: she says they began as small nursing homes in smaller towns, so a company's ambition grows over decades.

## Numbers given

- 4 crore rupees: founders' own money for the MVP. About 75 lakh went to capex and 30 lakh a month to operating cost, giving about ten months of runway.
- 15,000 to 20,000 rupees for a low-end chemotherapy and about 60,000 for a high-end one, averaging 45,000, in her centres.
- 17% of India has health insurance by her figure, of which 13% is state government cover and 4 to 5% private.
- 25,000 to 35,000 rupees a month: her figure for an experienced nurse's salary.
- 50% of her revenue comes from chemotherapy and related drugs, 25% from surgery and 10% from outpatient and diagnostics.
- 155 staff at the time of the talk, 250 expected by December. 16 oncologists, up from four.
- 7.5 million dollars: the Series A she says closed the previous year.
- 40% of cost goes to doctors and 30% to other employees, by her account.
