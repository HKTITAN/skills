# Hiring and managing

This file answers when and whom to hire, how to win engineers, how much equity to give, and how to manage and lead once there is a team. Harj Taggar, Michael Seibel, Brad Flora, Paul Graham, Aaron Epstein, Ali Rowghani, Sam Altman and Anu Hariharan speak in it, along with one unsigned Y Combinator post. Read each line for its stage, because the advice before product-market fit is close to the reverse of the advice after it, and every money figure belongs to the year or the undated post it comes from.

## When should I hire, and when should I hold off?

- Taggar, in a conversation with Seibel and Flora that the library dates to 2022, says founders in a YC batch very often tell the partners they need to hire, and that one of the partners' most frequent answers is not to. (Inside the Group Partner Lounge: Don't make these hiring mistakes, https://www.ycombinator.com/library/J2-inside-the-group-partner-lounge-don-t-make-these-hiring-mistakes)
- Seibel says in the same 2022 conversation that nearly all hiring advice online is written for companies past product-market fit, that most startups are before it, and that applying the advice before it might speed up the company's death. (Inside the Group Partner Lounge: Don't make these hiring mistakes, https://www.ycombinator.com/library/J2-inside-the-group-partner-lounge-don-t-make-these-hiring-mistakes)
- In the 2022 conversation between Seibel, Taggar and Flora, the partners say they ask about growth rate and runway in office hours to read what kind of company they are advising, and that a company past product-market fit and accelerating is the one that starts hiring. (Inside the Group Partner Lounge: Don't make these hiring mistakes, https://www.ycombinator.com/library/J2-inside-the-group-partner-lounge-don-t-make-these-hiring-mistakes)
- Seibel gives Airbnb as the example in the 2022 conversation: on his telling, which he says bends the history a little, the founders went eighteen months before hiring a first employee, an engineer, and took six months after YC and a fundraise to find someone great. (Inside the Group Partner Lounge: Don't make these hiring mistakes, https://www.ycombinator.com/library/J2-inside-the-group-partner-lounge-don-t-make-these-hiring-mistakes)
- In the 2022 conversation between Seibel, Taggar and Flora, a specialist hired from YouTube is credited with saving Justin.TV and Twitch a great deal on video infrastructure, and Seibel adds that he was brought on only once bandwidth spending was the problem, since the first problem had been that nobody cared to watch. (Inside the Group Partner Lounge: Don't make these hiring mistakes, https://www.ycombinator.com/library/J2-inside-the-group-partner-lounge-don-t-make-these-hiring-mistakes)
- Taggar says in the 2022 conversation that successful CEOs who give talks always name hiring as their most important skill, so founders will have to learn it, but that for the founders the partners advise it is not the most important muscle to build right now. (Inside the Group Partner Lounge: Don't make these hiring mistakes, https://www.ycombinator.com/library/J2-inside-the-group-partner-lounge-don-t-make-these-hiring-mistakes)
- Seibel says his company had a saying, "every hire should increase the average IQ of the company", and that judging candidates this way often ends with the team deciding to do the work themselves. (Inside the Group Partner Lounge: Don't make these hiring mistakes, https://www.ycombinator.com/library/J2-inside-the-group-partner-lounge-don-t-make-these-hiring-mistakes)
- In the 2022 conversation between Seibel, Taggar and Flora, the closing advice splits by stage: past product-market fit, ignore the warnings and hire; before it, hold to them, because "Hiring is probably not your savior"; and if unsure which side you are on, settle that first and do not hire to find out. (Inside the Group Partner Lounge: Don't make these hiring mistakes, https://www.ycombinator.com/library/J2-inside-the-group-partner-lounge-don-t-make-these-hiring-mistakes)
- Graham's 2006 essay calls hiring a lot of people the classic way to burn through cash, and says it hurts twice, because costs rise and the company slows down, so money spent faster has to last longer. (The 18 Mistakes That Kill Startups, https://paulgraham.com/startupmistakes.html)
- Graham's 2006 essay advises not hiring at all if it can be avoided, and at first hiring only people who will write code or go out and get users, since those are the only two things needed then. (The 18 Mistakes That Kill Startups, https://paulgraham.com/startupmistakes.html)
- The Y Combinator post's closing checklist carries two one-line items on this: do not scale the team or product until you have built something people want, and before product-market fit stay small and nimble. (YC's essential startup advice, https://www.ycombinator.com/library/4D-yc-s-essential-startup-advice)
- Altman, in a talk the library dates to 2014, says to hire a full-time recruiter once product-market fit is reached and not sooner, because a recruiter brought in too early makes a company hire too fast, which he says usually implodes. (Later stage advice for startups, https://www.ycombinator.com/library/4q-later-stage-advice-for-startups)

## How do I find and hire the first engineer?

- Taggar discloses in both of his posts on hiring engineers that he co-founded Triplebyte, a company that places engineers, and both posts end by promoting it. (How to hire your first engineer, https://www.ycombinator.com/library/4H-how-to-hire-your-first-engineer; Convincing engineers to join your team, https://www.ycombinator.com/library/4G-convincing-engineers-to-join-your-team)
- Taggar's undated post is addressed to early-stage founders hiring a first engineer, and says methods that serve larger companies, such as hiring a recruiter, work less well at this stage. (How to hire your first engineer, https://www.ycombinator.com/library/4H-how-to-hire-your-first-engineer)
- Taggar calls personal networks the most important source by far for a first engineer, and says to put all hiring effort there and look elsewhere only once every possibility in the network is used up. (How to hire your first engineer, https://www.ycombinator.com/library/4H-how-to-hire-your-first-engineer)
- Taggar says someone you have already worked with is the best first hire because you know you will like working with them, which at that size could decide whether the company perseveres or shuts down; he cautions that under startup stress you may enjoy working with a friend less than you did at a bigger company. (How to hire your first engineer, https://www.ycombinator.com/library/4H-how-to-hire-your-first-engineer)
- Taggar says asking someone to be the first hire asks for an order of magnitude more commitment than pitching an investor, and that a personal connection tells you what would motivate them and lets friends help persuade them. (How to hire your first engineer, https://www.ycombinator.com/library/4H-how-to-hire-your-first-engineer)
- Taggar's plan for working a network: list the best engineers you know whether or not they seem available, take each to lunch or dinner, ask them to join, then whatever they answer ask which engineers they would most want to hire, get introduced, and repeat. (How to hire your first engineer, https://www.ycombinator.com/library/4H-how-to-hire-your-first-engineer)
- Taggar says a founder hiring a first engineer should expect at least a third of their time to go to this network loop alone, and that he knows founders of public companies who still run it. (How to hire your first engineer, https://www.ycombinator.com/library/4H-how-to-hire-your-first-engineer)
- Taggar recommends hiring the first three engineers only through personal networks and then adding other channels, because a network is usually not the best route to a varied team and the balance gets harder to change as the team grows. (How to hire your first engineer, https://www.ycombinator.com/library/4H-how-to-hire-your-first-engineer)
- Taggar, in an undated post, rates hiring marketplaces such as his own Triplebyte strictly below personal networks for a first hire, since most of their candidates want an existing engineering team and the fee per hire is usually around $25,000 for an experienced engineer. (How to hire your first engineer, https://www.ycombinator.com/library/4H-how-to-hire-your-first-engineer)
- Taggar still ranks good hiring marketplaces second for a first engineer, because they give quick access to vetted engineers who mostly want to move now, and the calls let a founder practise the pitch and sharpen what they are looking for. (How to hire your first engineer, https://www.ycombinator.com/library/4H-how-to-hire-your-first-engineer)
- Taggar says cold outreach to engineers needs personalised messages, email addresses in preference to platform messages, and follow-ups, with two or three emails before a reply; he would realistically expect it to take up to 6 months to produce a first hire. (How to hire your first engineer, https://www.ycombinator.com/library/4H-how-to-hire-your-first-engineer)
- Taggar says a technical recruiter is hard to make work for the first engineering hire, since a recruiter's cold messages are unlikely to beat a founder's response rate; if you try one, he recommends an hourly contract that you cancel when no candidates appear. (How to hire your first engineer, https://www.ycombinator.com/library/4H-how-to-hire-your-first-engineer)
- Taggar says every channel other than a friend requires treating hiring like fundraising, starting with the pitch, and that the investor pitch needs changing because candidates think less about market size and more about the most interesting product challenges. (How to hire your first engineer, https://www.ycombinator.com/library/4H-how-to-hire-your-first-engineer)

## How do I convince an engineer to accept?

- Taggar, in an undated post, reports that offer acceptance rates among companies using Triplebyte run from as low as 10% to as high as 90%. (Convincing engineers to join your team, https://www.ycombinator.com/library/4G-convincing-engineers-to-join-your-team)
- Taggar puts the cost of one engineering offer at about five onsite interviews, using an industry onsite-to-offer rate of around 20%, which at six engineering hours each is 30 hours of engineering time, and argues that this cost justifies working hard on acceptance. (Convincing engineers to join your team, https://www.ycombinator.com/library/4G-convincing-engineers-to-join-your-team)
- Taggar says to treat every candidate who drops out before you replied as a failure of your own, since running a job search quickly becomes overwhelming, most of all for the best candidates with the most options. (Convincing engineers to join your team, https://www.ycombinator.com/library/4G-convincing-engineers-to-join-your-team)
- Taggar recommends a first call of about 25 minutes that asks early what the candidate wants from their next role, because leaving that question to the end wastes the chance to shape the pitch around the answer. (Convincing engineers to join your team, https://www.ycombinator.com/library/4G-convincing-engineers-to-join-your-team)
- Taggar says that at a company of fewer than 20 people a founder should always take the first call with engineering candidates, and recommends the sales founder do it so the technical founder keeps uninterrupted time to build. (Convincing engineers to join your team, https://www.ycombinator.com/library/4G-convincing-engineers-to-join-your-team)
- Taggar reports that engineers on Triplebyte blamed bad onsite experiences almost entirely on interviewers: ones who were unprepared and knew nothing about them, who were unfamiliar with their own questions, or who were set on proving their intelligence. (Convincing engineers to join your team, https://www.ycombinator.com/library/4G-convincing-engineers-to-join-your-team)
- Taggar says to present the actual offer before asking how likely a candidate is to accept, in a letter covering salary, benefits and equity, with the number of shares, the total shares outstanding, the percentage that represents, the exercise price and the vesting schedule. (Convincing engineers to join your team, https://www.ycombinator.com/library/4G-convincing-engineers-to-join-your-team)
- Taggar's order for the offer: the hiring manager, who at an early-stage company is the founder, calls as soon as possible after the onsite, the same day if possible, with specific reasons for wanting the candidate, then calls the next day with the details. (Convincing engineers to join your team, https://www.ycombinator.com/library/4G-convincing-engineers-to-join-your-team)
- Taggar says an early-stage company should bring its investors into every engineering offer, choosing the investor most likely to sell that candidate and never one who is out of date on the company. (Convincing engineers to join your team, https://www.ycombinator.com/library/4G-convincing-engineers-to-join-your-team)
- Taggar says what matters most while closing an engineer is how long it has been since the last contact, so find reasons to reach out that are not a request for the decision, such as a relevant news story or an invitation to a team event. (Convincing engineers to join your team, https://www.ycombinator.com/library/4G-convincing-engineers-to-join-your-team)
- Taggar advises against exploding offers, because good candidates with several options push back and a rival recruiter can use the deadline against you; the softer version he says worked at Triplebyte is a signing bonus that expires on a set date. (Convincing engineers to join your team, https://www.ycombinator.com/library/4G-convincing-engineers-to-join-your-team)
- Taggar says a startup cannot match Facebook or Google on total compensation unless it is public too, and that the arguments he has seen win are faster learning through real responsibility, faster career progression, the uniqueness of this startup against the always-available big company, and mentorship from experienced engineers. (Convincing engineers to join your team, https://www.ycombinator.com/library/4G-convincing-engineers-to-join-your-team)

## How much equity should I give?

- Graham's 2007 essay gives one test for any trade of stock, whether for money, an employee or a deal: give up a share n of the company if what you get raises the average outcome enough that the rest is worth more than the whole was, that is, if it makes the company worth more than one divided by one minus n. (The Equity Equation, https://paulgraham.com/equity.html)
- Graham's 2007 essay runs the formula backwards for a hire: two founders who feel an additional programmer would raise the company's average outcome by 20% break even by trading 16.7% of the company for him. (The Equity Equation, https://paulgraham.com/equity.html)
- Graham's 2007 essay says 16.7% is not therefore the amount to offer, because salary and overhead are also costs and a deal on which the company only breaks even is not worth doing. (The Equity Equation, https://paulgraham.com/equity.html)
- Graham's 2007 essay converts salary and overhead into stock by multiplying the annual figure by about 1.5, reasoning that most startups grow fast or die and that a growing one pays next year's salary out of a valuation he assumes is three times this year's. (The Equity Equation, https://paulgraham.com/equity.html)
- Graham's 2007 worked example: take a third off 16.7% for a 50% margin to the company, giving 11.1%; a hire costing $60k a year in salary and overhead comes to $90k, which is 4.5% of a $2 million valuation; so the offer is 6.6%. (The Equity Equation, https://paulgraham.com/equity.html)
- Graham's 2007 essay points out how much it matters for early employees to take little salary, since salary comes straight out of the stock they could otherwise be given. (The Equity Equation, https://paulgraham.com/equity.html)
- Graham's 2007 essay says it is not reducing stock grants to a formula and that founders always have to guess in the end, but should know what they are guessing, including when the number comes from a VC firm's table of typical grant sizes. (The Equity Equation, https://paulgraham.com/equity.html)
- Graham's 2006 essay suggests paying people with equity in place of salary, not only to save money but because it selects for people committed enough to prefer that. (The 18 Mistakes That Kill Startups, https://paulgraham.com/startupmistakes.html)
- Altman, in a talk the library dates to 2014, tells founders to keep granting equity after the early employees, planning on three to five percent of the company every year for the next ten years, and says YC's data shows the most successful companies keep granting stock year after year. (Later stage advice for startups, https://www.ycombinator.com/library/4q-later-stage-advice-for-startups)
- Altman says in the 2014 talk to plan refresher grants early and stay ahead of vesting schedules, so that no employee reaches three vested years out of four and starts thinking about leaving. (Later stage advice for startups, https://www.ycombinator.com/library/4q-later-stage-advice-for-startups)
- Altman says in the 2014 talk that he personally likes big grants vesting over six years, because companies take a while to build, and he names back-weighted vesting and grants re-upped automatically every year as other structures in use. (Later stage advice for startups, https://www.ycombinator.com/library/4q-later-stage-advice-for-startups)

## Which hiring mistakes should I avoid?

- Taggar says in the 2022 conversation that founders treat headcount as a marker of progress, expecting investors and customers to be more impressed by more employees, and that it risks turning into a KPI. (Inside the Group Partner Lounge: Don't make these hiring mistakes, https://www.ycombinator.com/library/J2-inside-the-group-partner-lounge-don-t-make-these-hiring-mistakes)
- Seibel says in the 2022 conversation that hiring a smart person because they happen to be available is a lie founders tell themselves, and that the companies that do this well are very large ones with product-market fit; he names Google. (Inside the Group Partner Lounge: Don't make these hiring mistakes, https://www.ycombinator.com/library/J2-inside-the-group-partner-lounge-don-t-make-these-hiring-mistakes)
- Flora, by the transcript's labelling of the 2022 conversation, says a VP of Sales from a known company might be excellent at managing a hundred salespeople and still be worse than the founder at making the first sales call and winning the first ten customers. (Inside the Group Partner Lounge: Don't make these hiring mistakes, https://www.ycombinator.com/library/J2-inside-the-group-partner-lounge-don-t-make-these-hiring-mistakes)
- Altman, in a talk the library dates to 2014, says hiring senior people in a startup's early days is usually a mistake, because willingness to work hard and aptitude matter more than experience then. (Later stage advice for startups, https://www.ycombinator.com/library/4q-later-stage-advice-for-startups)
- Taggar says in the 2022 conversation that when a founder shows him a hiring plan in office hours he goes down the list answering that each item is the founder's job for now, because working out what the customer wants and adjusting the product to do it cannot be hired for at this stage. (Inside the Group Partner Lounge: Don't make these hiring mistakes, https://www.ycombinator.com/library/J2-inside-the-group-partner-lounge-don-t-make-these-hiring-mistakes)
- Taggar calls early executive hiring a cargo cult in the 2022 conversation: most startup stories come from companies that already succeeded, so founders look at a page like Airbnb's and try to fill the same titles on their own team page. (Inside the Group Partner Lounge: Don't make these hiring mistakes, https://www.ycombinator.com/library/J2-inside-the-group-partner-lounge-don-t-make-these-hiring-mistakes)
- Seibel says in the 2022 conversation not to hire now for a problem you expect later: a startup has one or two six-alarm fires and around fifty smoldering ones, one of the fifty turns into a fire and forty-nine do not, and it is still better to fight that one once it is large. (Inside the Group Partner Lounge: Don't make these hiring mistakes, https://www.ycombinator.com/library/J2-inside-the-group-partner-lounge-don-t-make-these-hiring-mistakes)
- Flora, by the transcript's labelling of the 2022 conversation, says a company without product-market fit has to be able to change direction fast, that more people means more coordination to turn, and that a turn taking three months can leave the company dead. (Inside the Group Partner Lounge: Don't make these hiring mistakes, https://www.ycombinator.com/library/J2-inside-the-group-partner-lounge-don-t-make-these-hiring-mistakes)
- In the 2022 conversation between Seibel, Taggar and Flora, over-hiring is tied to burn and runway: when a company is running low on money after YC the cause is almost always salaries, and never some service it uses. (Inside the Group Partner Lounge: Don't make these hiring mistakes, https://www.ycombinator.com/library/J2-inside-the-group-partner-lounge-don-t-make-these-hiring-mistakes)
- Seibel, by the transcript's labelling of the 2022 conversation, says people problems are one of the main causes of founder burnout, and that founders who expected to love managing a team found it drained their energy. (Inside the Group Partner Lounge: Don't make these hiring mistakes, https://www.ycombinator.com/library/J2-inside-the-group-partner-lounge-don-t-make-these-hiring-mistakes)
- In the 2022 conversation between Seibel, Taggar and Flora, one speaker assumes for argument a five to ten percent chance that any hire ends up toxic, admits nobody knows the actual odds, and concludes it is better not to play that game. (Inside the Group Partner Lounge: Don't make these hiring mistakes, https://www.ycombinator.com/library/J2-inside-the-group-partner-lounge-don-t-make-these-hiring-mistakes)
- Graham's 2006 essay says hiring bad programmers is not a serious problem for founders who program, but that business founders cannot tell which programmers are good and get no shot at the best, because nobody really good wants a job implementing a business person's vision; he says he does not think there is an answer for how they should pick. (The 18 Mistakes That Kill Startups, https://paulgraham.com/startupmistakes.html)

## How do I manage people for the first time?

- Epstein says many first-time founders raise money and start building a team with no management experience, that he was one of them at Creative Market, and that people leave managers, not companies. (Advice for new managers, https://www.ycombinator.com/library/2s-advice-for-new-managers)
- Epstein tells founders who are starting to manage a team to delegate, because they cannot focus on the highest-leverage work until things are off their plate; expecting to work especially late or not getting to the day's tasks is the signal. (Advice for new managers, https://www.ycombinator.com/library/2s-advice-for-new-managers)
- Rowghani says the CEO of a startup still working to build a product users love is the doer-in-chief, deep in building the product and winning customers, and "Delegation should not be a word in your vocabulary". (The second job of a startup CEO, https://www.ycombinator.com/library/3k-the-second-job-of-a-startup-ceo)
- Epstein says great people often care about personal growth far more than money, so delegating also creates chances for the team to do new things, learn and grow, which a founder should not hoard. (Advice for new managers, https://www.ycombinator.com/library/2s-advice-for-new-managers)
- Epstein says a new manager should deflect all credit and absorb all blame: speak in the first person singular about a failure and in the plural, or better with contributors' names, about a success. (Advice for new managers, https://www.ycombinator.com/library/2s-advice-for-new-managers)
- Epstein says to repeat the mission, vision and KPIs until it feels like a broken record, and that it is enough only when every person on the team could explain them to a stranger the way the founder would. (Advice for new managers, https://www.ycombinator.com/library/2s-advice-for-new-managers)
- Epstein says hiring well is the most important thing a founder can do for the business and the team, so hire slowly, fire fast, do not fill open roles with mediocre candidates, and be thrilled about each hire. (Advice for new managers, https://www.ycombinator.com/library/2s-advice-for-new-managers)
- Epstein says to include the team in important decisions but state the process first, whether by committee, by an owner on the team, or by the founder after gathering feedback, so that a call against the consensus is still understood and supported. (Advice for new managers, https://www.ycombinator.com/library/2s-advice-for-new-managers)
- Epstein says to hold a 1-on-1 with each employee every week, let the employee drive the agenda, and go out of the way not to cancel or reschedule, because steady attention signals care for their happiness. (Advice for new managers, https://www.ycombinator.com/library/2s-advice-for-new-managers)
- Epstein warns that a strong individual contributor promoted to manager tends to keep both jobs, 75% effort on making and 75% on managing, a 150% load with under 100% output in each, so the founder should take the maker duties off their plate. (Advice for new managers, https://www.ycombinator.com/library/2s-advice-for-new-managers)
- Altman, in a talk the library dates to 2014, says most founders delegate badly by having an employee research a question and then deciding themselves, which does not scale and makes people feel bad; the version that works hands over the decision along with the founder's thinking and trust. (Later stage advice for startups, https://www.ycombinator.com/library/4q-later-stage-advice-for-startups)
- Altman describes hero mode in the 2014 talk: a manager, in his example the head of a customer service team, who keeps doing more of the work personally in place of hiring; his fix is to accept falling behind for two or three weeks while hiring, and he says most people stay in it until they burn out. (Later stage advice for startups, https://www.ycombinator.com/library/4q-later-stage-advice-for-startups)

## What is the CEO's job as the company grows?

- Rowghani describes three phases for the CEO of a successful startup: first build a product users love, second build a company that makes the most of the opportunity the product opened, third use the core business's profits to invest in new products. (The second job of a startup CEO, https://www.ycombinator.com/library/3k-the-second-job-of-a-startup-ceo)
- Altman, in a talk the library dates to 2014, says that at about 25 employees the founder's main job stops being the product and becomes the company, for good, and calls it probably the biggest shift in being a founder. (Later stage advice for startups, https://www.ycombinator.com/library/4q-later-stage-advice-for-startups)
- Rowghani says that once a startup reaches 20 to 30 people the CEO has to spend more time leading, and because time is limited, leading more means doing less; a CEO who does not delegate ends up slowing everyone else down. (The second job of a startup CEO, https://www.ycombinator.com/library/3k-the-second-job-of-a-startup-ceo)
- Rowghani says a CEO in the company-building phase can in time hand off day-to-day responsibility for everything done earlier, product included, by hiring people better than themselves into leadership; he cites Weebly's David Rusenko, who often first learns of a product feature from the company blog. (The second job of a startup CEO, https://www.ycombinator.com/library/3k-the-second-job-of-a-startup-ceo)
- Rowghani names three operational tasks only the CEO can do in the company-building phase: hiring the leadership team and making sure it works well together, creating purpose and alignment, and nurturing culture. (The second job of a startup CEO, https://www.ycombinator.com/library/3k-the-second-job-of-a-startup-ceo)
- Rowghani says the final decision on each senior hire has to be the CEO's own, and warns against hiring by compromise, meaning looking for the person everyone around the CEO likes. (The second job of a startup CEO, https://www.ycombinator.com/library/3k-the-second-job-of-a-startup-ceo)
- Rowghani says to expect at least 25% of leadership hires not to work out, that most CEOs take too long to fire their first executive, and that acting quickly and leaving a gap beats leaving an ineffective executive in place. (The second job of a startup CEO, https://www.ycombinator.com/library/3k-the-second-job-of-a-startup-ceo)
- Rowghani says alignment needs little work when fewer than 10 people sit together, and that in the company-building phase the CEO stops rowing and instead defines the mission, the strategy and the metrics, which he compares to the voyage's purpose, the boat's direction and the rowers' pace. (The second job of a startup CEO, https://www.ycombinator.com/library/3k-the-second-job-of-a-startup-ceo)
- Rowghani says the mission, strategy and metrics have to be repeated far more than feels efficient, and that the real test is whether employees can make good decisions in the CEO's absence from that context, not whether they can repeat it. (The second job of a startup CEO, https://www.ycombinator.com/library/3k-the-second-job-of-a-startup-ceo)
- Altman's test in the 2014 talk for a company starting to struggle with scale: founders always tell him ten random employees would name the same top three goals, and when he asks, no two employees give the same three in order. (Later stage advice for startups, https://www.ycombinator.com/library/4q-later-stage-advice-for-startups)
- Rowghani says culture is not the CEO's job alone, that values written by a single author usually fail because they do not match how employees experience the company, and that CEOs should get co-founders and early employees to codify values that fit how the company has acted in the past. (The second job of a startup CEO, https://www.ycombinator.com/library/3k-the-second-job-of-a-startup-ceo)
- Altman, asked in the 2014 talk when founders should hire a professional CEO, answers never, pointing to the most successful tech companies being run by their founders for a very long time; he adds that a person who does not want to be the long-term CEO probably should not start a company, while saying he is not totally sure and that there are exceptions. (Later stage advice for startups, https://www.ycombinator.com/library/4q-later-stage-advice-for-startups)

## How do I know whether I am a good leader?

- Rowghani says judging leaders by the success of their businesses is tempting but confounded by timing and luck, and gives no clue about how a leader can improve. (How to measure leadership, https://www.ycombinator.com/library/3j-how-to-measure-leadership)
- Rowghani bases his measure on working closely with Ed Catmull, Steve Jobs, John Lasseter and Bob Iger, who he says could not have differed more in style and temperament yet all built trust by doing the same three things well. (How to measure leadership, https://www.ycombinator.com/library/3j-how-to-measure-leadership)
- Rowghani's three areas are clarity of thought and communication, judgment about people, and personal integrity and commitment; he says a leader who excels in the three and so earns the trust of the people around them is a good one. (How to measure leadership, https://www.ycombinator.com/library/3j-how-to-measure-leadership)
- Rowghani says clear thinking comes before clear language, so the way to communicate better is to spend more time deciding what truly matters for the business and then practise saying it in simple terms. (How to measure leadership, https://www.ycombinator.com/library/3j-how-to-measure-leadership)
- Rowghani says nothing damages an organization or a leader's standing more than picking the wrong leaders or failing to correct the mistake, and that the first hiring or promotion decision matters most, because leaders who fire too many of their own picks lose credibility and trust too. (How to measure leadership, https://www.ycombinator.com/library/3j-how-to-measure-leadership)
- Rowghani says judgment about people improves with more data: meet as many of the best people in a field as possible, spend as much time as you can with executives you may hire, and run extensive reference checks that ask for examples of good judgment and integrity. (How to measure leadership, https://www.ycombinator.com/library/3j-how-to-measure-leadership)
- Rowghani's integrity test is to ask whether anything would embarrass you if your team could see all your private communications and behaviour toward employees; he calls it a high bar. (How to measure leadership, https://www.ycombinator.com/library/3j-how-to-measure-leadership)
- Rowghani says great leaders treat hard decisions, such as firing people or saying no, as chances to build trust, and choose the course of action and style of communication that will raise employees' trust in them. (How to measure leadership, https://www.ycombinator.com/library/3j-how-to-measure-leadership)
- Rowghani suggests startups begin gathering employee sentiment systematically at about 50 people, and offers survey questions for the CEO and for employees, one of which asks the CEO to write the mission, strategy and key metrics in under 2 minutes. (How to measure leadership, https://www.ycombinator.com/library/3j-how-to-measure-leadership)

## What changes once the company is past product-market fit?

- Altman, in a talk the library dates to 2014, says that, with a few exceptions he flags, everything on his later-stage list can be ignored until after product-market fit, and that for most companies it becomes important between months 12 and 24, at around 25 people. (Later stage advice for startups, https://www.ycombinator.com/library/4q-later-stage-advice-for-startups)
- Altman says a flat company with everyone reporting to the founder is the best structure up to about 20 employees but that lack of structure fails all at once; the written version of the 2014 talk says what works at 20 can be disastrous at 40, and the spoken transcript says it is disastrous at 30. (Later stage advice for startups, https://www.ycombinator.com/library/4q-later-stage-advice-for-startups)
- Altman says in the 2014 talk that the structure a company of that size needs is simple: every employee has exactly one manager and knows who it is, every manager knows their direct reports, and founders should innovate on product and business model, not on management structure. (Later stage advice for startups, https://www.ycombinator.com/library/4q-later-stage-advice-for-startups)
- Altman says in the 2014 talk that once the company is scaling it is valuable to add executives who have built companies before, and that almost all founders, after their first great executive takes over big pieces of the business, say they wish they had done it earlier. (Later stage advice for startups, https://www.ycombinator.com/library/4q-later-stage-advice-for-startups)
- Altman says in the 2014 talk to write down how the company does things and why, and have every employee read it, because what is written becomes law as the company grows to a hundred and then a thousand people; otherwise new hires pick up a random oral tradition. (Later stage advice for startups, https://www.ycombinator.com/library/4q-later-stage-advice-for-startups)
- Altman says in the 2014 talk that performance feedback, which happens organically early on, gets lost as a company reaches 25 to 45 people and has to be set up as something simple and frequent, along with compensation bands, since pay negotiated one by one with the founder is all over the place and everyone eventually finds out everyone else's pay. (Later stage advice for startups, https://www.ycombinator.com/library/4q-later-stage-advice-for-startups)
- Altman says in the 2014 talk to think ahead about the path for the first 10 or 15 employees, who may want executive roles the company cannot give them, and to talk with them directly about where they want their careers inside the company to go. (Later stage advice for startups, https://www.ycombinator.com/library/4q-later-stage-advice-for-startups)
- Altman says in the 2014 talk that he thinks productivity falls with the square of the number of employees unless a company makes an effort, because every pair of people adds communication overhead, and that the systems should be thought about when the company is 25 to 50 people. (Later stage advice for startups, https://www.ycombinator.com/library/4q-later-stage-advice-for-startups)
- Altman says in the 2014 talk that the single word that matters most for staying productive while growing is alignment, and prescribes a weekly meeting of the founder's direct reports, an all-hands at least once a month, and a plan every quarter. (Later stage advice for startups, https://www.ycombinator.com/library/4q-later-stage-advice-for-startups)
- Altman says in the 2014 talk that team diversity is one item to think about before the 12 to 24 month mark, and that most founders he has spoken to who hired only men for the first 15 or 20 people on their engineering team regret it. (Later stage advice for startups, https://www.ycombinator.com/library/4q-later-stage-advice-for-startups)
- Hariharan says not to hire a full growth team or put major ad money into growth until the company has shown it can retain customers, and calls investing in growth before that a great way to waste money and endanger the company. (How to set up, hire, and scale a growth strategy and team, https://www.ycombinator.com/library/59-how-to-set-up-hire-and-scale-a-growth-strategy-and-team)
- Hariharan gives the most common first-year growth team as one growth product manager, two to three growth engineers and one to two growth data scientists, and says most companies made the first growth hire when they had about 15 engineers working on product. (How to set up, hire, and scale a growth strategy and team, https://www.ycombinator.com/library/59-how-to-set-up-hire-and-scale-a-growth-strategy-and-team)
- Hariharan says the most common mistake CEOs make with growth teams is waiting too long to hire a growth-focused product manager, who in a company with strong retention is likely to be the third or fourth product manager on the team. (How to set up, hire, and scale a growth strategy and team, https://www.ycombinator.com/library/59-how-to-set-up-hire-and-scale-a-growth-strategy-and-team)
