# Negotiating through agents, framing and first offers

The first half of the fifth session of the six-part negotiation course, taught by the course professor, who is not named in this recording. She debriefs the Bullard Houses case, in which each student negotiated as an agent for a buyer or a seller, then teaches how to work with agents and plays a three-part training video of a negotiator and his boss. Student stories about their own deals are left out.

## What was said

### Lessons from the Bullard Houses case

- The instructor says the case had a positive bargaining zone on price but no zone of agreement on interests: the seller did not want the houses torn down for commercial use and the buyer wanted to build a hotel.
- She says the seller's agent was told to maximise the price only after those interests were satisfied.
- She says a deal in this case could happen only if one representative defied their instructions or one party lied.
- She says numbers are easy to process, so negotiators focus on them and ignore the other things that matter.
- She says that in many business negotiations the non-money issues matter more, and that if a price zone exists the two sides will reach agreement on money anyway.
- Her takeaway is that information that is easy to process is not necessarily the most important information.
- She says the idea is not to make a deal. If your interests are not being satisfied, you walk away.
- She accepts that walking away feels like a loss and feels unkind to the other person, and says you still have to put your own or your client's interests first.

### Prepare for the questions you will be asked

- She says preparation is more than numbers. The other side will ask what you plan to do, what other offers you have and what your competitors offered.
- She says you should decide in advance how you will answer such questions, including whether you will lie if you have no alternative.
- She says an unprepared liar fumbles and contradicts himself under follow-up questions.
- She says that when something feels wrong you should ask more questions and, where it fits, propose a contingency.
- Her example from past classes is a contingent contract where the price depends on what the buyer later builds on the property.
- She says the first thing to settle is the context: who you are in the negotiation and, if you are an agent, what you are authorised to decide.

### AI and negotiation

- She says her team asked ChatGPT to build a scoring sheet for a new case and it did not produce a correct formula.
- She says negotiators need not fear AI taking their job in the near future, because negotiation is about people and not only numbers.

### Why and how to use agents

- She says the reasons to hire an agent are that agents are professionals with more expertise, hold more market data and are generally not emotional.
- She says the drawbacks are misrepresentation and misaligned incentives, because agents have their own incentives.
- Her example is a rental agent who earns one month's rent only when a deal closes, so the agent gains from closing and from a higher rent.
- She says the fix is to tie the agent's pay to your interests: a set type of flat, within a set budget, within a set time.
- For a selling agent she suggests a tiered incentive: a set reward for reaching the target price and extra for going beyond it.
- She says everything must be in writing, but the principal must not disempower the agent, who is there to find creative solutions.
- She says the principal should spell out what the agent may decide alone and what must be checked first.
- She says abstract interests, such as not wanting a property demolished, should be quantified for the agent through a scoring system.

### The principal and agent video

- In the video she plays, a boss tells his negotiator to keep a five million dollar contract and also squeeze out 5 to 10 percent more margin, and says the firm has no alternative.
- She points out that the negotiator asks three things before he goes: the top priorities, his decision authority and the alternatives if the deal fails.
- She says the negotiator stays assertive and never aggressive, even when he tells his boss the two goals pull against each other.
- She says a principal must communicate the most important priorities very carefully.
- She says you should always have an alternative, and that the boss's claim of having none is a weak place to negotiate from.
- In the video the other side brings in a procurement consultant who is paid on how much he cuts, and the negotiator solves it by reducing scope, removing free extras and fixing a rate for additional work.
- Her lessons from the video are to stay professional in internal negotiations, to avoid blame, and to tell the boss or client what is and is not possible along with a creative option.

### Exploding offers

- She calls a take-it-today offer an exploding offer and ties it to the scarcity principle: a limited-time offer is given more value at once.
- She says sellers use deadlines because a buyer who has time to reflect may not come back.
- She says that if you ask for more time, you should know what you will do with it, such as getting more data or a better offer.

### First offers and anchors

- She says everyone gets anchored by numbers, even random ones, so she generally tries to make the first offer.
- She says an aggressive first offer is based on an estimate of the other side's budget and market prices, and should sit a little beyond that estimate and not far beyond it.
- She says an aggressive offer is acceptable but a ridiculous one usually means the deal will not happen.
- Her example of forcing the other side to anchor is an artists' agent who says his own quoted price is non-negotiable, but that he will negotiate if the buyer states a budget.
- She thinks in terms of two numbers: a walk-away price and a target, with the target set close to the other side's reservation price.

### Reasons, stories and framing

- She cites an experiment at a copying machine where a person who gave a reason for jumping the queue was let through more often than one who gave none.
- She cites research that stories stay in memory long after bare numbers are forgotten.
- Her advice when asked for a number, such as last salary, is to tell the story first and then quote the number.
- She says people remember what is vivid, and that a coherent story is believed even when it is false.
- She says people remember negatively framed information more and are loss averse, and names prospect theory and Daniel Kahneman.
- She says that if you want someone to accept a risky option about the future, you present it in a loss frame.
- She says much of influence in negotiation depends on how information is presented.

### On using cases

- She says she teaches frameworks from research rather than famous deals, because with real deals nobody knows what actually worked.
- She recommends the book Negotiation Genius for worked examples.

## Examples and cases used

- Bullard Houses: a property sale negotiated through agents, used to show a price zone without any overlap of interests.
- A rental agent paid one month's rent: used to show how a badly designed incentive works against the client.
- A training video of a negotiator and his boss over a five million dollar service contract: used to show how to brief an agent and how to report back.
- The copying machine queue experiment: used to show that giving a reason raises compliance.
- Prospect theory, Daniel Kahneman: used to explain loss aversion and framing.

## Numbers given

- Five million dollars: the contract value in the training video, with the boss asking for 5 to 10 percent more margin.
- One month's rent: the usual agent fee in her rental example.
