# SAFE discounts and Series A term sheets, Bhagwan Chowdhry

A finance class in two halves. Bhagwan Chowdhry opened at the board with risk premiums, discounting and why a SAFE carries a discount. A second finance instructor, addressed as Prachi, then took the class through a teaching case about a company called ELCA choosing between two Series A term sheets, using a spreadsheet. The recording starts partway into the first half.

## What was said

### Two kinds of risk and the discount rate

- Bhagwan Chowdhry says the risk premium is the reduction in price an investor asks for when a payoff is uncertain.
- He says the market risk premium depends on when the money comes: a business that pays more in good times and less in bad times is disliked, and one that pays more when everything else is going badly is liked.
- He calls this market risk, systematic risk or covariance risk.
- He says cyclical businesses such as automobiles call for a premium, and counter cyclical businesses call for less.
- He builds his discount rate from three parts. In his example they are a 2% real risk free rate, 4% inflation and a 5% risk premium, giving 11%.
- He says a startup carries a second, separate risk, which is the probability that the business fails altogether. The 5% premium does not cover it.
- He says finance uses the single word risk for both of these, and people mix them up all the time.
- He says "expected" in finance means the statistical average and not what the founder hopes for. His example is a business that pays 200 half the time and nothing half the time, which has an expected value of 100.

### How a SAFE works and where its discount comes from

- He says the investor's equity share is the money put in divided by the value of the firm. His example is 25 lakh rupees into a firm worth 10 crore, which is 2.5%.
- He says the trouble at the earliest stage is that nobody knows the value, and a SAFE solves this by taking the money today and fixing the value later, when a future investor prices the company.
- He says money given today and a value set a year later cannot be compared directly, so one of them has to be moved in time. Either the future value is discounted, or today's money is grown at the same rate, and the answer is the same.
- In his example the future value is divided by 1.11, which he rounds to multiplying by 0.9.
- He then multiplies by a second factor for survival. His example is a 90% chance the startup survives, so another 0.9.
- He says the two factors together come to about 0.8, and that this is the 20% discount written into a SAFE.
- He says a SAFE discount of 20 to 25% is pretty standard and is not fixed. A riskier business gets a bigger discount.
- He says a local lender is different because it asks for collateral, so it does not carry the risk of failure.

### What drives a valuation

- He says the cash flow forecast is the number one thing in a valuation, and the other inputs are fairly standard.
- He says no professor can teach a founder to forecast cash flow. It comes from understanding the business, its sales strategy and its marketing strategy.
- He gives three principles. Cash is king, counted when it comes in and not before. Opportunity cost matters. Two things with identical cash flows must have the same price.
- He says these principles together give the discounted cash flow model, also called NPV, which works on expected cash flows.
- Asked about a startup with no cash flow yet, he says the future cash flow must be forecast, and "if you can't see light at the end of the tunnel, there's no light".

### Pre-money, post-money and the investor's stake

- The co-instructor says pre-money valuation is the value belonging to the existing owners before the new money comes in, and post-money valuation is pre-money plus the investment.
- She says the investor's stake is always the investment divided by the post-money valuation.
- Her analogy is a wallet: put 1,000 rupees in it and its value goes up by exactly 1,000, because the money has not been used yet.
- She says an offer can be stated three ways that mean the same thing: a pre-money valuation and an amount, a post-money valuation and an amount, or an amount for a percentage.
- She says news articles about funding rounds usually do not distinguish pre-money from post-money, but term sheets always do, in every round.
- She says a fundraise is an outside investor putting money in while the business carries on, and a merger or acquisition is a corporate buyer taking over. Her example of an acquisition is Zomato buying Blinkit.
- She says the next round's pre-money valuation is not today's post-money valuation. It is set afresh from the stage and prospects of the company at that time.
- She says earlier investors are also diluted in later rounds, and may put in anti-dilution provisions to reduce that.
- She says if a buyback of an investor's stake is not written into the term sheet, it becomes a negotiation at the time the founders want to buy.

### The ESOP pool

- She says VCs write the option pool into the term sheet so that ESOPs issued later do not dilute the VC's stake.
- She says the pool therefore comes out of the founders' share and not the investor's.
- She says the founders still decide who gets options, how many and when.
- She says pool shares are authorised but not yet issued, and are usually shown in the cap table as issued and not yet issued.
- She says co-founders are diluted in the same proportion unless new shares are issued to one of them.
- To find the new share count she keeps the founders' existing shares fixed and divides by their new percentage.

### Founder vesting

- She says founder vesting in the case means the company has the right to buy back the founders' shares at cost, which is so small it can be treated as zero.
- She says the right shrinks over time: after one year 25% of the founders' shares are free of it, and after the full period the founders own everything outright.
- She says VCs want this so that founders stay and so that founder and investor interests are aligned, and that in the vast majority of cases it is never triggered.
- She says the buyback right belongs to the company and is decided by the board, not by an individual VC.
- She says founders should not refuse such terms outright but must understand what they imply, because money often comes with strings.
- She says the terms of a later funding round override the vesting terms of this one.

### Liquidation preference

- She says investors get preferred shares, founders and the option pool hold common shares, and preferred shares are paid before common.
- She says a plain preferred share gets back the investment plus a yearly preference dividend, 8% in her example, and no upside.
- She says non-participating preferred means the investor takes the higher of its original investment or its percentage stake of the exit value.
- She says that with non-participating preferred, the investor's share of proceeds is above its stake whenever the exit is below the post-money valuation, and equals its stake above it.
- She says participating preferred means the investor first takes back its investment and then also takes its percentage of whatever remains. She says this is also called double dipping.
- She says that with participating preferred the investor always ends up with more than its percentage stake, even at high exit values.
- She says about 60% of VC investments either barely recover the original investment or return less.

### Choosing between term sheets

- She says a higher valuation means less dilution, but that does not automatically mean a higher payout for founders once the other terms are counted.
- She says there is no right or wrong choice between a hands-off investor and a hands-on investor with sector expertise. Founders have to judge whether the guidance is worth the control.
- She says founders should work out what they are left with in the best case and in the worst case, and decide what they can live with. It is not a purely mathematical decision.
- She says when a lot of money is chasing startups, founders get both higher valuations and friendlier terms.
- She says a founder's only real negotiating power is having several VCs making offers. Otherwise it comes down to take it or leave it.
- She says a term sheet has many dimensions, and a founder can trade on the one that matters most to them, such as control or salary, instead of on valuation alone.

## Examples and cases used

- ELCA Series A teaching case: an online lessons company with two founders holding 60% and 40%, choosing between two VC term sheets.
- First term sheet in the case: 5 million on a 20 million pre-money valuation, so 25 million post-money and a 20% stake, with a 15% option pool, four year founder vesting and non-participating preferred shares.
- Second term sheet in the case: 4 million on a 26 million pre-money valuation, so 30 million post-money and a 13.3% stake, with five year founder vesting and participating preferred shares, from a hands-on investor with sector expertise.
- Zomato acquiring Blinkit: her example of an acquisition as opposed to a fundraise.

## Numbers given

- 2% real risk free rate, 4% inflation and 5% risk premium, giving an 11% discount rate, in Bhagwan Chowdhry's board example.
- 90% survival probability in his example, which with the 11% discount gives a factor of about 0.8.
- 20 to 25%: the SAFE discount he calls pretty standard.
- After the first term sheet the cap table is founders 65%, option pool 15%, investor 20%. The two founders go from 60% and 40% to 39% and 26%.
- With one million founder shares, the new total is about 1.54 million shares and the pool is about 230,000 shares, in her spreadsheet.
- Under the first term sheet, an exit at 5 million gives the investor all 5 million and common shareholders nothing. An exit at 15 million gives the investor 5 million, which is 33%. An exit at 100 million gives the investor 20 million.
- Under the second term sheet, an exit at 14 million gives the investor 4 million plus 13.3% of the remaining 10 million.
- About 60% of VC investments barely recover the investment or less, in the co-instructor's telling.
