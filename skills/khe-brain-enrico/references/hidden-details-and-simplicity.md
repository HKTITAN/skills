# Hidden details and simplicity

This is the centre of his channel: the claim that everyday tech only feels simple because designers and engineers buried a large amount of work under it. It comes mostly from his series on the design, engineering and psychology behind everyday tech, which runs from late 2023 to 2026, plus the episodes he filmed with the Notion and Figma teams. Product facts are his research as of each video's date, several names in the captions are unclear and are left generic here, and his statements about AI carry their year.

## Why does simple-looking tech take so much work?

- He holds that the tech products and apps people use every day look simple, sleek and modern, but to build that apparent simplicity they need to be incredibly complex, a point he made after tracing what happens when you press play on YouTube. (YouTube Does This Anytime You Watch And You Don't Realize It at 6:45, https://www.youtube.com/watch?v=49_a3jkdBdI&t=405s)
- He says making something simple is extremely hard, and contrasts clunky, complex Microsoft Word 2003 with minimal, simple Notion: "Making something simple is extremely hard." (The Ridiculous Engineering Of Notion at 17:15, https://www.youtube.com/watch?v=b00sgRR_Vc0&t=1035s)
- He argues that products which feel simple do not have less complexity than scary-looking ones. They probably have more, it is only hidden, and the hidden part is what makes everything work. (The Ridiculous Engineering Of Figma at 9:00, https://www.youtube.com/watch?v=5t8dh3DSdBk&t=540s)
- He says the catch of making creative software simple enough for a kid is that it becomes far more complex behind the scenes, so the products that just work are the ones hiding the most. (The Ridiculous Engineering Of Figma at 0:45, https://www.youtube.com/watch?v=5t8dh3DSdBk&t=45s)
- He frames the root problem as a mismatch: humans run on intuition with brains that evolved to survive in a forest, while computers are cold and precise, so interaction design exists to pull the machine closer to people. (The Lies That Make Your Tech Actually Work at 3:00, https://www.youtube.com/watch?v=rAVV4kAYlI8&t=180s)
- He names three sources of interface decisions: copying nature, exploiting psychology, and technical limits that stuck around long after the limit was gone. (The Lies That Make Your Tech Actually Work at 9:45, https://www.youtube.com/watch?v=rAVV4kAYlI8&t=585s)
- He says the hardest thing about building tech is not the clever hack or the complex build but humans and their odd psychology, which he calls the most unpredictable part of tech. (How Your Tech Tricks You at 7:30, https://www.youtube.com/watch?v=7nnyv4yJGJc&t=450s)
- He says a product feels good because of small details. Each one alone does not matter, and he agrees with skeptics on that, but when you add up a hundred or a thousand of them, with hours of design and engineering in each, you get the feeling of something that feels good, which he calls the secret power of good design. (The Ridiculous Engineering Of Notion at 16:30, https://www.youtube.com/watch?v=b00sgRR_Vc0&t=990s)
- He says these details go unnoticed by 99% of people yet are what make tech usable, and that designing tech is not only about making things look nice. Some details exist purely for fun, like the Street View figure that clings to the cursor and reacts to gravity. (The 7 Levels of Tech Design at 3:00, https://www.youtube.com/watch?v=FQVRfadJkJk&t=180s)
- He says someone had to **invent the obvious**. You understand stories in 0.1 seconds, and you know you can drag a square's corner to resize it, only because a person once invented those patterns. (The Ridiculous Engineering Of Figma at 19:30, https://www.youtube.com/watch?v=5t8dh3DSdBk&t=1170s)
- He says designing something simple and intuitive is not simple at all, and in January 2025 he pointed to the Dynamic Island as Apple's latest attempt to create a new pattern. (Social Media Is Broken But We Can Fix It at 8:15, https://www.youtube.com/watch?v=HqYIUV7Eqjk&t=495s)
- He says a detail as small as how one button works can be worth billions at Spotify's scale, because a subscriber deciding whether to stay will only know that the shuffled playlist on the drive home felt better. (The Spotify Shuffle Button is a Lie at 4:30, https://www.youtube.com/watch?v=NhAIA-o7f4M&t=270s)
- He says the simplest fix is often the hardest to see: "Sometimes the simplest solution is the hardest one to find." His case is Nintendo showing the Joy-Con lights through the Switch grip with tiny mirrors at 45 degrees instead of extra LEDs or electronics. (How Your Tech Tricks You at 0:45, https://www.youtube.com/watch?v=7nnyv4yJGJc&t=45s)
- He says in map design simple and easy are two very different things, because mountains of data need strict rules before they can look simple. (The Most Dangerous App That Everyone Loves at 6:00, https://www.youtube.com/watch?v=P2qOiKA56tI&t=360s)

## What are his levels and layers of product design?

- He describes seven levels of designing and building tech products, running from the smallest details up to the entire product strategies of some of the biggest tech companies. (The 7 Levels of Tech Design at 0:45, https://www.youtube.com/watch?v=FQVRfadJkJk&t=45s)
- For level one he uses Google's footer copyright line. He says early 2000s tests showed students staring at the minimal page for 45 seconds waiting for it to finish loading, so the line was added to mark the end of the page, with no legal need for it. (The 7 Levels of Tech Design at 0:00, https://www.youtube.com/watch?v=FQVRfadJkJk&t=0s)
- At level three he says great products start from the perfect interaction and then find the technology for it, not the other way round. The technology that matters is the kind that disappears. (The 7 Levels of Tech Design at 7:30, https://www.youtube.com/watch?v=FQVRfadJkJk&t=450s)
- His level three story is Spotify. He says plain server streaming took 30 seconds to a minute to start a song, so Spotify bought uTorrent to get its developer, who rebuilt the system as peer to peer. He cites a 2010 study that found only 8.8% of the music came from Spotify's servers. (The 7 Levels of Tech Design at 6:45, https://www.youtube.com/watch?v=FQVRfadJkJk&t=405s)
- Level five is what he calls **structural design**: deciding the logic of the whole app. In his product Flask he replaced a fixed done toggle with tags, then made everything a tag, which let him build filtering in one place and add a new comments feature as one more tag. (The 7 Levels of Tech Design at 12:00, https://www.youtube.com/watch?v=FQVRfadJkJk&t=720s)
- He says Notion and Slack made the same kind of structural decision: in Notion everything is a page and in Slack everything is a channel. A good structural decision makes everything after it simpler. (The 7 Levels of Tech Design at 13:30, https://www.youtube.com/watch?v=FQVRfadJkJk&t=810s)
- Level six is the reframe: changing what a technology is for without changing the technology. He says Loom reframed video as a replacement for meetings and sold to Atlassian for $975 million, and Superhuman reframed email as the fastest email and charges $30 a month. (The 7 Levels of Tech Design at 14:15, https://www.youtube.com/watch?v=FQVRfadJkJk&t=855s)
- He says vision alone fails. General Magic had the touchscreen smartphone vision years before the iPhone and flopped, and what separated Steve Jobs's team from everyone else was all the levels underneath. (The 7 Levels of Tech Design at 15:45, https://www.youtube.com/watch?v=FQVRfadJkJk&t=945s)
- He reads the iPhone back down the levels: a giant screen with no buttons, a computer in your pocket, everything is an app, Apple's own apps and Google search as defaults, multi-touch glass and a day-long battery, pinch to zoom and rubber band scrolling, then squircles and keyboard hit boxes that resize. (The 7 Levels of Tech Design at 15:45, https://www.youtube.com/watch?v=FQVRfadJkJk&t=945s)
- In another video he uses three levels for what sits behind everyday tech: the surface you see, the thousands of small details most people never notice, and interactions that look simple but hide complex systems. (Why Simple Tech is Almost Impossible to Build at 0:45, https://www.youtube.com/watch?v=qSQZ8B-2nU0&t=45s)
- In his videos on YouTube and on short-form feeds he says three layers make the product work and only the first is what you can actually see, and he starts both with the interface. (The Ridiculous Engineering Of YouTube Videos at 0:45, https://www.youtube.com/watch?v=giP1iIbSVh0&t=45s; The Ridiculous Engineering Of Short Form Content at 0:45, https://www.youtube.com/watch?v=69Gw7aoWMMI&t=45s)
- For physical products he says the feeling that something is solid and good to hold is not random, and that four building blocks make physical tech products work. The first is the curvature of the corners. (The Lies That Make Hardware Actually Work at 1:30, https://www.youtube.com/watch?v=wLIgrGAIRsE&t=90s)

## Which small details does he point to?

- He says the space bar on iOS and Android has the key pop-up switched off on purpose. It is the most used key, the pop-up would annoy, the key is too big to miss, and someone had to program that exception by hand. (Why Simple Tech is Almost Impossible to Build at 0:00, https://www.youtube.com/watch?v=qSQZ8B-2nU0&t=0s)
- He names the **two-state problem**: a toggle icon can show the current state or the action a click will take, and mixing them confuses people. A video pause button shows the action while Google Meet's mute button shows the state. (Why Simple Tech is Almost Impossible to Build at 1:30, https://www.youtube.com/watch?v=qSQZ8B-2nU0&t=90s)
- His preferred fix for the two-state problem is to separate status from action, as the Tesla app does with a lock icon for status and a separate button to change it. The other route is clear colours and shapes for state, as on the iPhone call screen. (Why Simple Tech is Almost Impossible to Build at 1:30, https://www.youtube.com/watch?v=qSQZ8B-2nU0&t=90s)
- He shows that what surrounds an element tells you how it works in 0.1 seconds. The same three words read as tabs, tags or toggles when only the shapes around them change. (Why Simple Tech is Almost Impossible to Build at 2:15, https://www.youtube.com/watch?v=qSQZ8B-2nU0&t=135s)
- He says design patterns are what make tech usable without an instruction manual, and that without them you could not use your tech at all. You know three dots mean more options because you have seen them hundreds of times. (Why Simple Tech is Almost Impossible to Build at 3:00, https://www.youtube.com/watch?v=qSQZ8B-2nU0&t=180s; Social Media Is Broken But We Can Fix It at 0:45, https://www.youtube.com/watch?v=HqYIUV7Eqjk&t=45s)
- He says the text cursor blinks only when you stop typing. It blinks so you can find it at rest and stays solid while you type so it does not distract, and he used computers for 25 years before noticing. (The Lies That Make Your Tech Actually Work at 3:00, https://www.youtube.com/watch?v=rAVV4kAYlI8&t=180s; Why Simple Tech is Almost Impossible to Build at 3:45, https://www.youtube.com/watch?v=qSQZ8B-2nU0&t=225s)
- He says the pointer changing shape is what makes desktop interfaces usable at all. A blue underlined link feels wrong without the hand, which he says Bill Atkinson invented in 1987 for HyperCard. (The 7 Levels of Tech Design at 1:30, https://www.youtube.com/watch?v=FQVRfadJkJk&t=90s)
- He says macOS cuts a long file name in the middle because an ellipsis at the end hides the version number and one at the start hides the name. He credits Scott Jenson, who built it at Apple in the 80s. (The 7 Levels of Tech Design at 2:15, https://www.youtube.com/watch?v=FQVRfadJkJk&t=135s)
- He says a small change in corner radius changes what people think a control does. Round off checkboxes to match a rounded interface and the app feels broken, because repetition has taught us that round means pick one and square means pick many. (The 7 Levels of Tech Design at 0:45, https://www.youtube.com/watch?v=FQVRfadJkJk&t=45s)
- He says when to validate a form field depends on the field. An email should be checked when the user leaves the box, while a password with a minimum length should be checked on every keystroke. (Why Simple Tech is Almost Impossible to Build at 3:45, https://www.youtube.com/watch?v=qSQZ8B-2nU0&t=225s)
- He says a dark mode background should not be pure black. On an OLED screen black pixels switch off and take slightly longer to come back, which shows as lag when content scrolls. (Why Simple Tech is Almost Impossible to Build at 5:15, https://www.youtube.com/watch?v=qSQZ8B-2nU0&t=315s)
- He says Apple's icons are squircles, a shape between a circle and a square, and that the whole Apple interface and the iPhone's physical corners use it so everything sits concentric and aligned. (Why Simple Tech is Almost Impossible to Build at 7:30, https://www.youtube.com/watch?v=qSQZ8B-2nU0&t=450s; The Secrets That Make Your Phone Actually Work at 4:30, https://www.youtube.com/watch?v=-YVtZV4DjCE&t=270s)
- He suggests the position of a collapse control can change behaviour at scale. YouTube's reply toggle stays put so two clicks open and close it, while Instagram's sits under the comments, and he says this might be why YouTube users read replies more. (Why Simple Tech is Almost Impossible to Build at 5:15, https://www.youtube.com/watch?v=qSQZ8B-2nU0&t=315s)
- He says Google Photos files a photo taken at 1:24 a.m. on Saturday with Friday night, a rule someone built only to make the product feel more human. (Why Simple Tech is Almost Impossible to Build at 12:00, https://www.youtube.com/watch?v=qSQZ8B-2nU0&t=720s)
- He says icons make people faster only while they are familiar. He cites an extra 500 milliseconds to process an unfamiliar icon, which is why YouTube shows a thumbs-up without a label but keeps subscribe as text. (How Your Tech Tricks You at 18:00, https://www.youtube.com/watch?v=7nnyv4yJGJc&t=1080s; How Your Tech Tricks You at 18:45, https://www.youtube.com/watch?v=7nnyv4yJGJc&t=1125s)
- He says the mouse arrow points to the top left because images were drawn from the top-left corner, and pointing anywhere else would have cost a subtraction when computing power was scarce. The decision stayed for decades. (The Lies That Make Your Tech Actually Work at 9:00, https://www.youtube.com/watch?v=rAVV4kAYlI8&t=540s)

## What systems sit underneath one simple interaction?

- He says the first iPhone keyboard was unusable until an Apple engineer made each key's invisible touch area grow or shrink with the most likely next letter. After t and h the target for e becomes much bigger, and he says this still powers phone keyboards. (The Lies That Make Your Tech Actually Work at 0:45, https://www.youtube.com/watch?v=rAVV4kAYlI8&t=45s)
- He says the battery percentage is a guess. Voltage shifts with temperature and load, counting energy in and out compounds small errors, so phones predict the number with machine learning on your usage history. (The Secrets That Make Your Phone Actually Work at 0:00, https://www.youtube.com/watch?v=-YVtZV4DjCE&t=0s)
- He calls moving a text cursor up and down a nightmare to build. Letters have different widths, so the system needs a nearest-position algorithm, a memory of the column you came from, and a reset of that memory when you move sideways. (The Secrets That Make Your Phone Actually Work at 1:30, https://www.youtube.com/watch?v=-YVtZV4DjCE&t=90s; The Ridiculous Engineering Of Text at 6:00, https://www.youtube.com/watch?v=7DN3Ectm6UY&t=360s)
- He says text hides engineering at every layer: emojis stored as several codes glued together, letters merged into shared shapes, curves bent to land on the pixel grid, and thousands of stored spacings for letter pairs. (The Ridiculous Engineering Of Text at 0:00, https://www.youtube.com/watch?v=7DN3Ectm6UY&t=0s; The Ridiculous Engineering Of Text at 3:45, https://www.youtube.com/watch?v=7DN3Ectm6UY&t=225s)
- He says a volume slider is a lie because loudness is perceived on a logarithmic scale. To feel even, the control has to map speaker power on an exponential curve, and he says iOS and Android do this while YouTube's desktop slider packs all the control into the last 10%. (How Your Tech Tricks You at 0:00, https://www.youtube.com/watch?v=7nnyv4yJGJc&t=0s; How Your Tech Tricks You at 0:45, https://www.youtube.com/watch?v=7nnyv4yJGJc&t=45s)
- He says an accurate progress bar is still unsolved. Counting files fails, counting megabytes fails, a plain time estimate fails when speed changes, so well-built bars average the speed over a recent window such as the last 10 seconds. (How Your Tech Tricks You at 7:30, https://www.youtube.com/watch?v=7nnyv4yJGJc&t=450s; The Ridiculous Engineering of Loading Screens at 3:45, https://www.youtube.com/watch?v=XqhduyTnEM8&t=225s)
- He lists what pressing play sets off, all in under one second: a GET request with the video ID, cookies and browser info, a lookup of the server's IP address through the cache or a DNS server, and the request broken into numbered packets that travel over real copper or fibre optic cables to Google's servers. (YouTube Does This Anytime You Watch And You Don't Realize It at 0:00, https://www.youtube.com/watch?v=49_a3jkdBdI&t=0s)
- He says a YouTube video is really hundreds of videos, since each upload is re-encoded at several resolutions and cut into segments of two to 10 seconds. (The Ridiculous Engineering Of YouTube Videos at 11:15, https://www.youtube.com/watch?v=giP1iIbSVh0&t=675s)
- He calls seamless delivery the opposite of easy. Getting a video to you in 0.1 seconds across 106,000 years of video watched per day is, in his words, one of the hardest problems in tech, and he says YouTube built a custom chip only for encoding. (The Ridiculous Engineering Of YouTube Videos at 12:45, https://www.youtube.com/watch?v=giP1iIbSVh0&t=765s)
- He says zooming a map hides a live label system. Google Maps keeps text and icons the same size, runs an algorithm so two labels never overlap, keeps the places most relevant to you visible, gives landmarks and geographical area names a hierarchy, and lets pinned locations overlap as the one exception: "And if all of this works, you never notice." (Why Simple Tech is Almost Impossible to Build at 9:45, https://www.youtube.com/watch?v=qSQZ8B-2nU0&t=585s; Why Simple Tech is Almost Impossible to Build at 11:15, https://www.youtube.com/watch?v=qSQZ8B-2nU0&t=675s)
- He says the phone waits before it registers a tap, to see whether your finger moves into a scroll. He reads Android's source as a 100 millisecond tap timeout and 8 dp of movement, and says the iPhone is generally agreed to be 150 milliseconds. (The Most Important Tiny Decision In Tech History at 4:30, https://www.youtube.com/watch?v=7EVZpW9Rdwk&t=270s)
- He says real-time collaboration that looks smooth hides hard questions: what happens when two people edit the same thing, when one of them presses undo, or when someone edits offline and syncs later. (The Ridiculous Engineering Of Figma at 6:00, https://www.youtube.com/watch?v=5t8dh3DSdBk&t=360s)
- He says Figma sits on something that should not be possible, a creative tool running in a browser at 60 frames per second on a weak computer. Its on-canvas controls live in a separate layer and have their position recalculated 60 times a second. (The Ridiculous Engineering Of Figma at 9:45, https://www.youtube.com/watch?v=5t8dh3DSdBk&t=585s; The Ridiculous Engineering Of Figma at 11:15, https://www.youtube.com/watch?v=5t8dh3DSdBk&t=675s)
- In hardware he says a lid that feels good took real engineering. The AirPods case holds four magnets, two that attract and two that repel, balanced against the lid angle so it always snaps fully open or shut. (The Lies That Make Hardware Actually Work at 0:00, https://www.youtube.com/watch?v=wLIgrGAIRsE&t=0s)

## How do products hide complexity from the user?

- He uses Tesler's law and puts it this way: "Complexity can never be destroyed, it can only be moved." His examples are copy and paste and Claude Code, where the machine does the complicated work and the user only perceives simplicity. (Why Everyone is Suddenly Obsessed With Claude Code at 6:00, https://www.youtube.com/watch?v=gzt52Trk9w0&t=360s)
- He says great products offer **perceived simplicity**, not real simplicity. Copy and paste is two key combinations that work everywhere while the computer handles clipboard formats, rich text, images and talk between apps. (Why Everyone is Suddenly Obsessed With Claude Code at 6:00, https://www.youtube.com/watch?v=gzt52Trk9w0&t=360s)
- In 2026 he said Claude Code is simple because it solves its own problems, not because it is polished or never fails. When a connection to Notion failed it tried the API and read the documentation itself, which he calls Tesler's law on steroids. (Why Everyone is Suddenly Obsessed With Claude Code at 6:45, https://www.youtube.com/watch?v=gzt52Trk9w0&t=405s)
- He says the biggest problem all professional tools face is that each starts simple, advanced users ask for advanced features, and basic and advanced options end up mixed together in a scary interface. He calls the result cognitive overload: given too much information, the brain cannot process it all at once. (Why Everyone Is Obsessed With Figma at 0:45, https://www.youtube.com/watch?v=0jze5RbyoW4&t=45s; Why Everyone Is Obsessed With Figma at 1:30, https://www.youtube.com/watch?v=0jze5RbyoW4&t=90s)
- He credits Figma's success to progressive disclosure: basic tools up front, and an advanced option such as corner smoothing shown only inside the corner panel and hidden again when you deselect. He adds that it is very hard to design well. (Why Everyone Is Obsessed With Figma at 3:00, https://www.youtube.com/watch?v=0jze5RbyoW4&t=180s; Why Everyone Is Obsessed With Figma at 3:45, https://www.youtube.com/watch?v=0jze5RbyoW4&t=225s)
- He says that when a product has a powerful system for advanced users, the way to keep it simple is to let them explore the more complex version step by step. (The Ridiculous Engineering Of Figma at 15:45, https://www.youtube.com/watch?v=5t8dh3DSdBk&t=945s)
- He says the best products are built by saying no. Once things go well users ask for more features, views and customization, and that is how tools get complicated. (The Ridiculous Engineering Of Figma at 12:45, https://www.youtube.com/watch?v=5t8dh3DSdBk&t=765s)
- He says the true genius of Figma was being lazy. It let the community build advanced features as plugins that run instantly with no install, and with millions of users sometimes the best move is to do nothing and let users build it. (Why Everyone Is Obsessed With Figma at 3:45, https://www.youtube.com/watch?v=0jze5RbyoW4&t=225s; The Ridiculous Engineering Of Figma at 17:15, https://www.youtube.com/watch?v=5t8dh3DSdBk&t=1035s)
- He says WhatsApp did the hardest thing a product can do while rivals pile on features, which is to stay on the basics. A new user sees only messaging, the app stays small enough for old phones, and that simplicity let about 50 people serve 465 million users. (Why Half The World Uses WhatsApp at 3:45, https://www.youtube.com/watch?v=1O1u0UO99NE&t=225s; Why Half The World Uses WhatsApp at 6:45, https://www.youtube.com/watch?v=1O1u0UO99NE&t=405s)
- He says Notion avoided bloat by keeping only what was absolutely needed, with no A4 pages, no extra fonts and only 10 colours, and by building everything from a few generic blocks that users assemble themselves. (Why Everyone Is Obsessed With Notion at 1:30, https://www.youtube.com/watch?v=aWGviOMQqSw&t=90s; The Ridiculous Engineering Of Notion at 2:15, https://www.youtube.com/watch?v=b00sgRR_Vc0&t=135s)
- He says reaching the mainstream means focusing on simplicity and hiding the technology. Coinbase, which arrived in 2012, looked very similar to PayPal, gave no complicated explanations of Bitcoin or blockchain, and was successful because it hid that complexity. The majority wants the real value front and centre and does not care how the algorithm works. (Famous Apps Looked Like This at 3:00, https://www.youtube.com/watch?v=_Sn8Glj5NW4&t=180s; Famous Apps Looked Like This at 3:45, https://www.youtube.com/watch?v=_Sn8Glj5NW4&t=225s)
- He draws a **complexity line**, the most complexity your users can take. Terminals sat above it for most people, graphical interfaces brought tech under it, and ChatGPT stayed under it because loose text input forgives typos. (The Ridiculous Engineering Of Text at 9:00, https://www.youtube.com/watch?v=7DN3Ectm6UY&t=540s; The Ridiculous Engineering Of Text at 12:00, https://www.youtube.com/watch?v=7DN3Ectm6UY&t=720s)
- In September 2026 he said agent workflows have pushed AI tools back above that line and locked mainstream users out, and that bringing them back down to regular people is one of the most valuable problems in tech. (The Ridiculous Engineering Of Text at 13:30, https://www.youtube.com/watch?v=7DN3Ectm6UY&t=810s; The Ridiculous Engineering Of Text at 14:15, https://www.youtube.com/watch?v=7DN3Ectm6UY&t=855s)
- In May 2024 he said the fix for AI features is **invisible AI**: start from a real problem, apply AI inside the interface people already know, and do not announce it, because users only care that the problem gets solved. (AI Has A Huge Problem at 12:00, https://www.youtube.com/watch?v=CY_b8w8u9NY&t=720s; AI Has A Huge Problem at 12:45, https://www.youtube.com/watch?v=CY_b8w8u9NY&t=765s)
- He says good design is where you do not need an instruction manual, and in 2024 he said chatbots and prompts had recreated the command line problem that graphical interfaces solved 40 years earlier. (AI Has A Huge Problem at 10:30, https://www.youtube.com/watch?v=CY_b8w8u9NY&t=630s; AI Has A Huge Problem at 9:00, https://www.youtube.com/watch?v=CY_b8w8u9NY&t=540s)
- He says the first level of Plants vs. Zombies teaches its rules with not one instruction on screen, so players would probably say the game had no tutorial. Getting users to grasp the sunflower mechanic without an explicit tutorial meant changing its cost, rebalancing and retesting the entire game, and dozens of hours of work. (The Weird Way Tech Steals From Games at 1:30, https://www.youtube.com/watch?v=GypAKofWGBk&t=90s)
- He says the OP-1 synthesizer has the best software design of any product he has used. Professional music software takes weeks to learn, while he learned the OP-1 from a 30 minute tutorial because it swaps numbers and sliders for playful visuals and a tape reel you can spin. (The Best Tech Product Ever Designed at 6:00, https://www.youtube.com/watch?v=n1cMrkMqmnw&t=360s; The Best Tech Product Ever Designed at 6:45, https://www.youtube.com/watch?v=n1cMrkMqmnw&t=405s)

## How do defaults steer people?

- He places defaults at level four of product design and says they control the behaviour of millions for good and bad, because most people never change a setting. Netflix playing the next episode is his example. (The 7 Levels of Tech Design at 7:30, https://www.youtube.com/watch?v=FQVRfadJkJk&t=450s)
- He compares read receipts, which are on by default in WhatsApp while iMessage asks you: "Same feature, different default, different relationship." Given the choice, people tend to pick the option that reveals less. (The 7 Levels of Tech Design at 8:15, https://www.youtube.com/watch?v=FQVRfadJkJk&t=495s; The 7 Levels of Tech Design at 9:00, https://www.youtube.com/watch?v=FQVRfadJkJk&t=540s)
- He illustrates **default bias** with a chart of organ donor rates in Europe that splits into a very high group and a very low group. He says the only difference is whether donation is the default, as in Austria, or needs an opt-in. (The 7 Levels of Tech Design at 9:00, https://www.youtube.com/watch?v=FQVRfadJkJk&t=540s; Ads Are Coming To Your Lockscreen For Real at 6:00, https://www.youtube.com/watch?v=SQ_hBlGNR4c&t=360s)
- He says Google bought Android and built Chrome because whoever controls the platform sets the defaults, including the search engine, and that it pays Apple 20 billion dollars a year to be the default on Safari because it estimated it would lose 60 to 80% of iPhone search traffic otherwise. People use whatever is already there. (The 7 Levels of Tech Design at 9:00, https://www.youtube.com/watch?v=FQVRfadJkJk&t=540s)
- In 2022 he said the low opt-out rate claimed for the Glance lock screen was default bias and not approval, because the average person accepts whatever ships on the phone. (Ads Are Coming To Your Lockscreen For Real at 6:00, https://www.youtube.com/watch?v=SQ_hBlGNR4c&t=360s)
- He says WhatsApp shipped read receipts as a default in 2014 thinking it was just a nice feature, and the backlash made it add a way to hide them 10 days later. It made hiding reciprocal so people would not all switch theirs off at no cost. (The Insane Design Of Messaging at 0:00, https://www.youtube.com/watch?v=6DSW1rN-ZV4&t=0s; The Insane Design Of Messaging at 0:45, https://www.youtube.com/watch?v=6DSW1rN-ZV4&t=45s)
- He says YouTube's autoplay arrived in 2014 with a 10 second countdown that is now five seconds, and reads the shrinking number as proof the default works. (The Ridiculous Engineering Of YouTube Videos at 4:30, https://www.youtube.com/watch?v=giP1iIbSVh0&t=270s)
- His advice to users is to question the defaults for software they spend 10 to 50 hours a week in, such as the browser or email client, since companies pay to be the default exactly because people trust it. (How I Finally Fixed My Digital Life at 3:00, https://www.youtube.com/watch?v=7PhEfKCHr7Y&t=180s)

## How is tech built around human mistakes?

- His thesis is that everyday tech is not designed for perfection, speed or looks: "It's secretly designed to make up for how humans fail." He calls this being **designed for imperfection**. (How Your Tech Is Built For You To Fail at 0:45, https://www.youtube.com/watch?v=4HWPFd8CQo8&t=45s)
- He says Gmail's undo send is an artificial delay. The email waits in a hidden queue for 5 to 30 seconds before it leaves, which is technically worse since every email arrives late, yet Google made it impossible to switch off. (How Your Tech Is Built For You To Fail at 0:00, https://www.youtube.com/watch?v=4HWPFd8CQo8&t=0s)
- He says undo keeps a history like a stack of plates and stores edits, not full copies of the document. When two people undo in the same spot the answer depends on operational transformation, which he says Google invented for Docs. (How Your Tech Is Built For You To Fail at 1:30, https://www.youtube.com/watch?v=4HWPFd8CQo8&t=90s; How Your Tech Is Built For You To Fail at 2:15, https://www.youtube.com/watch?v=4HWPFd8CQo8&t=135s)
- He traces four stages of error handling: a terminal that rejects one wrong character, Word's red line that tells you to fix it, YouTube quietly searching the corrected spelling, and AI chatbots that understand typos and five minutes of rambling. (How Your Tech Is Built For You To Fail at 3:45, https://www.youtube.com/watch?v=4HWPFd8CQo8&t=225s)
- He says deletion, the most dangerous action in tech, was made more human over time. A wrong command once wiped a machine with no warning, then came the trash can, and now he says Google Photos keeps a deleted photo for 60 days and recoverable for 30 more. (How Your Tech Is Built For You To Fail at 5:15, https://www.youtube.com/watch?v=4HWPFd8CQo8&t=315s)
- He says forgiveness can be subtle, fixing the mistake without making you feel dumb. The to-do app Things 3 quietly turns June 31st, which does not exist, into July 1st with no error. (How Your Tech Is Built For You To Fail at 6:00, https://www.youtube.com/watch?v=4HWPFd8CQo8&t=360s)
- He says a well-made button shows feedback on press, fires on release, cancels if the finger slides off, re-arms if it slides back, and has a tap area larger than what you see. None of that is native behaviour, so someone designed it. (How Your Tech Is Built For You To Fail at 6:00, https://www.youtube.com/watch?v=4HWPFd8CQo8&t=360s; The Lies That Make Your Tech Actually Work at 1:30, https://www.youtube.com/watch?v=rAVV4kAYlI8&t=90s)
- He says games pretend your reactions are perfect. Coyote time gives you a few extra milliseconds to jump after you have already left the platform. (How Your Tech Is Built For You To Fail at 6:45, https://www.youtube.com/watch?v=4HWPFd8CQo8&t=405s)
- He says autosave removed a whole class of failure. Twenty years ago forgetting to save meant losing everything, while Google Docs, Notion and Figma save every change by default. (How Your Tech Is Built For You To Fail at 7:30, https://www.youtube.com/watch?v=4HWPFd8CQo8&t=450s)
- He uses Fitts's law, that the time to hit a target depends on its distance and size, to explain why right-click menus open under the pointer, why important buttons are big and close, and why back became a swipe from the screen edge. (How Your Tech Is Built For You To Fail at 7:30, https://www.youtube.com/watch?v=4HWPFd8CQo8&t=450s; The Lies That Make Your Tech Actually Work at 8:15, https://www.youtube.com/watch?v=rAVV4kAYlI8&t=495s)
- He says submenus have to forgive the diagonal path people take toward an item, which leaves the menu and would close it. Ignoring such rules is something users feel, as with a Spotify desktop panel that opens on the far side of the screen from the click. (How Your Tech Is Built For You To Fail at 8:15, https://www.youtube.com/watch?v=4HWPFd8CQo8&t=495s; How Your Tech Is Built For You To Fail at 9:45, https://www.youtube.com/watch?v=4HWPFd8CQo8&t=585s)
- His favourite forgiveness is tech that reads frustration. He says Apple made the cursor grow when you shake the mouse and built shake to undo because shaking is how annoyance looks. (How Your Tech Is Built For You To Fail at 11:15, https://www.youtube.com/watch?v=4HWPFd8CQo8&t=675s)
- He says optimistic updates are why some apps feel instant. The app assumes success, updates the screen, saves in the background and speaks up only on failure, because slowness frustrates people more than uncertainty about each save. (How Your Tech Is Built For You To Fail at 12:45, https://www.youtube.com/watch?v=4HWPFd8CQo8&t=765s; Why Simple Tech is Almost Impossible to Build at 12:45, https://www.youtube.com/watch?v=qSQZ8B-2nU0&t=765s)
- He says shuffle was made less random because people judge randomness badly. Listeners complained that a truly random shuffle kept playing the same artist, so Apple and Spotify spread artists apart to make it feel random. (The Spotify Shuffle Button is a Lie at 1:30, https://www.youtube.com/watch?v=NhAIA-o7f4M&t=90s; The Secrets That Make Your Phone Actually Work at 3:00, https://www.youtube.com/watch?v=-YVtZV4DjCE&t=180s)
- He sums up that your technology is lying to you. He says that when Larry Tesler invented Ctrl-Z he was declaring that humans have a right to make mistakes, that our tools should forgive us, and that perfection should not be a prerequisite for using technology. (How Your Tech Is Built For You To Fail at 13:30, https://www.youtube.com/watch?v=4HWPFd8CQo8&t=810s)

## Why does tech copy nature and the physical world, and when should it stop?

- He says designers copy nature because the real world is what human brains understand. A swipe you cannot reverse halfway feels wrong, since a page in a book can be turned back, and the iPhone app switcher gets this right. (The Lies That Make Your Tech Actually Work at 3:45, https://www.youtube.com/watch?v=rAVV4kAYlI8&t=225s)
- He says smooth scrolling copies friction and is a mathematical mess to build. A linear slowdown looks bad because most of nature is not linear, and the bounce at the end of a list is a modelled spring with weight and stiffness. (The Lies That Make Your Tech Actually Work at 6:00, https://www.youtube.com/watch?v=rAVV4kAYlI8&t=360s; The Lies That Make Your Tech Actually Work at 6:45, https://www.youtube.com/watch?v=rAVV4kAYlI8&t=405s)
- He says copying nature in motion is why toddlers can use devices. In the 90s pages jumped in chunks and adults needed manuals, and now a one-year-old tries to swipe a magazine. (The Ridiculous Engineering Of Animations at 3:00, https://www.youtube.com/watch?v=z3Lo5QT7nME&t=180s)
- He says copying nature has a problem: nature is slow. An animation teaches the first time and by the hundredth time only makes you wait, so knowing when not to animate is probably even more important than knowing when to. (The Ridiculous Engineering Of Animations at 8:15, https://www.youtube.com/watch?v=z3Lo5QT7nME&t=495s; The Ridiculous Engineering Of Animations at 9:45, https://www.youtube.com/watch?v=z3Lo5QT7nME&t=585s)
- He says the way to design good digital controls is to copy physical ones. A digital button should look pressable, show a pressed state and fire on release, and radio buttons select one option because a real radio could tune one frequency. (What Happened To Physical Tech at 2:15, https://www.youtube.com/watch?v=TIFF-rinlbo&t=135s; What Happened To Physical Tech at 3:00, https://www.youtube.com/watch?v=TIFF-rinlbo&t=180s)
- He says explicit affordances and real-world metaphors were needed 20 years ago when most people were new to digital interfaces, with Microsoft Bob, which made real-world objects literally digital, as the wildest example. Now that technology has rewired our brains we no longer need them, which gave birth to today's minimalistic design. (The Lies That Make Your Tech Actually Work at 4:30, https://www.youtube.com/watch?v=rAVV4kAYlI8&t=270s; The Lies That Make Your Tech Actually Work at 5:15, https://www.youtube.com/watch?v=rAVV4kAYlI8&t=315s)
- He says everything became rounded because sharp shapes read as danger and nature has few perfect angles, so rounding feels approachable. He also says there is now too much rounding everywhere. (Why Simple Tech is Almost Impossible to Build at 6:45, https://www.youtube.com/watch?v=qSQZ8B-2nU0&t=405s)
- He says rounded corners matter even more in AR and VR. Using the Kiki and Bouba effect, he says the sense of threat from sharp shapes is multiplied 10 times when windows float in the space around you. (The Secrets That Make Your Phone Actually Work at 13:30, https://www.youtube.com/watch?v=-YVtZV4DjCE&t=810s)
- In December 2023 he said natural interaction took 15 years to build for touch and must be rebuilt from scratch for AR, and predicted this would make or break the successor to the smartphone. (The Lies That Make Your Tech Actually Work at 9:45, https://www.youtube.com/watch?v=rAVV4kAYlI8&t=585s)

## What does simplicity cost, and where does it go wrong?

- He says minimalism sometimes goes too far and leaves no way to know how you can interact with something, and asks whether you knew you can swipe on Spotify's player to change songs. (The Lies That Make Your Tech Actually Work at 6:00, https://www.youtube.com/watch?v=rAVV4kAYlI8&t=360s)
- He calls one-finger zoom in maps genius and a failure at once, because nothing tells you it exists, like a glass door with no handle. His fix is to detect a pinch and then show the easier gesture. (The 7 Levels of Tech Design at 4:30, https://www.youtube.com/watch?v=FQVRfadJkJk&t=270s; The Secrets That Make Your Phone Actually Work at 6:45, https://www.youtube.com/watch?v=-YVtZV4DjCE&t=405s)
- He files pull to refresh as genius and also **geniusly evil**. It is intuitive and removes a button, but paired with an infinite feed it feeds the urge to keep pulling and is probably worth billions in ad revenue. (The Secrets That Make Your Phone Actually Work at 5:15, https://www.youtube.com/watch?v=-YVtZV4DjCE&t=315s; The 7 Levels of Tech Design at 5:15, https://www.youtube.com/watch?v=FQVRfadJkJk&t=315s)
- He blames usability testing for stripping character from interfaces. The plainer option wins the measured test, so products became simpler and more approachable but also boring. (What Happened To User Interfaces at 3:45, https://www.youtube.com/watch?v=HdozqSVF-VM&t=225s; What Happened To User Interfaces at 4:30, https://www.youtube.com/watch?v=HdozqSVF-VM&t=270s)
- He says popular consumer products get dumber because of the **law of the marginal user**. Tests are decided by the next user not yet acquired, who tolerates no complexity, so features that give control lose and get buried in menus. (Why Tech Gets Terrible When It Gets Popular at 7:30, https://www.youtube.com/watch?v=BQLVgmhgtpQ&t=450s; Why Tech Gets Terrible When It Gets Popular at 9:45, https://www.youtube.com/watch?v=BQLVgmhgtpQ&t=585s)
- He says business products go the other way and turn into a mess of a thousand toggles, because sales teams push for the one button each big prospect needs before signing. (Why Tech Gets Terrible When It Gets Popular at 10:30, https://www.youtube.com/watch?v=BQLVgmhgtpQ&t=630s)
- He says simplicity has to be balanced against advanced users. It gets new users in, but the risk of sliding back into something like Word 2003 is always there. (The Ridiculous Engineering Of Notion at 18:00, https://www.youtube.com/watch?v=b00sgRR_Vc0&t=1080s)
- He says any clean replacement for Word would end up rebuilding Word. Courts need exact type and margins, law firms need clause numbering and footnotes, and feature by feature you recreate the same bloated mess. (The Weird Reason Your Tech Stays Broken at 10:30, https://www.youtube.com/watch?v=-51ocAD6zBI&t=630s)
- He describes the **1% dilemma**: reaching 99% of use cases is fairly easy, and the last 1% of odd edge cases takes exponential resources. The more often a product is used, the more often someone lands in that 1%. (The Most Dangerous App That Everyone Loves at 1:30, https://www.youtube.com/watch?v=P2qOiKA56tI&t=90s)
- He says a feature that seems obvious can hide so many complexities that even Google and Apple decide to pass on it. In his hypothetical scenario, showing unsafe areas on a map discriminates against crime-heavy areas, drives people and tourism away from them, turns the app into a constant source of anxiety, and puts the company at the centre of controversy, media backlash and government scrutiny. (Why Google Maps Doesn't Show You Unsafe Areas at 6:00, https://www.youtube.com/watch?v=4i66ht6YHkw&t=360s)
- He says that at WhatsApp's scale a single feature must be built for every platform and rolled out to two billion people on different devices and app versions, which is why basics like reactions took forever. (Why Half The World Uses WhatsApp at 5:15, https://www.youtube.com/watch?v=1O1u0UO99NE&t=315s)
- He says chasing simplicity and power together will anger some users, as Figma's new left sidebar did, and that Figma has not cracked everything: the layer tree turns into a mess and pages have confused him many times. (The Ridiculous Engineering Of Figma at 15:45, https://www.youtube.com/watch?v=5t8dh3DSdBk&t=945s; Why Everyone Is Obsessed With Figma at 6:00, https://www.youtube.com/watch?v=0jze5RbyoW4&t=360s)
- In hardware he says designing a premium corner is easy and paying for it is the hard part. Cheap brands will not raise production cost for a fraction of a millimetre, and a better-feeling button can drop from 5 million rated presses to 10,000. (The Lies That Make Hardware Actually Work at 3:00, https://www.youtube.com/watch?v=wLIgrGAIRsE&t=180s; The Lies That Make Hardware Actually Work at 6:00, https://www.youtube.com/watch?v=wLIgrGAIRsE&t=360s)
- He says what looks sleek in a rendering can fail in manufacturing. The PS5 looked cheap to him because a large glossy plastic surface shows every flaw left by injection molding. (How Your Tech Tricks You at 12:00, https://www.youtube.com/watch?v=7nnyv4yJGJc&t=720s; The Lies That Make Hardware Actually Work at 9:00, https://www.youtube.com/watch?v=wLIgrGAIRsE&t=540s)
- In 2022, presenting designer Ted Hunt's philosophy, he asked whether products optimised for simplicity and effortlessness are always the better option, and noted that making the user reflect, question or choose is not on the radar of most companies because it will not increase engagement. (The Man Who Made Apple Possible In 1968 at 7:30, https://www.youtube.com/watch?v=BXSHJXHeaqM&t=450s)
